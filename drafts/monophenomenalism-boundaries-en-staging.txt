Monophenomenalism: distinctions from monism, idealism, phenomenalism, solipsism, and nondual teachings
Why an independent philosophical position cannot be defined by importing the premises of theories that resemble it.

## Introduction. Define the position first, then compare

The word “one” almost inevitably evokes monism. The word “experiencing” suggests idealism or phenomenalism. The refusal to recognize an independent owner of experiencing recalls some nondual teachings. Resemblances between terms and particular conclusions can indeed be worth investigating. But there is a fundamental difference between comparing two positions and substituting one for the other.

Premature identification reverses the direction of inquiry. Instead of asking, “What exactly does monophenomenalism assert?” one asks, “Which familiar theory does it correspond to?” Its definitions are then reinterpreted through the definitions of another theory, and its arguments are supplemented with premises taken from elsewhere. Sometimes one unnoticed addition is enough to leave an entirely different philosophical construction under the original name.

The order of this inquiry is therefore the reverse. First, the content of monophenomenalism itself is established, together with the boundaries of its assertions and permissible inferences. Only then is it examined which claims made by other approaches genuinely coincide with it, which merely resemble it, and which require fundamentally different grounds. Monism and the other traditions are objects of comparison, not the initial language in which the position is constructed.

Drawing such boundaries does not require refuting the history of philosophy or declaring every similarity illusory. The rule is stricter: no resemblance licenses the silent addition to an examined claim of something it does not contain.

Nor should the independent origin of a position be replaced by its subsequent classification. Monophenomenalism was formulated through an independent examination of experience and logical relations; comparison with historical doctrines became a separate research task. This does not establish the truth of the position or the absolute novelty of every proposition it contains. But resemblance to an existing teaching does not by itself demonstrate borrowing either: the history of how an idea arose and the content of that idea must be investigated separately.

## 1. The starting position: unity needs no second foundation

The positive starting position of monophenomenalism is formulated as follows:

> Everything that exists is a phenomenon. The phenomenon is one.

This formula does not report the discovery of a gigantic object in which all other objects are located. It does not introduce a new material from which the universe is made, or appoint a common owner of everything that happens. Here, the phenomenon is not one more entity added to what exists.

One person differs from another; pain from joy; a thought about a tree from the tree; a promise from its fulfillment. The starting position neither erases these distinctions nor declares their practical significance illusory. It rejects a different, logically unwarranted move: because two contents have been distinguished, each must already have been established as an independent ultimate ground of existence.

Difference does not establish that kind of independence. An additional argument is required. The fact that two contents are distinguishable does not supply one.

For example, a person, a tree, and the perception of that tree can be distinguished in experience. This does not yet imply that two utterly isolated worlds—inner and outer—exist from the outset, and that we must subsequently discover how to connect them. Such a formulation has already assumed the ontological rupture that remains to be justified.

The phrase “there is nothing to connect” does not mean that distances, communication, the need to understand others, or differences between people have disappeared. It means that building a bridge between independently established first principles is not declared an obligatory task before the existence of those first principles has itself been established.

Yet refusing to begin with a rupture does not in itself prove unity. The unity of the phenomenon is an explicitly adopted starting position. Both the methodological refusal to introduce a second independent foundation and the positive assertion of one phenomenon matter, but they are not identical. Conflating these two levels would be the first substitution—before any other philosophy had even entered the discussion.

## 2. Why this is not merely replacing the word “world”

The objection is obvious: if everything that exists is called “the phenomenon,” what has changed?

Merely changing a name explains almost nothing. The content of the position becomes apparent in the starting assumptions it does not automatically accept and in the steps of reasoning it requires us to justify.

The question “How does consciousness reach the real world?” often comes with a ready-made scheme: there is a separate subject, its consciousness is a sealed container, and reality lies outside. Inquiry can begin not by searching for a way out of this container but by asking what warrants the assumed arrangement in the first place.

The experience of being enclosed does not prove the existence of a metaphysical chamber. The image of an inner observer does not prove an independent observer. The grammar of the phrase “an experience is given to me” does not establish a separately existing owner of what is experienced.

This way of examining the issue does not automatically resolve every problem of knowledge. It makes it possible to distinguish a genuine problem from a difficulty created by first introducing unjustified entities.

Nor does the fact that every observation, doubt, or argument is accessible within experiencing prove monophenomenalism. The limits of a viewpoint available for verification do not deductively entail the impossibility of independent reality. Indirect arguments, the agreement of observations, scientific explanations, and successful predictions retain their evidential significance within the investigations to which they belong.

Three things must therefore be distinguished: the positive starting position; arguments that explain why it may be adopted; and further conclusions that require independent examination. If an argument does not establish its conclusion, a confident tone does not close the gap.

The same applies to objections. The fact that, within the position, every objection is also considered as a phenomenon does not make the position infallible or excuse it from answering the substance of that objection. Criticism may be expressed in a different philosophical vocabulary; its force depends on its arguments, not on acceptance of the starting terminology.

## 3. Monophenomenalism and the Model of the Phenomenon: the position is not its instrument

The *Model of the Phenomenon* has been proposed as a way of examining distinctions within the adopted position. Its five theses are:

1. Everything is one phenomenon.
2. The phenomenon is sensory experiencing.
3. A distinction within sensory experiencing is a concept.
4. Concepts structure the phenomenon into experience.
5. Experience is the phenomenon structured through concepts.

“Sensory experiencing” is not limited to sensations received through the sense organs: thoughts, emotions, and states are also experienced. A “concept” here is not necessarily a word, a theory, or a consciously articulated idea. “Experience” has a specific meaning: the phenomenon structured through concepts. Experiencing and experience are not interchangeable terms.

The theses do not describe a manufacturing process in which a formless phenomenon first arrives, outside forces called concepts then process it, and some subject produces the finished world. A distinction does not require an independent distinguisher. Structuring does not turn concepts into separate causes of the phenomenon's existence. The model itself is also considered a concept within the phenomenon.

This is an internal boundary without which external comparisons become unreliable. Monophenomenalism establishes a starting position; the *Model of the Phenomenon* offers a particular conceptual instrument for studying it. Accepting the position does not automatically entail accepting every specialized definition in the model. A conclusion that depends on additional theses cannot be attributed to the starting formula alone.

If even these two original works are not distinguished, premises taken from elsewhere will enter the discussion still more imperceptibly.

## 4. Monophenomenalism and monism: the same word “one” does not mean the same theory

Monism is not a single doctrine with one obligatory definition. Under this heading one finds, among other things, the claim that there is just one concrete object, the thesis that the whole is prior to its parts, the claim that there is one fundamental kind of being, and theories of a single substance. These versions ask different questions and should not be conflated.

In the broadest use of the word “monism,” monophenomenalism can be grouped among monistic positions because it affirms unity. But this is precisely where a firm boundary must be drawn: that classification does not explain what “one” means in the starting position, nor does it bring with it a substance, the priority of the whole, or a theory of how multiplicity emerges. The name of a family of doctrines has no evidential force.

Monophenomenalism does not assert that exactly one concrete object exists instead of many objects. It does not establish the necessary metaphysical priority of the whole over its parts. It does not posit a substance out of which things are made. It does not explain plurality as the transformation of an originally single material into multiple objects.

The limit is precise: “the phenomenon is one” does not entail the existence of a single concrete object, a substantialist theory, or a hierarchy of ontological dependence. The absence of those conclusions does not prove the corresponding monistic theories false, but it does not make them part of monophenomenalism either. Each such inference requires additional premises.

The possibility of calling the position “monistic” does not give monism authority to determine its internal structure. The name of a family of theories cannot replace examination of a particular claim.


## 5. Materialism and idealism: one first principle cannot simply be replaced by another

The familiar dispute over whether matter or consciousness is primary invites us to choose which of these already named foundations is fundamental. Monophenomenalism is not obliged to accept the framing of that choice as its own initial problem.

Matter is not introduced as an independent first principle; neither, however, is consciousness, thought, or spirit appointed in its place. Physical objects, the brain, the body, measuring instruments, experiments, and physical laws remain meaningful subjects of inquiry. Connections established by science do not lose their significance merely because they are considered within experience.

But the success of a physical explanation of particular processes does not by itself establish the stronger metaphysical claim that the physical has thereby been proved to be the independent foundation of everything that exists. That step calls for a separate justification.

A symmetrical error arises in an idealist reading. The fact that a tree is accessible within experiencing does not mean that the tree is produced by thinking about it or comes into existence only when individually perceived. Idealism is heterogeneous; not every form of idealism literally claims that thought manufactures things. Yet primary spirit, a universal mind, or the dependence of the world's existence on individual consciousness does not appear in monophenomenalism merely because it uses the word “experiencing.”

The boundary is not a prohibition on physical or idealist explanations. It is a refusal to present any such explanatory program as the unspoken content of the starting position.

## 6. Neutral monism: not choosing either side does not establish a third nature

If neither matter nor consciousness has been declared primary, it is easy to suppose that fundamental reality must be neutral with respect to both. But that is already a new positive claim.

Neutral monism is not homogeneous: its versions may speak of neutral elements, events, or some other fundamental nature, rather than necessarily of a single substance that split over time into the mental and the physical. Certain versions assign a special role to experience. For this reason, proximity to monophenomenalism cannot be dismissed by a label alone.

Monophenomenalism does not introduce a neutral nature or basic elements through which the derivative character of the mental and the physical must be explained. Nor is neutral monism accused here of accepting two fundamentally independent beginnings: many of its versions treat the mental and physical as derivative. Simply declining to make matter and consciousness first principles does not establish the explanatory program of neutral monism.

To assign the phenomenon the role of such a foundation, it would be necessary to show which assertions and arguments warrant doing so. The word “neutral” does not innocently fill a gap: it introduces a particular metaphysical program.

## 7. Solipsism, panpsychism, and cosmopsychism: experiencing acquires no owner

The formula “everything is a phenomenon” is sometimes hastily taken to entail “only my consciousness exists.” But the word “my” has suddenly appeared in that conclusion. The starting position does not establish that everything which occurs belongs to a particular individual.

Monophenomenalism does not posit the individual “I” as the fundamental owner of the world. Replacing “the phenomenon” with “my impressions” is therefore not a neutral paraphrase. It adds precisely the independent foundation with which the position did not begin.

Increasing the scale of the owner does not correct the error. If a universal consciousness that perceives through all beings is substituted for an individual observer, the problem does not disappear: a new universal subject has been proposed. Neither such a cosmic observer nor the fundamental and ubiquitous character of the mental follows from the model's five theses. Panpsychism does not necessarily attribute a separate consciousness to every object, and cosmopsychism has multiple versions. Both approaches require arguments of their own. “Sensory experiencing” in the model does not mean that each distinguishable thing possesses an experience of its own.

At the same time, not introducing the “I” as an independent first principle is not an instruction to eliminate the ordinary sense of self. A person remembers promises, makes choices, errs, cares for those close to them, and is responsible for the consequences of their actions. Other people do not cease to matter either: the unity of the phenomenon does not grant access to their thoughts or turn another person's pain into an invention.

Rejecting an assumed metaphysical isolation does not replace the task of understanding other people. Nor does the mere fact that people differ prove the independence of their supposed ontological first principles.

## 8. Phenomenalism and constructivism: distinguishing does not mean producing or abolishing

A particularly dangerous substitution occurs when the model's technical meaning of “concept” is replaced by the everyday meaning of “fiction.”

The error takes a simple form: pain is distinguished; a distinction is a concept; a concept is unreal; therefore pain does not exist. The mistake occurs at the third step, where the model's definition has silently been replaced by another meaning of the word.

The fact that pain is distinguished does not cancel the pain. The same applies to color, the body, danger, and hunger. One must also distinguish the actuality of experiencing from the truth of a judgment about it: fear may genuinely be experienced while a belief about a specific threat proves false. The model does not force a choice between “everything is true” and “nothing exists.”

Nor does a phenomenalist program of analyzing statements about physical objects in terms of actual and possible sensations follow from the model. Distinguishing a table, its visible appearance, and the memory of it is not the same as reducing a statement about the table to claims about possible sensations. That analysis would require its own justification.

Likewise, “concepts structure the phenomenon into experience” does not mean “the knowing subject produces reality.” The theses contain neither independent raw material nor a builder nor a procedure for manufacturing the world. Radical constructivism examines the construction of knowledge and the viability of cognitive schemes; it acquires no authority to define the model's content merely because the word “structure” appears in both.

## 9. Phenomenology, Kant, and philosophy of language: a shared subject does not imply a shared method

Examining experiencing does not in itself amount to adopting the phenomenological method. Phenomenology studies modes of givenness and the structure of experience; in some of its branches, a significant place is assigned to intentionality—the directedness of experiencing toward something. These questions may overlap with questions explored through the *Model of the Phenomenon*, but such overlap does not establish the identity of their starting positions.

The unity of the phenomenon is not derived from phenomenological reduction. Equally, it would be a mistake to portray all phenomenology as a doctrine of two independent substances and then refute this caricature by pointing to the absence of a separate observer. Intentionality as a subject of inquiry does not itself establish that kind of substantial division.

The word “phenomenon” does not automatically import Kant's distinction between appearances and things in themselves, his account of a priori forms, or the transcendental unity of apperception. Sharing a term does not transfer an entire philosophical system with it.

The same is true of language. A concept in the model is not exhausted by a word: a distinction may lack verbal expression. The limits of language therefore cannot, without a separate argument, be declared the limits of the phenomenon. Philosophy of language and Wittgenstein's investigations may be compared with the model, but they do not become its concealed foundation.

## 10. Advaita Vedanta: Brahman is not another name for the phenomenon

The phrase “Advaita without Sanskrit” might sound like a quick explanation of monophenomenalism. In fact, it would import another doctrine's premises before the content of monophenomenalism had even been examined.

In Advaita Vedanta, the relationship between Atman and Brahman is bound up with a particular understanding of reality, knowledge, ignorance, and liberation. Monophenomenalism does not establish the identity of Atman and Brahman; it does not rename Brahman “the phenomenon,” *maya* “concept,” or liberating knowledge “acceptance of the five theses.”

There is a genuine point of contact: the changing empirical person does not acquire the status of an independent ultimate foundation. But in Advaita this distinction belongs to a doctrine of Atman and Brahman. In the *Model of the Phenomenon*, the ordinary “I” is considered a distinction within experience, with no need to introduce an independent true Self.

The mechanism of substitution is especially clear in a thought experiment. The study is labeled “Advaita without Sanskrit”; the assertion that “the phenomenon is one” is read as a claim about Brahman; the absence of an independent owner of experiencing is read as a concealed doctrine of the true Atman; and the absence of an obligatory task of liberation is treated as a deficiency. What is then assessed is no longer the stated position of monophenomenalism but its conformity to expectations imported from another system. Resemblance on one question does not justify replacing the answers to all the others.

## 11. Buddhism and Zen: the absence of an independent “I” does not become a path to salvation

Comparison with Buddhism requires even greater care. Buddhism comprises different schools and ways of understanding non-self, dependent origination, suffering, and liberation. The Madhyamaka critique of independent intrinsic nature does not amount to a positive thesis that there is one phenomenon. Moving from one claim to the other requires an argument, not a resemblance between words such as “unity” and “emptiness.”

A concept in the model is not simply *avidya* under a different name. The absence of an independent owner of experiencing is not identical with the Buddhist doctrine of *anatman* in its full doctrinal setting. Nirvana does not become another name for absolute freedom in monophenomenalism.

A serious Madhyamaka objection is nevertheless possible: does the one phenomenon itself become a new intrinsic nature? The question concerns not only some potentially concealed substance, essence, or necessary mechanism, but also the status of the assertion “the phenomenon is one”: is an independent, ultimate standing being assigned to it which requires separate justification? The reply “no substance has been introduced” is insufficient here. Monophenomenalism explicitly adopts a positive starting position; adopting it does not demonstrate that it is obligatory for every possible philosophical approach. The objection must be addressed on its merits, rather than deflected by pointing out that the objection itself is also considered within the phenomenon.

Zen does not, in turn, become a method for adopting monophenomenalism. Buddha-nature is not another name for the phenomenon; *zazen* is not an unwritten sixth thesis; and Dogen's “forgetting the self” cannot simply be replaced by the formula “the I is a distinction.” Zen retains its historical and normative independence.

## 12. Daoism and other nondual approaches: unity without a prescribed manner of acting

Dao does not become the phenomenon because of a superficial resemblance between statements about unity. *Wuwei* concerns a particular quality of action, and *ziran* concerns naturalness. These concepts have their own Daoist history and admit different interpretations. Monophenomenalism does not make following *wuwei* or attaining naturalness a condition of the phenomenon's unity.

This does not imply that Daoism posits an original rupture and then demands its overcoming: such an opposition would itself distort Daoist texts. The difference lies not in a doctrine invented for the other tradition but in the status of particular propositions and teachings.

Another possible substitution consists in claiming that all nondual traditions “say the same thing deep down.” Their positive claims, methods of justification, practices, and aims may nevertheless differ fundamentally. Resemblances between particular formulas do not prove the existence of a secret common truth which monophenomenalism is supposedly repeating.

Neither membership in any particular nondual tradition, nor mystical experience, nor a perennialist theory that all traditions are ultimately one follows from the thesis of one phenomenon. Nor does monophenomenalism's difference from those approaches amount to a conclusive refutation of them.

## 13. Unity is not something that must be attained

The difference from teachings about liberation also has an immediate human significance. In monophenomenalism, the phenomenon's unity is not a reward for an inner achievement. There is no prior requirement to eliminate the “I,” purify one's thoughts, cease to fear, or undergo some exceptional state.

The boundary is not between unity that has to be created and unity that already exists: some other teachings, including Advaita Vedanta, likewise do not regard nonduality as something produced by practice. Within those teachings, however, liberating recognition or special knowledge may be of fundamental importance. No obligatory task of such recognition, acquisition of special knowledge, or self-transformation as a condition of the phenomenon's unity follows from monophenomenalism's starting position.

A person may begin the day anxious, make mistakes, feel exhausted, and reach evening without finding peace. On another day, the same person may understand what is happening clearly and act with confidence. The differences between these states are real for human life, but neither makes the phenomenon less or more one. A change in understanding or self-experience is not a condition of the original unity.

This does not prohibit change. One may learn, practice, seek treatment or help, reconsider habits, and act for those one loves. The reasons may be entirely concrete: pain, love, interest, responsibility, and consequences. None requires declaring existence itself to be in need of prior justification.

On this human reading, absolute freedom means the absence of any requirement derived from the starting position to change oneself first in order to justify one's existence. It is not a property of the phenomenon or a special state one is obliged to experience. It is not omnipotence, an uncaused choice, or guaranteed tranquility. The absence of a metaphysically necessary path to liberation does not abolish promises, care, or practical responsibilities.

Making it mandatory to free oneself even from the task of liberation would simply create another condition on which the starting position does not depend.

## 14. The boundary also applies to convenient conclusions drawn by supporters

A philosophical position can be distorted not only by its critics but also by those who seek to make it more powerful or more comforting.

The original unity entails neither personal immortality, nor the necessary continuation of impersonal experiencing after death, nor the existence of a posthumous observer. Nor does it establish the opposite universal claim that every possible form of experiencing necessarily ends. Refusing to introduce an independent owner does not prove that human beings are powerless. The distinguishability of action and its consequences is not abolished because the agent is not assigned the status of an independent first principle.

It is especially dangerous to turn the non-introduction of a premise into a new property of the phenomenon. Not introducing time as a fundamental external foundation does not establish eternity. Not requiring an independent controller does not establish chaos or universal predetermination. Not assigning an owner to the phenomenon does not disclose a hidden metaphysical nature of “subjectlessness.”

Such claims can only be examined as new propositions requiring grounds of their own. The position must not be expanded by multiplying promises or by replacing one set of unjustified first principles with another—now constructed from negations.

## 15. How to compare without substituting

A rigorous comparison can proceed through six questions.

1. What exactly does monophenomenalism assert, in its original formulation?
2. Does the claim belong to the position itself, to the model's specialized definitions, or to a later interpretation?
3. What specific claim is made by the theory under comparison, and in what context?
4. Do the meanings of the terms, the premises, and the consequences coincide—or only certain words?
5. What additional assertions had to be introduced to obtain resemblance or identity?
6. What is the actual outcome: agreement on a particular proposition, partial resemblance, compatibility, difference, or contradiction?

If one position asserts something about which another is silent, a contradiction has not yet been established. If an additional theory is compatible with the model, it does not follow that the theory can be derived from the five theses. If agreement in content has been found, borrowing does not thereby follow.

The converse error is just as significant: the independent origin of a position does not in itself establish its truth or its absolute historical novelty. Criticism may examine the initial unity, the precision of definitions, logical transitions, and explanatory limits. It need not first accept the vocabulary of monophenomenalism, but it must specify exactly which of its claims is being challenged.

The comparison with priority monism illustrates how the procedure works. Monophenomenalism's starting position is “Everything that exists is a phenomenon. The phenomenon is one”; the specialized definitions of the model are not required for it. Priority monism asserts the metaphysical priority of the whole over its parts. Both positions speak of unity, but identifying them would require the additional introduction of a relationship of ontological priority. Because the starting position does not establish that relationship, the result is not identity but a difference alongside a possible resemblance between particular themes. In this way, comparison identifies an added premise instead of rewriting the original thesis.

Drawing a clear boundary does not remove the need to answer substantive objections. If an actual contradiction is identified, it must be considered. If a conclusion fails examination, it must be corrected.

## 16. Conclusion. Identification is not a substitute for inquiry

Monophenomenalism does not have to be assigned a place within the taxonomy of monism before it can possess its own content. Its starting position is defined by the assertion of one phenomenon and the refusal to introduce a second independently established foundation as an obligatory premise. The *Model of the Phenomenon* offers a separate instrument of investigation, but neither proves the position nor becomes a compulsory vocabulary for all its possible interpretations.

Monism may provide a broad classificatory category. Idealism, phenomenalism, neutral monism, Buddhism, Advaita, Zen, and Daoism may each show particular points of contact. But none of these resemblances licenses replacing the phenomenon with a substance, experiencing with an owner, distinction with error, conceptual structuring with the production of the world, or absolute freedom with a mandatory spiritual achievement.

Distortion rarely begins with an open rejection of the original theses. More often, a familiar explanation quietly introduces one unnamed premise. That explanation is then cited as part of the starting position, and further conclusions are built upon it. It is therefore necessary to examine not only a conclusion but every step leading to it.

Independence does not mean immunity from examination, and it does not require denying real similarities. It means that the position must be considered according to its own content rather than through a philosophical genealogy assigned to it in advance. Criticism may challenge its foundations; classification may note similarities. Neither is entitled to change what is actually asserted without proof.

Resemblance is a reason to compare. Not permission to substitute.
