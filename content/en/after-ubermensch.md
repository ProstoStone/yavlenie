Why existence needs no ultimate affirmation: monophenomenalism in dialogue with Nietzsche.

## The human being as a bridge

One of the most radical images in European philosophy is that of a human being who is not the final goal of himself.

For Friedrich Nietzsche, the human being is a bridge. Not a completed form, not a final destination, not a creature whose purpose is merely to preserve an established nature. Human greatness lies in the capacity to cross over, overcome, and become otherwise.

The Übermensch is not simply a stronger or more successful representative of humanity. It represents the possibility of a life that no longer receives its values from an inherited higher authority. Once the unconditional foundation of the old morality has been lost, the problem cannot be solved merely by returning to familiar commandments.

But if inherited values no longer command unconditional allegiance, what remains?

One answer is to create new ones.

To stop obeying a ready-made measure and become capable of affirming one's own life. To stop waiting for the world to provide justification and learn to say Yes to it. To cease treating oneself as a finished thing and participate in one's own becoming.

This is a powerful answer. It offers no consolation postponed to another world. It addresses life itself.

And yet, after the rejection of inherited foundations, another question can be heard:

**Why should existence require any affirmation at all—even the freest, most courageous, most creative affirmation?**

Could the search for ultimate justification end, not because another justification has been created, but because justification is no longer required?

This is not a question about how to become greater than the Übermensch.

It is a question about whether one must become anything for what exists to be allowed to be as it is.

## Nietzsche had already dismantled the independent culprit

The easiest way to object to Nietzsche would be to portray him as a defender of absolute free will: as though an independent self created itself, chose its values, and bore ultimate responsibility for that choice.

But there is little point in refuting such a Nietzsche. He is far removed from Nietzsche's actual texts.

In §21 of *Beyond Good and Evil*, Nietzsche attacks the idea that a person could be the cause of himself. More strikingly, he also warns against the opposite construction, the “unfree will,” in which causality becomes a mechanical scheme claiming to provide the final explanation of action.

In the first essay of *On the Genealogy of Morality*, §13, he challenges our habit of imagining a separate doer behind a deed, as though an independent bearer of force existed first and then decided whether to exercise it.

In *Twilight of the Idols*, in “The Four Great Errors,” he connects the idea of free will to the practice of assigning guilt and insists that human existence cannot be detached from the whole within which it takes place.

These arguments already come close to a question raised by *Model of the Phenomenon*.

When we say, “I decided,” there is a decision, the experience of choosing, memories of circumstances, distinctions among possible outcomes, and subsequent actions. But where is the additional chooser, independent of all of these, who first exists in his own right and then takes possession of what happens?

Nietzsche does not require a naive belief in such a chooser. Our position cannot establish its difference by refuting one.

The disagreement, then, must be more subtle.

It begins where the critique of an independent doer meets the positive ideal of human greatness.

If there is no autonomous owner of deeds, in what sense must a human being become someone capable of affirming life? And is that capacity a condition of anything beyond human evaluation itself?

## The camel, the lion, and the child

In Zarathustra's speech “On the Three Metamorphoses,” the spirit passes through the images of a camel, a lion, and a child.

The camel bears weight. It is capable of carrying what has been commanded and testing itself against the severity of a demand.

The lion rebels against the command. Where the old authority says “You shall,” it answers “I will.” But its liberation is still predominantly negative: it can reclaim space from the old command, whereas creating new values requires a different movement.

The child becomes an image of a new beginning, play, innocence, and affirmation.

This is one of Nietzsche's most beautiful constructions, for its final figure is neither a ruler endowed with unlimited strength nor a judge laying down the ultimate law. It is a child at play.

Here Nietzsche comes close to a freedom that cannot be reduced to practical advantage.

But consider the structure of the transformation.

The camel bears. The lion is freed *from*. The child can create.

Liberation is associated with movement from one form of existence to another. Even if this is not a universal obligation but a philosophical image of a particular kind of life, it retains a direction: something is overcome, and another possibility is valued more highly.

Nietzsche is entitled to defend that distinction. It need not amount to a command issued to everyone.

But another question now arises:

**What if a person does not even need to become the child?**

What if the camel, the lion, and the child require no final evaluation on the part of the phenomenon as a whole?

One may continue bearing a burden. One may revolt. One may play. One may pass from one to another—or undergo no discernible transformation.

Within human life, these differences have different meanings and consequences. Yet it does not follow that the phenomenon itself must prefer one form over another.

We can admire Nietzsche's child without turning that admiration into the structure of reality.

Here begins the possibility of thinking a freedom that requires no transition into freedom.

## The great Yes, and the question that follows it

Nietzsche's most extreme test of life-affirmation is associated with eternal recurrence.

In §341 of *The Gay Science*, he asks us to imagine that our life—with every joy, suffering, decision, and smallest detail—must recur again, innumerable times.

This image admits different readings. It may be discussed as a thought experiment, a cosmological hypothesis, or a way of testing one's relationship to one's own existence. The precise status of eternal recurrence in Nietzsche remains a matter of interpretation.

What matters here is the possibility of reading it as a test of affirmation.

Could you wish to repeat not only your happiest moments but everything that happened? Could you affirm a life without requiring its past to be corrected?

Nietzsche is not merely asking whether life is pleasant. He takes the question to its extreme: can someone accept it in its entirety, without mentally removing what they would rather not have experienced?

Before such a test, familiar justifications lose their force. One cannot say, “I agree to my life provided that everything I dislike is taken out of it.” The imagined recurrence preserves even what one wishes had never occurred.

And precisely here we can ask a more radical question.

**What happens to existence if a person cannot say Yes to it?**

Have they failed the test?

Within a particular system of evaluation, perhaps. If the criterion is the capacity for unconditional affirmation, an inability to affirm becomes a significant distinction.

But does it follow that life itself has proved inadequate?

No. That conclusion would require showing that the criterion of affirmation is binding upon existence as such, rather than upon a particular philosophical ideal.

Imagine someone who refuses to wish for the repetition of their past. They remember a loss, a period of pain, or an action they regret. They do not want to go through it again.

Their refusal, too, takes place. It needs no additional approval in order to belong to what is.

We may offer that person a different assessment of life. We may investigate their suffering. We may try to help. But the refusal alone does not establish that the phenomenon as a whole fails to meet a standard to which it must conform.

A difference emerges:

**Nietzschean life-affirmation is an extreme possibility of one's relation to life. The monophenomenalist approach does not require life itself to receive affirmation from a separate judge.**

Even the refusal to affirm does not create an external standpoint outside everything that happens.

It therefore need not be overcome for the phenomenon to obtain final justification.

## Everything is one phenomenon: what changes in the question?

The starting position of monophenomenalism is expressed in the thesis:

**Everything is one phenomenon.**

In *Model of the Phenomenon*, it receives further conceptual development: the phenomenon is understood as sensory experiencing; a distinction within it is a concept; and concepts structure the phenomenon into experience.

Precision matters here.

This does not mean that behind people, bodies, cities, history, and nature there hides another enormous entity called “the phenomenon.”

It does not mean that the world's diversity must be declared imaginary.

Nor does it mean that the single phenomenon is a secret subject governing everything.

“One,” “phenomenon,” “distinction,” and “concept” are themselves conceptual distinctions through which the starting position is expressed. They must not quietly become independent objects beyond what is being described.

The question of the Übermensch therefore changes shape.

Instead of asking primarily which form of humanity ought to replace the old one, we ask on what grounds the phenomenon itself—within which human beings, change, higher and lower, desire and evaluation are distinguished—must conform to any form.

Suppose a person creates new values.

This can be distinguished as human activity with conditions, consequences, and significance. It can change societies and individual lives. It can be beautiful or cruel, fruitful or destructive.

But must there also exist an independent criterion by which this activity justifies the entire phenomenon?

There is no need to introduce such a criterion merely because we find the activity significant.

Suppose instead that someone creates nothing. This does not establish that the phenomenon lacks a creator, either.

**The possibility of evaluation within the phenomenon does not turn evaluation into a condition of the phenomenon's existence.**

This is not a strict logical consequence of the single thesis “Everything is one phenomenon.” It also depends on the working definitions of subject, distinction, freedom, purpose, and responsibility employed in *Model of the Phenomenon*.

Preserving the status of this argument allows us to investigate its force without passing off an interpretation as a proven theorem.

With these definitions in place, there is no need to assume a separate authority before which what happens must earn the right to be.

## Who owns the task of self-overcoming?

The idea of self-overcoming has a curious feature.

When someone says, “I must overcome myself,” we often imagine two figures.

One is the person as they are now, the one who must be overcome.

The other is whoever can already evaluate that present condition, pass judgment on it, and direct a change.

Yet if we examine experience, both figures are found within the same happening.

There is dissatisfaction with oneself. There is an imagined future. There is desire, the memory of failure, and the actions through which a person may actually change. There are the distinctions “better,” “worse,” “stronger,” and “weaker.”

Where among these is a separate entity existing before and independently of all such distinctions?

We may posit one. But why is it necessary to describe what happens?

Human change does not disappear without that assumption.

People learn, train, write books, alter their habits, repair relationships, and destroy them. Their actions have causes and consequences. Society evaluates them. They experience pride, regret, and hope.

But none of this requires an additional owner of change who must produce himself out of nothing.

This allows a different reading of the phrase “become who you are.”

If it refers to the development of character, the cultivation of capacities, and an engagement with circumstances, it can be an expressive description of life. There is no contradiction in that.

But if it is understood as a demand imposed on existence by a separate higher self, we may ask where this self comes from and to whom it issues its demands.

Monophenomenalism does not prohibit self-overcoming.

Nor does it demand the opposite: that one permanently renounce change.

It simply has no need for an independent fundamental doer and an ultimate goal that must explain or justify the very fact of change.

Self-overcoming can be distinguished within the phenomenon.

**The obligation to overcome oneself is not an obligation imposed upon the phenomenon itself.**

## Where Nietzsche almost speaks our answer

At this point an honest dialogue with Nietzsche becomes particularly difficult.

In *Twilight of the Idols*, he writes that no one is responsible for the bare fact of their existence, that an individual's fate cannot be torn away from the whole, and that outside that whole there is no tribunal capable of condemning it from a final standpoint.

Is this not almost our conclusion?

Yes. Ignoring the resemblance would ruin the inquiry.

Nietzsche does not simply replace free will with determinism. His critique also targets the treatment of cause and effect as independent metaphysical entities.

We therefore cannot portray his philosophy as a prison of necessity from which monophenomenalism liberates humanity for the first time.

Perhaps, at this level, Nietzsche has no need for an “after” at all.

Yet his philosophy retains another dimension: pronounced evaluations of particular ways of existing. Strength, creativity, the capacity to affirm, self-overcoming, the art of giving style to one's character—all have special significance for him.

This is not necessarily a universal command. Nietzsche need not believe that everyone should embody the same ideal in the same way.

Nevertheless, his philosophy distinguishes higher and lower forms of human life. This distinction is not an incidental turn of phrase; it belongs to his positive philosophical project.

Monophenomenalism need not dispute such evaluations *as human evaluations*.

It raises a different question: **why should any of these distinctions become the final measure of existence?**

The answer may be simple: it need not.

Nietzsche may prefer creation over submission, affirmation over denial, strength over weakness. We may understand the grounds of these preferences and even share them.

But no preference becomes a law external to the phenomenon merely because it is philosophically profound.

The difference is not between a thinker who understands the unity of the world and one who fails to understand it.

It lies between **a project that evaluates particular forms of life** and **an inquiry into whether any ultimate evaluation is required of the phenomenon as a whole**.

## Is this new freedom another ideal?

A powerful objection can be raised against the proposed position.

We say that one need not become the Übermensch, create meaning, or affirm life. But is this not itself a new ideal: someone who depends on nothing?

Are we not replacing the Übermensch with an even more unattainable figure, someone who has finally rid themselves of every purpose, fear, and desire?

If so, the objection is justified.

Such a person would be yet another project of self-overcoming. They would have to keep checking themselves: Is desire still present? Has fear returned? Am I pursuing a goal? Have I lost my freedom?

Every powerful feeling would then become a threat, and the desire to live would count as a failure.

But that would mean we had misunderstood our own point.

**Absolute freedom is not a quality possessed by someone who has succeeded in removing every limitation.**

In the strict terms of *Model of the Phenomenon*, even the expression “absolute freedom” remains a concept. It does not refer to a separate object beyond the phenomenon, nor to an independent property the phenomenon must possess.

When freedom is discussed in this limiting sense, we are not discovering a new state that was previously absent. We are no longer requiring an independent subject standing on the other side of the phenomenon and constrained by it as a whole.

The need to understand this idea is absent as well.

A person may consider themselves unfree.

They may resent their life.

They may devote years to an ambitious undertaking, seek recognition, struggle with themselves, create values, and experience fear and regret.

They do not thereby suffer a metaphysical fall from absolute freedom.

They simply live—with all the distinctions that make their life what it is.

If freedom requires the absence of desire, it depends upon desire.

If it requires the absence of fear, it depends upon fear.

If it requires a special understanding, it depends upon that understanding.

**A freedom that requires nothing cannot depend even upon the acknowledgment of freedom.**

It is not a higher human ability.

It is the refusal to assume the fundamental unfreedom of an independent owner of the phenomenon.

## Does that mean it makes no difference how we live?

No. But we should see precisely why.

When one person causes another pain, the pain does not disappear because “other,” “person,” and “responsibility” are distinctions within experience.

If someone has lost their home, the assertion that the phenomenon requires no justification will not keep them warm.

If an action has grave consequences, those consequences remain significant within human relationships, law, medicine, and everyday life.

A common confusion occurs here.

From “there is no independent judge of the entire phenomenon,” one concludes, “there is no reason to care about another person's suffering.”

But the second proposition does not follow from the first.

Reasons to help, refrain from violence, keep promises, or answer for one's actions can be distinguished within human experience. They do not require morality to become a law beyond the world.

Responsibility is meaningful where participants, actions, consequences, and rules connecting them can be distinguished.

It need not become metaphysical guilt for the existence of the phenomenon as a whole.

Nietzsche himself offered a radical critique of this kind of guilt. There may be little disagreement with him on that point.

The issue is not permission to abandon practical responsibility, but the scope of responsibility itself.

Within human life it remains an important instrument of living together.

Yet we cannot appoint someone ultimately responsible for the whole without separating that person from the very whole for which they are supposed to answer.

A person can take consequences seriously without regarding themselves as the absolute source of existence.

They can correct mistakes without supposing that the entire phenomenon has made a mistake.

They can help others without proclaiming themselves the savior of the world.

They can write philosophy without transforming it into a mission on which the final worth of life depends.

## Nietzsche's objection: is this a withdrawal from life?

Let us imagine an objection Nietzsche might make.

You say that nothing needs to be overcome, nothing needs to be affirmed, and nothing needs to be created. But is this not exhaustion disguised as philosophy?

Does the person who has ceased to strive not resemble the “last man,” preferring safety, comfort, and the absence of risk?

This would be a serious objection if the proposed position asserted, “It is better to desire nothing, do nothing, and become involved in nothing.”

But it does not.

The absence of a mandatory goal is not a prohibition against having goals.

The absence of mandatory self-overcoming is not a prohibition against overcoming oneself.

The absence of an ultimate justification for creativity does not mean that creation is pointless.

Someone may be passionate, active, daring. They may want to create something new. Nothing in monophenomenalism requires them to become motionless in order to be free.

But a person may also simply sit beneath a tree.

The point is not secretly to declare one of these ways of being freer than the other.

Even the image of a peaceful person without desires must not become our new icon of perfection.

Some people may genuinely prefer a life without grand projects. Others need challenge and resistance. Still others change direction throughout their lives.

These differences matter within experience.

But the freedom in question is not distributed among them as a reward.

**Nietzsche is entitled to ask which life is higher. We are entitled to ask why the phenomenon must answer that question.**

They are different questions.

## What if Nietzsche has already answered?

An even more difficult objection remains.

Perhaps we are not moving beyond Nietzsche at all, but merely repeating his thought of the innocence of becoming in other words.

Perhaps his Übermensch needs no ultimate justification and creates simply because creation happens. Perhaps Nietzsche's child already plays without a metaphysical obligation to play. Perhaps affirmation is not an examination one must pass, but the expression of a particular way of existing.

This interpretation deserves serious attention.

If it is right, we cannot present monophenomenalism as a refutation of Nietzsche or a necessary next stage of his philosophy.

Nevertheless, a distinct inquiry remains possible.

Instead of studying which values belong to a higher form of life, we can investigate **the status of the distinction between higher and lower itself**.

Instead of asking whether a human being can ultimately affirm the world, we can ask whether the world requires that affirmation.

Instead of searching for a form of existence liberated from an earlier dependency, we can ask whether there is a fundamentally independent subject whose freedom needs to be secured.

These questions do not make Nietzsche wrong.

But they change the direction of philosophical inquiry.

In Nietzsche we encounter a profound critique of inherited foundations and an equally profound effort to think human greatness after their collapse.

Here we investigate the possibility that the very requirement of greatness need not be the starting task.

This is not proof of philosophical superiority.

It is a different way of posing the problem—and perhaps another limit of the same movement of thought.

## After the Übermensch

Imagine someone who has spent a long time searching for a foundation for their life.

At first, they wanted to meet other people's expectations. Later they realized that those expectations held no unconditional authority.

They decided to define themselves.

And this was liberating.

They made plans, created values, learned to accept defeat, turn suffering into strength, and find beauty even in what they had not chosen.

But one day another question arose:

**Why must I do even this?**

Why must suffering be transformed into strength?

Why must the past be affirmed?

Why must life become a work of art?

Why must I say Yes to it for it to be what it already is?

The question did not destroy their plans or prohibit creativity.

It merely showed that none of these actions is an ultimate condition of existence.

The person could still write a book. Or stop writing.

They could strive for something great. Or go for a walk.

They could say Yes to life. Or say nothing at all.

The differences between these possibilities remained. What disappeared was the requirement to turn one of them into the final justification of everything that happens.

And one last step matters here: we must not turn this person into a new figure of superiority.

Calling them “someone who has gone beyond the Übermensch” would be to construct yet another ladder, appoint a highest rung, and prescribe a direction that existence is meant to follow.

But the proposed thought requires no ladder.

Nor does it require the ladder to be destroyed.

A person can keep climbing. They can stop. They can discover that they no longer care to compare their height with anyone else's.

**After the Übermensch, there need not be anyone else.**

One need not become stronger.

One need not become freer.

One need not arrive at unconditional agreement with life.

One need not even give up all these aspirations.

The world does not have to be affirmed in order to be.

A person does not have to overcome themselves in order to be themselves.

And the phenomenon needs no one granted the ultimate authority to permit it to happen.

One can go on creating values. One can love Nietzsche. One can argue with him. One can pass through his philosophy and discover that one no longer needs either to follow him or to defeat him.

Not because a final teaching has been found to which one must now submit.

But because **the very need for a final teaching is no longer required**.

This is not a victory over the Übermensch.

It is the freedom not even to become one.
