## Part I. Buddhism: conditionality, not-self, emptiness, and liberation

The philosophical foundations of Buddhist teaching and their development. An independent study of Buddhist philosophy.

### Introduction. Scope and limits

Buddhism is a philosophical and religious tradition spanning many centuries, in which questions of existence, consciousness, knowledge, suffering, and liberation develop in close relation to one another. It is often summarised in a few formulas: everything is impermanent, there is no permanent self, everything is empty, and liberation is achieved by ending desire. These formulas are familiar, but without context they can create a misleading impression of simplicity.

What exactly does the teaching of not-self deny? How are consciousness and the continuation of life possible without an unchanging owner? What does dependent origination mean? Why is emptiness not equivalent to nonexistence? How are nirvana, practice, ethics, and compassion related? Finally, why do some Mahayana texts speak of “original purity” and even a “true self”?

Here we offer a research-oriented reconstruction of the main strands of Buddhist philosophy, rather than a definitive account of all its schools. We begin with the early teachings, then turn to Abhidharma analysis, Madhyamaka, Yogacara, and teachings on Buddha-nature. “Early Buddhism” refers primarily to the available early collections of texts, not to a historically verified verbatim record of the Buddha’s speech. Later philosophical developments are not retrospectively attributed to the early discourses.

The purpose is to establish which claims each tradition accepts, why they are connected, and which questions remain disputed. We need not settle these disputes on behalf of Buddhist philosophy, and we will not use its terms as concealed names for the concepts of another system.

### 1. The central question: what sustains dukkha?

Early Buddhist teachings give priority to a question: why is life in samsara accompanied by dukkha—suffering and unsatisfactoriness—what conditions give rise to it, and can those conditions cease?

Samsara is understood as more than the psychological repetition of habits within a single lifetime. In traditional Buddhism it is the cycle of birth, death, and subsequent birth, connected with karma and ignorance. At the same time, Buddhist inquiry addresses very concrete experience: the body, feeling, intentions, attachments, change, and loss.

What matters is not merely the observation that pleasant things end, but our relationship to what is conditioned. We try to hold on to what changes, make the dependent independent, and find final security in what we cannot fully control. Buddhist philosophy examines this attempt together with the fear, craving, clinging, and dissatisfaction that arise from it.

Dukkha does not mean that every moment of life is agonising. Buddhism recognises pleasure, joy, goodwill, peace, and love. But no pleasant conditioned state, by itself, provides ultimate stability.

Traditionally, a distinction is made between straightforward suffering, the dukkha of change, and the dukkha of conditioned formations. The first includes pain and grief; the second points to the unreliability of pleasure; the third concerns the unsatisfactoriness of dependent existence itself as a possible absolute support. These distinctions help explain why liberation cannot be reduced to uninterrupted pleasure.

### 2. The Four Noble Truths

The Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta (SN 56.11) presents the path through the Four Noble Truths.

The first is dukkha. The susceptibility of conditioned existence to suffering and unsatisfactoriness is to be fully understood.

The second is the origin of dukkha. Tanha—craving—has particular importance. SN 56.11 names three forms: craving for sensual pleasure (kama-tanha), craving for existence or becoming (bhava-tanha), and craving for nonexistence or the ending of becoming (vibhava-tanha). Craving should not be identified with every intention, every wish to end pain, or every reasonable action to remove a danger.

The third is the cessation of dukkha. The cessation of craving and the clinging it conditions opens the possibility of liberation.

The fourth is the path. The Noble Eightfold Path brings together wisdom, ethical discipline, and cultivation of the mind.

These truths set out not only four claims but four tasks: to understand dukkha, abandon its origin, realise cessation, and develop the path. Familiarity with Buddhist formulas is therefore not regarded as equivalent to liberating understanding. In the Buddhist tradition, knowledge is connected with practical transformation in how one perceives and acts.

### 3. Three characteristics: impermanence, dukkha, and anatta

Three characteristics are important to the analysis of conditioned existence.

Anitya (Pali: anicca) means impermanence. Conditioned formations arise, change, and cease. This applies to the body, feeling, intentions, recognition, and states of consciousness.

Dukkha means unsatisfactoriness. What changes and depends on conditions cannot guarantee final satisfaction. Change is not always unpleasant, but an attempt to find unconditional support in what changes remains vulnerable.

Anatman (Pali: anatta) means not-self. What we take to be “I” and “mine” is not found to be a permanent, independent, and fully controllable self.

The classical formulas say that all conditioned formations are impermanent, all conditioned formations are subject to dukkha, but all dharmas are not-self. Their different scopes matter: traditionally, the third statement is taken to include the unconditioned. The three characteristics should therefore not be treated mechanically as three identical kinds of property applying to exactly the same set of things.

Another point is especially important: not-self is not equivalent to saying that people do not exist at all. Buddhist texts continue to speak of people, actions, responsibility, and liberation. What they investigate is the claim to an unchanging, independent essence that could be definitively identified with a person or made the owner of all that person’s processes.

### 4. The five aggregates and the question of “I”

Early texts examine human existence through five aggregates (skandhas; Pali: khandhas):

1. Rupa: form, embodiment, and material constituents.
2. Vedana: the pleasant, unpleasant, or neutral tone of feeling; not all emotions in general.
3. Samjna (Pali: sanna): recognition of features and perception.
4. Samskara (Pali: sankhara): volitional and other formative factors.
5. Vijnana (Pali: vinnana): conditioned consciousness.

These are not five successive stages through which a single experience must pass. They are analytical aspects of what is ordinarily regarded as a person and that person’s life.

Suppose someone hears an insulting remark. We can distinguish what is heard, its unpleasant tone, recognition of its meaning, an intention to reply, memories, and thoughts about oneself. The ordinary expression “I was insulted” is not prohibited. But an investigative question arises: which of these distinguished contents is the unchanging self to which all the others belong?

The Anattalakkhana Sutta (SN 22.59) applies the analysis to all five aggregates, including consciousness. They change, are subject to dukkha, and are not under a person’s unconditional control. They should therefore not be taken, in an ultimate sense, as “mine,” “what I am,” or “my self.”

This does not imply that one person’s pain is equivalent to another person’s report of it, or that social responsibility is cancelled. Rejecting an unchanging owner does not cancel meaningful differences between people and events.

### 5. Anatta and the middle way

The question “Does an independent self exist?” can easily become a choice between an eternal soul and its final annihilation. Early Buddhism seeks to examine that choice rather than simply accept it.

In SN 44.10, the Buddha refrains from answering either that a self exists or that it does not exist in his conversation with Vacchagotta. This does not make the teaching of anatta indeterminate. It shows how the same words, framed differently, can support the extremes of eternalism or annihilationism.

In the Kaccanagotta Sutta (SN 12.15), the extremes “everything exists” and “nothing exists” are connected with a failure to understand arising and cessation. The middle way is presented through conditionality, not by postulating a hidden permanent self.

Anatta, then, is more than a philosophical slogan. It examines the appropriation of changing processes as an independent “I” and the relationship of that appropriation to clinging. Buddhism is nevertheless not reducible to a thesis that human beings do not exist in the ordinary, practical sense.

### 6. Dependent origination

Pratitya-samutpada (Pali: paticca-samuppada) means dependent, or conditioned, origination. In its general formulation: with the presence of particular conditions, something arises; with their cessation, what they condition ceases.

This is neither the empty claim that “everything is connected to everything” nor merely a physical theory of causation. The early texts focus on the patterns that sustain samsara and dukkha.

The classical account (SN 12.2) considers twelve links: ignorance, formative activities, consciousness, name-and-form, the six sense bases, contact, feeling, craving, clinging, becoming, birth, and ageing and death. Different traditions explain their relationship and temporal structure differently; they cannot simply be presented as twelve moments within a single biography.

The movement from contact to feeling, and then to craving and clinging, is particularly important. An unpleasant sensation may arise without a person intending it. Subsequent rejection, fear of the future, appropriation of the situation, and attempts to secure permanence at any cost are additional conditioned processes.

This does not mean that every effort to reduce pain counts as Buddhist craving. Concern for health and reasonable action can remain. What is examined is a particular form of clinging, not a requirement to stop caring about life.

Dependent origination explains more than connectedness. Because dukkha arises under conditions, the conditions sustaining it can cease. This connection makes conditionality essential to the Buddhist account of liberation.

### 7. Consciousness: conditionality rather than an eternal observer

Early texts distinguish six kinds of consciousness: visual, auditory, olfactory, gustatory, bodily, and mental. The mind also participates in knowing thoughts and other mental contents.

MN 38 rejects the idea that one and the same consciousness travels unchanged between births. Consciousness is considered in relation to the conditions under which it arises. This matters for anatta: one cannot simply exclude the body and thoughts from the true self, then declare unchanging consciousness to be the ultimate “I,” without changing the Buddhist position.

Later traditions develop more complex explanations of cognition, continuity, and the relationship between perceiver and perceived. These cannot be reduced to the six kinds of consciousness in the early analysis. Yogacara is particularly important and will be considered separately.

### 8. Ignorance, craving, and clinging

Avidya (Pali: avijja) is not simply a lack of information acquired through study. A person can be well read and familiar with Buddhist concepts while still retaining ignorance in the sense relevant to the path.

Ignorance is connected with a failure to understand the Four Noble Truths, impermanence, not-self, and dependent origination. It sustains mistaken ways of relating to what happens, including the idea of a stable “I” and the possibility of permanently securing what is conditioned.

The tradition identifies three unwholesome roots: greed, hatred, and delusion. But Buddhism does not declare every desire bad. An intention to learn, care for someone, speak truthfully, or reduce harm can be wholesome. Nor does it seek to destroy thinking as such: reasoning and discernment are necessary for study and practice.

Ignorance is therefore not equivalent to conceptuality in general. It names a particular failure of understanding connected with the causation of suffering. This distinction must also be preserved when discussing schools that investigate designation and the construction of a picture of the world in detail.

### 9. Karma, samsara, and continuity without an unchanging soul

In traditional Buddhism, karma is primarily connected with intentional actions and their consequences. It encompasses not only the psychological habits of this life, but also continuity through births within samsara.

This creates a familiar philosophical difficulty: if there is no permanent self, what exactly is reborn, and to whom do the consequences of earlier actions belong?

Buddhist traditions respond with different theories of conditioned continuity. A common theme is that the continuation of causally connected processes does not require an unchanging soul to move between bodies. Schools nevertheless differ in how they explain the mechanism or kind of dependence that makes such continuity possible.

Two boundaries matter here. First, karma and rebirth are substantive traditional claims, not merely metaphors for habits. Second, their religious and philosophical status does not make rebirth an established scientific fact.

### 10. Abhidharma: what makes up experience without a permanent person?

Abhidharma systematises early Buddhist analysis by classifying dharmas: factors of existence and cognition. Depending on the school, these can include feelings, intentions, material factors, moments of consciousness, and other elements.

The aim is to explain meaningful processes without presupposing an eternal subject who owns them. Yet analytical decomposition itself raises a new question: if the person is conventional, are the dharmas ultimately real, independent elements?

Answers differ. Theravada Abhidhamma, Sarvastivada, Sautrantika, and other traditions develop their own classifications and explanations. Sarvastivada is known for the doctrine that dharmas exist in all three times. Sautrantika criticises a number of Abhidharma doctrines, especially Sarvastivadin ones, including this account of the three times. The term svabhava—“own-nature”—does not carry exactly the same force in every source either: it can indicate a dharma’s distinctive characteristic or a stronger ontological commitment.

It is therefore incorrect to describe all Abhidharma as a theory of eternal, indivisible substances. But the question of the ultimate existence of the factors under analysis becomes decisive for Madhyamaka.

### 11. Madhyamaka: emptiness and the absence of intrinsic existence

One of Mahayana’s most important thinkers is Nagarjuna, associated with the formation of the Madhyamaka school. His principal work is the Mulamadhyamakakarika, the “Root verses on the middle way.”

Madhyamaka develops the teaching of shunyata—emptiness. Emptiness does not mean a complete absence of existence. It indicates the absence of svabhava understood as an independent intrinsic nature.

#### Conditionality and intrinsic nature

If a thing exists entirely independently, its existence should not be determined by conditions. But dependent origination means that arising and change depend on conditions. Existence determined by conditions is therefore incompatible with independent intrinsic existence in the same respect.

Nagarjuna applies this analysis to causes, effects, time, motion, persons, consciousness, and other categories. At the beginning of the Mulamadhyamakakarika, he examines four possibilities for arising: from itself, from another, from both, or without a cause. Difficulties arise when causes and effects are assumed to be independently existing entities.

Arising “from itself” is problematic: if a thing already exists in its own right, why would it still need to arise? Arising “from something wholly other” likewise requires an explanation of why this particular independent source produces this particular effect. Combining these possibilities does not necessarily remove their difficulties; causeless arising, meanwhile, would undermine the meaningfulness of conditionality.

The argument is therefore directed not against causation, but against the supposed need to conceive of causes and effects as independently established principles.

#### Emptiness is not nonbeing

Consider a tree. It grows through water, light, soil, and many processes. It has a shape, provides shade, may bear fruit, and can die. None of these determinations disappears because the tree depends on conditions.

But if we try to find the tree as a self-existing entity entirely independent of every relationship and condition, a philosophical difficulty arises. Emptiness concerns precisely this independent intrinsic existence, not the absence of a tree in every sense.

In Mulamadhyamakakarika 24.18, Nagarjuna connects dependent origination, emptiness, and dependent designation. These are not three substances or stages in the production of a world. The connection means that what arises dependently lacks an independent intrinsic nature and is understood through dependent relationships and designations.

Emptiness does not destroy dependent origination. It allows us to avoid attributing independent intrinsic existence to what is conditioned.

In chapter 24, Nagarjuna answers the objection that emptiness makes the Four Noble Truths, practice, and liberation impossible. On the contrary: if things existed absolutely unchangeably through their own nature, it would be difficult to explain change and the cessation of suffering.

#### The two truths

Madhyamaka distinguishes conventional and ultimate truth. Conventional truth concerns practical and cognitive distinctions: people, objects, causes, actions, tests, help, and harm. Ultimate truth discloses the absence of an independent intrinsic nature in what is being considered.

These are not two universes positioned one behind the other. Ultimate analysis does not necessarily reveal a separate hidden substance behind the conventional world. Conventional truth, in turn, does not become absolute nothingness. Medicine can help a sick person even though neither person, illness, nor medicine possesses an independent intrinsic nature.

In verses 24.8–10, Nagarjuna emphasises the necessity of the two truths: the ultimate cannot be taught without reliance on the conventional, and liberation cannot be attained without understanding the ultimate. Later Madhyamaka schools disagree about exactly how their relationship should be described.

#### The emptiness of emptiness

If emptiness is declared the one independently existing absolute thing, philosophy once again acquires an independent principle—precisely what it sought to abandon. Emptiness itself is therefore also empty of independent intrinsic existence.

This does not make it meaningless. It identifies a particular mistake: attributing intrinsic existence to things and relationships. But it must not become a new object of clinging. In 13.8, Nagarjuna warns against turning emptiness into a fixed view.

#### Samsara and nirvana

Mulamadhyamakakarika 25.19–20 discusses the absence of an ultimate difference between samsara and nirvana. This does not mean that suffering and liberation are indistinguishable at the level of the Buddhist path, or that ethical action becomes meaningless.

On this reading, samsara and nirvana cannot be presented as two independently self-existing realms. Liberation retains its significance, but it too must not become an independently existing substance. The meaning of these verses depends on the interpretation of ultimate analysis; without further argument, they should not be turned into a claim that all states of existence are identical.

#### What Madhyamaka establishes

Madhyamaka does not affirm absolute nothingness or replace diversity with a final entity called “emptiness.” It shows the connection between dependent origination and the absence of an independent intrinsic nature, while conventional existence retains its meaningful content.

For Nagarjuna, emptiness is neither nonbeing, cosmic matter, nor a hidden ground of things. Madhyamaka analysis of the absence of independent intrinsic existence applies not only to conditioned phenomena, but also to nirvana and to emptiness itself.

### 12. Yogacara: consciousness and the division between perceiver and perceived

Yogacara is another major philosophical tradition of Mahayana, associated above all with Asanga and Vasubandhu. It is often called the teaching of “mind-only” or “representation-only” (citta-matra, vijnapti-matra).

But “everything exists in my head” is far too crude a translation. Yogacara examines how the experience of objects depends on cognition, the mistaken separation of perceiver and perceived, karmic continuity, and the transformation of consciousness on the path to liberation. Its different texts and interpreters do not always give the same answer about the status of external objects.

#### Store consciousness and karmic seeds

To explain continuity without an unchanging self, some Yogacara systems develop a theory of eight consciousnesses. Alongside the six kinds of consciousness, they introduce klishta-manas—the afflicted mind inclined towards appropriation—and alaya-vijnana, store consciousness.

Associated with the latter are “seeds” (bija): karmic dispositions that mature under appropriate conditions. This is an attempt to explain the continuity of processes even though individual states of consciousness arise and disappear.

Alaya-vijnana must not automatically become a new Atman. In classical explanations it is conditioned and changing; in some interpretations, its functioning is transformed or ceases on the path to liberation. Nevertheless, whether this theory adequately explains personal and karmic continuity remains a substantive philosophical question.

#### The experience of objects and “representation-only”

In the Twenty verses, Vasubandhu considers objections to representation-only. Why are experiences ordered in space and time? How is similarity between the experiences of different beings possible? Does this not prove that external objects exist independently in precisely the way the realist describes?

Vasubandhu uses dreams and other states to show that the regularity of experience in place, time, and agreement between perceptions does not by itself prove the necessary existence of independent material objects. But his argument is not limited to this objection to realism: he also examines contradictions in explanations of sensory objects through indivisible atoms, their collections, or a whole understood as an entity distinct from its parts. Vasubandhu thus defends his own representation-only thesis rather than simply withholding ontological conclusions. Scholars disagree about how strong a denial of external objects should be attributed to him; the dispute concerns the interpretation of his positive position, not whether he has one.

#### The three natures

The theory of trisvabhava distinguishes three natures:

1. Parikalpita, the imagined: the mistakenly established independent duality of perceiver and perceived.
2. Paratantra, the dependent: the conditioned arising of cognitive experience.
3. Parinishpanna, the perfected: the absence of imagined duality in the dependent nature.

These are not necessarily three worlds positioned one behind the other. The theory considers the determinacy of experience, conditionality, and the mistaken attribution of independence.

The limits of interpretation are especially important here. One strand of the tradition can be read as a stronger idealism, another as an investigation of false subject–object duality. There are nevertheless different answers to whether nondual consciousness is ultimate reality and how it relates to emptiness.

Yogacara need not mean either a single person dreaming the world or an eternal cosmic observer. Its own task is to explain cognition, the conditionality of consciousness, and liberation from mistaken division.

### 13. Madhyamaka and Yogacara: difference without artificial opposition

Madhyamaka primarily investigates the absence of independent intrinsic nature in all phenomena, including consciousness and emptiness. Yogacara closely analyses cognitive processes, karmic dispositions, and the way perceiver and perceived come to be established as independent sides.

It would be a mistake to reduce their difference to “Madhyamaka denies the world, while Yogacara affirms consciousness.” Both retain the Buddhist task of liberation, both examine ignorance and dependence, and later thinkers proposed different ways of bringing them into philosophical dialogue.

An overall account should leave open the question of the status of consciousness in particular Yogacara schools and its relationship to Madhyamaka’s denial of intrinsic existence. Shared language about nonduality does not yet establish identical ontological commitments.

### 14. Nirvana: cessation and the unconditioned

Nirvana (Pali: nibbana) is connected with the extinguishing of craving, hatred, and delusion, and with the cessation of bondage to samsara. It is more than emotional calm acquired through a particular technique.

Itivuttaka 44 distinguishes two aspects of nibbana. With nibbana “with residue,” a liberated person is still alive, uses the senses, and can experience the pleasant and unpleasant. But craving, hatred, and delusion have ceased. Nibbana “without residue” is connected with the final ending of conditioned samsaric existence after the liberated person’s death.

An important qualification follows: liberation during life does not require all experiencing or physical pain to disappear. But neither is it exhausted by a psychological change in attitude towards a single biography.

#### The unconditioned

Udana 8.3 describes nibbana in terms of the unborn, unmade, and unconditioned. The unqualified claim that nothing unconditioned exists therefore cannot be attributed to the entire Buddhist tradition.

It does not follow, however, that nirvana is Brahman, an eternal person, or a single substance. Its status is interpreted differently across traditions. Some Abhidharma systems regard it as an unconditioned dharma, while Madhyamaka questions the independent intrinsic existence even of nirvana.

#### The Tathagata after death

In MN 72, the Buddha accepts neither that the Tathagata exists after death, nor that he does not exist, nor both alternatives, nor the negation of both. The example of an extinguished fire shows the limitation of asking where a fire has “gone” when the conditions of its burning have ceased.

This example should not be turned into proof of a universal theory of language. It challenges the transfer of familiar categories of individual personhood to the liberated one. Buddhist tradition reduces nirvana neither to the migration of an eternal soul nor to the destruction of an eternal soul that previously existed.

### 15. Buddha-nature: the possibility of awakening or a positive ultimate nature?

Some Mahayana sutras develop the teaching of tathagatagarbha, often connected with Buddha-nature. Their explanations use images of a jewel hidden under dirt, gold amid impurities, or a Buddha within a withering lotus.

These images can indicate a possibility of awakening that does not have to be produced from nothing, since defilements are not a being’s final nature. But some texts use stronger language, speaking of original purity or an already present perfected nature.

Two claims must be distinguished: “Every being can awaken” and “Every being contains an independent, unchanging essence.” The second cannot automatically be inferred from the first.

#### Positive language about “self”

Some recensions and portions of the Mahayana Mahaparinirvana Sutra do use the language of a true self, atman, in relation to Buddha-nature. This historical fact cannot be dismissed in advance by claiming that all positive language in Buddhism must be a mere metaphor.

But the reverse conclusion—“Buddha-nature must therefore be identical to Advaita’s Atman–Brahman”—also requires a separate argument. Similar words can belong to different philosophical constructions.

The Lankavatara Sutra explicitly asks whether tathagatagarbha is the self of non-Buddhist philosophers; its explanation connects the teaching with emptiness, not-self, and a pedagogical way of giving instruction. Yet not all Buddha-nature texts explain positive language in the same way.

There is therefore no single uncontested formula that captures every historical teaching of Buddha-nature without remainder. A real tension remains between the language of anatta and emptiness and claims about an originally pure nature.

### 16. Arhats, bodhisattvas, and ideals of liberation

In the early tradition, an arhat is someone who has brought the causes of bondage to an end. This liberation is not merely superficial psychological consolation. SN 22.58 distinguishes the Buddha from a liberated disciple, among other things, by the role of independently discovering and teaching the path.

In Mahayana, the bodhisattva ideal becomes central: a being directed towards full awakening for the benefit of all living beings. This is connected with bodhicitta, the intention to awaken joined with concern for the liberation of others.

Buddhist thought does not make another’s suffering insignificant because there is no independent self. On the contrary, care, nonviolence, goodwill, and compassion have a definite place in the Buddhist path.

But an overly easy inference should be avoided: a philosophical claim about emptiness does not automatically produce compassion. Wisdom and compassion are cultivated together through ethical, cognitive, and practical means.

Different traditions evaluate the arhat’s path, a Buddha’s full awakening, and the bodhisattva path differently. We should not declare one ideal inferior on behalf of all schools without examining the relevant teachings.

### 17. Practice: wisdom, ethics, and cultivation of the mind

Buddhist liberation cannot be reduced to agreeing with “everything is empty” or “there is no unchanging self.” Practice encompasses habits of attention, intentions, speech, and actions.

The Noble Eightfold Path is usually organised into three groups. Prajna, wisdom: right view and intention. Shila, ethical discipline: speech, action, and livelihood. Samadhi, cultivation of concentration and mind: effort, mindfulness, and concentration.

Practice includes calming (shamatha) and insight (vipassana). But the whole Buddhist path cannot be confined to a single method of observation, the absence of thought, or a particular favourable experience. Meditation exists in connection with ethical conduct, right understanding, and the cessation of craving.

For some Yogacara traditions, transformation of the basis (ashraya-paravritti) becomes an important concept. It concerns a profound change in afflicted cognitive processes, not simply the repetition of an intellectual thesis.

When Buddha-nature is described as already present, the account of practice becomes complex: why is awakening necessary if the original nature is already pure? Different traditions offer different answers, emphasising the removal of defilements, the disclosure of what is present, or the transformation of conditioned processes.

We should not artificially settle this difficulty on their behalf.

### 18. What differs between traditions

Buddhist history cannot be reduced to the development of one unchanging ontological thesis. A few enduring strands are enough to make this clear.

Early teachings investigate dukkha, the five aggregates, not-self, dependent origination, karma, the path, and nibbana.

Theravada preserves the Pali Canon and develops its own Abhidhamma and commentarial tradition. It cannot be fully identified with historically reconstructed early Buddhism.

Abhidharma schools develop detailed classifications of dharmas, their conditionality, and their status. They disagree on fundamental matters.

Madhyamaka investigates the absence of independent intrinsic existence in phenomena and connects emptiness with dependent origination.

Yogacara develops a philosophy of consciousness, karmic continuity, cognitive representations, and subject–object duality.

Buddha-nature teachings use different ways of speaking about the possibility of awakening, original purity, and, in some texts, a positively understood ultimate nature.

Vajrayana develops distinctive tantric methods within a Buddhist religious and philosophical context, especially in Tibetan traditions. It should not be treated as one separate ontology opposed to Mahayana as a whole.

Zen belongs to Buddhist history, but its distinctive account of awakening requires a separate study and cannot be deduced from one brief formula about emptiness. Daoism is a distinct Chinese tradition, not a Buddhist school; its interaction with Buddhism also falls outside this overview.

### 19. Questions Buddhist philosophy leaves open

As the overview draws to a close, several questions can be identified that have substantive standing within different Buddhist traditions.

The status of dharmas. If a person is not an independent self, do the factors under analysis exist ultimately? Different Abhidharma systems give different answers.

Emptiness and knowledge. How is the absence of intrinsic existence known if the means of knowledge also lack an independent intrinsic nature? How are conventional and ultimate truth related?

Consciousness and continuity. How does conditioned consciousness differ from an unchanging self? Is alaya-vijnana sufficient to explain karma without a concealed Atman?

Nirvana. What exactly does being unconditioned mean in the early tradition, and how can this be reconciled with Madhyamaka’s critique of independent existence?

Buddha-nature. Does tathagatagarbha indicate the possibility of awakening, provide a way of speaking about emptiness, or name a positively understood ultimate nature? Historical texts do not give a single answer.

Practice and original presence. If awakening is described as the disclosure of what is already present, what exactly changes as a result of the path? And how are wisdom, ethics, compassion, and the transformation of consciousness related?

These questions cannot be regarded as proven defects in the teachings merely because schools disagree. But neither can they be declared resolved by one general statement about nonduality.

For an independent philosophical study, it is enough to establish their content and the limits of the available answers honestly.

### Conclusion. What Buddhist inquiry reveals

Buddhism investigates conditioned existence, ignorance, craving, clinging, and the possibility of their cessation. It considers a person through changing bodily and mental processes rather than as an unchanging independent soul, while preserving the significance of human actions, responsibility, and help.

Early texts disclose not-self together with dependent origination. Abhidharma refines the analysis of the factors of experience. Madhyamaka investigates the absence of independent intrinsic nature even in what seems to be an ultimate foundation. Yogacara develops complex explanations of consciousness, representation, and continuity. Buddha-nature teachings introduce further ways of speaking about awakening, original purity, and ultimate reality.

Across these traditions, the practical task of ending dukkha remains important, although the philosophical ways of explaining it can differ substantially.

It would be a mistake to reduce all Buddhism to a denial of the world’s existence, an affirmation of a single consciousness, psychological calm, or a simple call to stop desiring. It would be equally mistaken to smooth over historical disagreements as though there were no substantive differences between early discourses, Nagarjuna, Yogacara, and Buddha-nature teachings.

Buddhist philosophy is not confined to “What exists?” It examines how bondage arises, how understanding and action sustain it, and how its conditions can cease.

This concludes our independent account. It does not settle the questions of nirvana, consciousness, self, and emptiness for every school, but makes them sufficiently clear for a subsequent, separate comparison with another philosophical position.

## Part II. Buddhism and monophenomenalism: unity, distinction, and liberation

An investigation of Buddhist philosophy from the independent position of monophenomenalism.

### 1. A different starting point

Buddhist philosophy considers dependent origination, the absence of a permanent self, ignorance, suffering, and liberation.

Its schools explain these questions differently. Early texts investigate the five aggregates and the cessation of dukkha; Madhyamaka develops the teaching of emptiness; Yogacara examines consciousness and what is perceived; Buddha-nature teachings offer further ways of understanding awakening.

Monophenomenalism does not arise from these teachings and does not require their claims as a prior foundation.

It adopts an independent philosophical position:

Everything that exists is a phenomenon. The phenomenon is one.

Unity here does not mean that everything distinguished is the same. People, objects, states, and relationships retain their determinacy.

Yet differences within one phenomenon do not become independent first principles of existence simply by being distinguished.

This is monophenomenalism’s positive claim. It does not follow automatically from Buddhist not-self or emptiness.

Accepting one phenomenon is likewise not presented as a necessary deductive consequence of the fact that any inquiry available to us takes place in experiencing.

Monophenomenalism is an independent starting position, not a proof that every other ontology is untenable.

### 2. The five theses of the Model of the Phenomenon

The Model of the Phenomenon is one possible tool of inquiry within monophenomenalism. Accepting the position does not require accepting this particular tool. Its five theses are:

1. Everything is one phenomenon.
2. The phenomenon is sensory experiencing.
3. A distinction within sensory experiencing is a concept.
4. Concepts structure the phenomenon into experience.
5. Experience is the phenomenon structured through concepts.

In the model, sensory experiencing includes sensations, emotions, thoughts, and states—everything that is experienced.

Distinction is the way sensory experiencing is. A concept is a system of distinctions.

These theses do not describe stages in which experience is produced from initially unstructured experiencing.

Concepts do not function as an independent productive mechanism.

Nor do the words “phenomenon,” “experiencing,” “distinction,” “concept,” and “experience” designate five independently existing entities.

The model investigates the determinacy of what exists.

It is itself a concept and does not establish its own truth beyond the philosophical position adopted.

We can now consider how questions raised by Buddhism appear within this position.

### 3. Anatta: what does the absence of self mean?

Early Buddhism examines the body, feelings, recognition, formations, and consciousness—the five aggregates of human existence.

None is accepted as an independent, unchanging self.

Ordinary human existence is not thereby declared nonexistent.

The Model of the Phenomenon allows the question to be considered from a different angle.

“I,” body, consciousness, memory, feelings, and actions are distinguished within one phenomenon.

Their being distinguished does not require the additional establishment of an independent owner to whom they must all belong.

For example, the statement “I feel pain” distinguishes a person, pain, and the relationship of experiencing.

This is a meaningful statement.

But by itself it does not prove the existence of a separate ontological subject that first exists independently of experiencing and then receives pain as its property.

A significant parallel with anatta appears here: a critique of an independent owner need not deny the person or what that person experiences.

Yet the Buddhist teaching of not-self and the consideration of “I” as a distinction are not identical.

Buddhism supports anatta through its own analysis of the aggregates, impermanence, unsatisfactoriness, and the impossibility of complete control over them. These claims are connected with the cessation of clinging.

The model begins with one phenomenon and considers the determinacy of “I” within it.

It need not first investigate the impermanence of each aggregate in order to consider “I” without an independent owner.

A similarity in outcome does not therefore establish an identity of philosophical foundations.

### 4. The five aggregates and conceptual distinction

The Buddhist teaching of the five aggregates offers a particular classification of human existence.

The Model of the Phenomenon allows each aggregate to be investigated as something distinctly articulated within experiencing.

But it would be incorrect to claim that the five aggregates and the model’s concepts are the same thing.

Vedana has a specific Buddhist meaning: the pleasant, unpleasant, or neutral tone of feeling.

Sankhara encompasses particular groups of formative factors.

Vijnana designates consciousness within the relevant system of Buddhist analysis.

A concept in the model has a different definition: a system of distinctions.

It is not another Buddhist aggregate, nor does it replace all five.

The model allows us to examine how feelings, perception, consciousness, and their relationships are distinguished without turning an accepted Buddhist classification into the necessary structure of the phenomenon itself.

A classification is a meaningful way of investigating. But the fact that what is classified is distinguished does not oblige us to regard that classification as the ultimate structure of everything that exists.

This is not a criticism of Buddhist analysis. Within Buddhist practice, its purpose is independent and definite.

### 5. Dependent origination and causation

In Buddhism, dependent origination is a central principle for explaining samsara and the cessation of dukkha.

Ignorance, formative activities, consciousness, contact, feeling, craving, and clinging are considered through relationships of conditionality.

The Model of the Phenomenon need not deny such relationships.

In the model, causation, time, sequence, condition, and result are also distinguished within experience.

We can investigate how pain is related to bodily injury, how intention is related to action, and how particular circumstances are related to subsequent consequences.

Acknowledging causal relationships does not by itself require causation to be conceived as an external force producing the phenomenon. Buddhism need not accept that premise either: SN 12.20 describes conditionality as a regularity that the Buddha discerns rather than creates.

The difference between the teachings should therefore not be drawn as an opposition between an “external causal force” and its absence.

Buddhism uses dependent origination as a crucial explanation of bondage and the possibility of liberation.

The Model of the Phenomenon considers causal relationships as relationships articulated through conceptual distinctions within experience. Its five theses, however, do not entail the specifically Buddhist account of conditionality connecting ignorance, craving, clinging, and the cessation of dukkha. The difference concerns the content, justification, and investigative purpose of these relationships.

This does not establish that Buddhist causation is mistaken.

Nor can the model be said to automatically confirm Buddhist claims about karma and rebirth.

It can be used to consider how action, consequence, birth, death, and continuity are distinguished. But that consideration alone does not establish the truth of all the Buddhist tradition’s religious and metaphysical claims.

To distinguish a causal relationship is neither to refute it nor to prove every philosophy that relies on it.

### 6. Emptiness: the closest parallel and the greatest risk of conflation

Nagarjuna’s Madhyamaka provides a particularly interesting point of comparison.

It investigates the emptiness of phenomena with respect to svabhava—independent intrinsic existence.

Nagarjuna shows that dependent origination is incompatible with regarding things as entirely self-established entities.

Emptiness does not thereby become a new independent substance.

Monophenomenalism likewise does not begin with a plurality of independently established first principles.

But a fundamental boundary must be drawn here.

The absence of independent intrinsic existence is not equivalent to the positive claim that everything that exists is one phenomenon.

Madhyamaka need not accept such unity.

Indeed, its philosophical analysis can be directed against any attempt to turn unity into an independent entity endowed with its own absolute nature.

Monophenomenalism does not claim that the phenomenon is such an entity.

Unity does not mean one enormous object, a single substance, or an independent foundation containing all things.

There can therefore be a substantive affinity between the critiques of independent first principles in the two philosophies.

But the difference remains:

Madhyamaka investigates the absence of intrinsic existence and its liberating significance.

Monophenomenalism accepts everything that exists as one phenomenon.

Whether accepting one phenomenon follows from the absence of intrinsic existence cannot be decided by simply identifying these expressions.

Such an inference would require a further argument.

Monophenomenalism does not need that inference, since its starting position is adopted independently.

A Madhyamaka thinker is nevertheless entitled to ask a question in return: what exactly does the positive assertion “one” add, and what is its philosophical status if it introduces no intrinsic nature? Monophenomenalism’s answer is that unity is adopted as a starting philosophical position, not as a deductive result of emptiness. That answer clarifies the claim’s status, but does not automatically remove a possible Madhyamaka objection.

#### The emptiness of emptiness

There is another interesting parallel here.

Madhyamaka does not permit emptiness to be turned into a final independent thing.

The model does not permit the phenomenon to be turned into a separate object to which the properties of the distinctions under investigation can be transferred.

But these restrictions are not identical.

Nagarjuna’s emptiness is disclosed through the critique of svabhava.

The model does not turn “phenomenon” into the name of an additional bearer, substance, or separate object possessing its own properties. This restriction is connected with its starting definitions, not with a claim that the phenomenon does not exist.

It would therefore be incorrect to say that “phenomenon” means Buddhist “emptiness.”

Such renaming would erase the substantive content of each philosophy.

### 7. Yogacara: consciousness and the division between perceiver and perceived

Yogacara investigates the arising of consciousness, the object-directed character of experience, and the idea of independently existing subject and object in particular detail.

Here we encounter a question close to the model’s concerns: does distinguishing observer from observed really establish two independent foundations?

In the model, the observer, consciousness, what is perceived, and the relationship of perception are distinguished within one phenomenon.

The existence of experiencing does not require a separate subject that must first receive it.

But this does not mean that the model asserts the primacy of mind.

In a number of systems, Yogacara explains cognitive experience through karmic seeds, alaya-vijnana, and the dependence of what is perceived on processes of consciousness.

The model offers no such causal theory.

It does not claim that consciousness produces objects or that the experienced world arises from store consciousness.

In the model, consciousness is a distinction, not an independent producer of the phenomenon.

Yogacara also considers three natures: imagined, dependent, and perfected.

This creates an opportunity to compare different ways of determining perceiver and perceived.

But Yogacara’s three natures are not three levels of the Model of the Phenomenon.

The difference is especially clear in the later Buddhist epistemology of Dignaga and Dharmakirti. There, perception (pratyaksha) is regarded as nonconceptual, whereas inference (anumana) is conceptual cognition involving general features and linguistic mediation. This is a specific epistemological contrast, not the same as a contrast between meaningful and contentless experiencing. Nor should it be attributed to every strand of Yogacara without qualification.

In the Model of the Phenomenon, a concept is a system of distinctions, not necessarily a word, judgement, or mistaken construction. What a Buddhist author calls nonconceptual perception therefore need not lack distinctions in the model’s sense. The model can consider such perception through concepts in its own special meaning, but that would be an independent reading, not proof that the Buddhist author meant the same thing.

It remains to investigate the conditions under which the two descriptions are compatible, and whether shared terminology conceals a real difference in their philosophical tasks.

### 8. Ignorance and the concept

One of the most important boundaries lies between Buddhist ignorance and the model’s concept.

In Buddhism, avidya is connected with a failure to understand the actual character of existence and sustains clinging.

In the model, a concept designates a system of distinctions.

A concept does not become a mistake simply because it is a concept.

Otherwise, pain, joy, a person, memory, a question, and every meaningful distinction would have to be declared mistaken.

The model does not claim this.

Nor does it regard the cessation of concepts as a necessary goal.

Conversely, Buddhist liberation from ignorance is not simply the destruction of thought.

In Buddhism, right understanding, ethical discernment, and practice themselves require meaningful actions.

Both positions can therefore be presented without the false opposition “concepts versus no concepts.”

The real question lies elsewhere.

Buddhism investigates why a particular understanding of existence is ignorance and how overcoming it is related to liberation.

The model allows us to investigate how knowledge, error, understanding, and ignorance are distinguished without making the necessity of Buddhist liberating knowledge a starting premise.

### 9. Dukkha: conceptuality does not cancel suffering

If the model considers suffering through concepts, it does not follow that suffering is invented.

Pain remains a particular form of experiencing.

Loss differs from joy.

Danger, illness, care, and the consequences of actions retain their meaningful content.

Calling suffering conceptually determined does not amount to discovering a way to end it automatically.

This is especially important in a comparison with Buddhism.

Buddhist inquiry distinguishes unpleasant feeling from the additional processes of craving, aversion, and clinging.

The model allows these differences to be considered within experience.

The model’s five theses do not by themselves establish the Buddhist explanation of samsaric dukkha through ignorance and craving. Nor do they imply that removing a particular concept necessarily ends pain, or that the absence of an independent owner of experiencing guarantees calm.

Buddhist teaching itself distinguishes bodily pain from additional mental suffering sustained by resistance and clinging, as in the discourse on the two arrows (SN 36.6).

All such conclusions would require separate grounds.

The model discloses the determinacy of suffering, but does not promise to remove suffering through its application.

Here the Buddhist path retains its independence: it offers a specific teaching about the causes of dukkha and practices for bringing them to an end.

### 10. Nirvana, Buddha-nature, and absolute freedom

Buddhism gives liberation central importance.

In the early tradition, nibbana is connected with the cessation of craving, hatred, ignorance, and further samsaric bondage.

Madhyamaka investigates the absence of independent intrinsic existence even in nirvana.

In some Mahayana traditions, Buddha-nature teachings use positive expressions concerning original purity and the possibility of awakening.

These claims cannot simply be combined into a single explanation.

Let us now consider the question through the model. “Absolute freedom” here belongs to its vocabulary and application; it is not an additional foundational thesis or a promise of a particular state.

Bondage, obstacle, freedom, liberation, attainment, and nonattainment are distinguished within one phenomenon.

But absolute freedom is not established as an independent property of the phenomenon or as a special state one must possess.

The model does not require discovering a true self, attaining nirvana, or revealing Buddha-nature.

This does not deny the corresponding Buddhist teachings.

Nor does it claim that Buddhist practice is useless.

A person can practise, learn, meditate, care for others, or seek liberation.

All these relationships have content of their own.

The difference is that Buddhist teaching organises a path around the cessation of dukkha, whereas accepting monophenomenalism and applying the model presuppose neither mandatory entry onto such a path nor a predetermined outcome of inquiry. This does not imply that Buddhism claims the world exists to fulfil a cosmic purpose.

The Buddhist path treats liberation as its central task.

Monophenomenalism does not turn acceptance of one phenomenon into a task of attaining a different state.

Nirvana therefore cannot be identified with absolute freedom as considered through the model.

A meaningful parallel is possible in their reconsideration of bondage, but their philosophical foundations and significance remain different.

### 11. Ethics and compassion

The absence of an independent self in Buddhism does not make another being’s suffering insignificant.

Especially in Mahayana, wisdom is connected with compassion and the bodhisattva ideal.

Monophenomenalism likewise does not erase differences between people.

Help and harm, care and indifference, one person’s pain and another’s report of it are distinguished within one phenomenon.

But not every ethical rule can be derived automatically from the assertion of unity.

Compassion does not follow from the first thesis as a formal theorem.

Nor does the model replace the Buddhist ethical path with a compulsory programme of behaviour of its own.

Nevertheless, adopting it can change one’s relationship to the familiar opposition between “I” and “others.”

Care and responsibility may then be experienced without having to turn a person into an independent owner of experience.

This is a possible practical significance of the position, not a necessary psychological or ethical consequence.

### 12. The main result of the comparison

We can now see both the similarities and the differences between the philosophical starting points.

Buddhism investigates the conditionality of existence, the absence of a permanent self, ignorance, suffering, and liberation.

Madhyamaka develops a critical investigation of independent intrinsic existence.

Yogacara offers its own explanations of consciousness and what is perceived.

Monophenomenalism begins with a positive assertion of the unity of the phenomenon.

The Model of the Phenomenon investigates the determinacy of this phenomenon through sensory experiencing and concepts.

Both philosophical approaches allow us to reconsider the idea of an independently existing owner of experiencing.

Their identity does not follow from this.

Buddhist emptiness does not mean one phenomenon.

Buddhist avidya does not mean a concept.

Yogacara consciousness does not mean the model’s sensory experiencing.

Nirvana does not mean absolute freedom as considered in the model.

Conversely, the model’s five theses are not new formulations of the Four Noble Truths.

The most meaningful parallel lies in the possibility of investigating familiar ideas about the person, the world, and the independence of what exists without treating those ideas as unquestionable foundations.

But the ways of investigating differ.

Buddhism develops its questions in connection with dependent origination and liberation.

Monophenomenalism considers everything as one phenomenon and investigates its distinctions without establishing liberation as a compulsory goal.

It is precisely this difference that prevents one philosophy from dissolving into the other.

We can understand Buddhist questions, then consider them through an independent philosophical position and see where correspondences emerge, where differences remain, and where new questions arise.

Such a comparison requires neither proving monophenomenalism’s superiority nor turning Buddhism into a preliminary stage of its development.

It allows the independent content of both approaches to become clearer.
