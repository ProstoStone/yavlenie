Weightlessness
When meaning, freedom, and the self no longer have to be held on to: a philosophical essay on the phenomenon without ultimate justification.
Essay
2026-11-04

For a long time, someone felt as though they were falling.

Not necessarily every day. Life could be full of things to do: work, love, friends, conversations, plans. Yet behind all of it there remained an indistinct feeling that if they stopped holding on to something, the fall would become final.

They could hold on to success. To convictions. To an image of themselves as a worthy person. To the hope of someday understanding what it had all been for. Even to the thought that none of it had any meaning.

The handholds changed, but the need for a handhold remained.

And one day a thought appears that is difficult to put into words:

**What if nothing has to be held on to?**

Not because everything has finally become secure.

Not because the strongest possible foundation has been found.

Not because the fall has been conquered through extraordinary spiritual effort.

But because the very picture of falling may depend on the assumption that existence must be held up by something.

We will call this possibility weightlessness.

Not a physical condition. Not a promise of serenity. Not a new stage of human development.

Simply an attempt to consider life without a mandatory ultimate support.

## From falling to weightlessness

Falling has a particular logic.

To fall is to move downward relative to something. To fear a final fall is to imagine somewhere it will end, and what may happen there.

In ordinary life, falling is perfectly real: someone may stumble, be injured, or fall from a height. The metaphor does not abolish gravity.

But when someone says “my life is falling,” the situation is different.

They may experience anxiety, the loss of direction, or the absence of a clear goal. All of this can be genuinely difficult.

Yet another distinction is added to these experiences: life is supposed to arrive somewhere, and if it does not, it must be sinking lower and lower.

A scale appears.

There is a worthy life and an unworthy one. A life lived properly and one lived in vain. A meaningful life and a meaningless one.

Somewhere along this scale, there supposedly lies a point beyond which existence can no longer be justified.

The person struggles not to fall there.

But the scale itself can be examined.

What makes a goal a condition of life as a whole? Why must the loss of direction count as movement downward? Who established the direction relative to which failure to achieve something becomes a fall?

Sometimes the answers are important and concrete. If someone cannot pay their rent, they really do need to deal with a financial problem. If a relationship ends, the pain does not disappear when philosophy gives it another name.

Yet a particular difficulty does not entail the metaphysical defeat of existence.

**Weightlessness begins as a question about the need for the vertical axis itself—not as a command to stop feeling heavy.**

## Not a final victory over Nietzsche

In *After the Übermensch*, we explored a possible continuation of a question Nietzsche raised with exceptional force.

If inherited higher values have lost their unconditional authority, human beings may create new ones, overcome themselves, affirm life, and answer existence with their own great Yes.

Nietzsche cannot be reduced to a demand for heroic achievement. His image of the child, who plays and begins anew, already calls into question the heavy obligations of conventional morality. Nor does he need a simplistic notion of a separate free will that governs actions without causes.

Still, a question remains—one directed not so much against Nietzsche as beyond him.

**Why should existence require even affirmation?**

If someone loves life, creates, makes an effort, or delights in difficulty, all of this is possible.

But what if they do not wish to turn their life into a project of self-overcoming?

What if they offer it no grand Yes?

What if today they create no new values, surpass no earlier version of themselves, and make no artwork out of what they have lived?

Must they then regard themselves as having failed at existence itself?

Weightlessness does not deprive anyone of the possibility of growth.

It questions the need to treat growth as the ultimate justification of a person.

One may cross the bridge.

One may remain on the shore.

Philosophy need not turn either image into a law governing the whole world.

## Not consolation after nihilism

In *Nihilism without a Verdict*, we took a different step.

The absence of ultimate meaning does not automatically mean that existence is deficient.

To regard an absence as a deficiency, we must first adopt a requirement: meaning has to be there.

That requirement can survive even after the belief in meaning itself has fallen away.

Someone says, “There is no ultimate purpose.”

And then, “So everything is futile.”

Yet “futile” already presupposes a criterion for how everything should have turned out.

The point is not to prove the opposite—that everything must be wonderful, saturated with meaning, or moving toward a happy ending.

That would simply replace one verdict with another.

Weightlessness is not an optimistic form of nihilism.

It does not say, “There is no meaning, and that is marvelous.”

For that would oblige people to be happy about the absence of meaning. And if they were not happy, they would once again be doing something wrong.

**Weightlessness is not a positive evaluation of emptiness. It is the possibility of treating neither the presence nor the absence of meaning as a final evaluation of the phenomenon.**

We may find meaning.

We may lose it.

We may suffer because of the loss.

But none of these conditions must become a verdict on everything that happens.

## Five theses and the absence of a second holder

We should now be precise about the philosophical ground on which this discussion proceeds.

*Monophenomenalism* proposes a starting position: everything that exists is considered one phenomenon.

*Model of the Phenomenon* develops this through five theses:

1. Everything is one phenomenon.
2. The phenomenon is sensory experiencing.
3. A distinction within sensory experiencing is a concept.
4. Concepts structure the phenomenon into experience.
5. Experience is the phenomenon structured through concepts.

They do not describe a sequence in which someone first receives the phenomenon, then distinguishes it, and finally produces experience.

Experiencing, distinction, concept, and experience are not several independently manufactured worlds. They are ways of considering what the model takes as one phenomenon.

Another point matters here.

If the “I” is considered a distinction within the phenomenon, we need not begin by positing a separate fundamental owner standing behind the body, memory, thoughts, and fears.

But it does not follow that human beings disappear.

A person may fear, love, plan, wish to stay alive, or seek help. Their experience is not declared nonexistent.

We merely decline, without further grounds, to posit another independent holder behind all this—a holder who must possess and maintain everything that happens as their property.

The possibility of weightlessness arises where two questions meet.

Must the phenomenon have an external foundation in order to be?

And must the distinguished “I” discover an ultimate foundation so that existence will not have been futile?

Accepting the five theses does not turn negative answers into scientifically demonstrated truths. But it allows us to inquire consistently into **why we thought these requirements were necessary in the first place**.

## Who has to hold the self together?

In everyday speech, it is natural to say, “Pull yourself together,” “Keep a grip on yourself,” or “Don't lose yourself.”

Sometimes this is useful advice. In a crisis, someone may need to concentrate, accept help, and take concrete action.

But understood literally as a philosophical picture, these expressions create a strange construction.

Here is the self that must be held together.

And here is another self that must do the holding.

If the second one can also become lost, a third will be required.

Where does the sequence end?

Within experience, we can distinguish anxiety, the wish for control, an image of oneself, and actions that help us deal with circumstances.

But there is no need to assume in advance a separate invisible owner to whom all this belongs like objects held in someone's hands.

The question “How do I hold myself together?” can therefore change shape.

We do not necessarily need to look for a better holder.

We can investigate how the distinction emerged between the self that needs holding and the one supposed to hold it.

This does not make psychological distress an illusion.

It does not prohibit looking after oneself.

But it shows that **the need to continuously confirm one's existence through a separate act of holding it together is not self-evident**.

## Even freedom need not be held on to

When familiar supports lose their unconditional force, a wonderful feeling of freedom may arise.

A person stops believing they must become someone great. They no longer search for an ultimate mission. They no longer feel compelled to prove that their life deserves to be lived.

But almost immediately another danger appears.

They want to preserve this feeling.

Remember it.

Make sure it never disappears.

If I was free yesterday but feel anxious again today, have I lost my freedom?

Freedom becomes an object that can be dropped.

Now it must be protected from anxiety, desire, habit, and ordinary human life.

In *Model of the Phenomenon*, freedom is distinguished in relation to a constraint. Absolute freedom is not introduced as a special metaphysical property of the phenomenon that someone might acquire.

So freedom from a mandatory goal does not create a new duty **always to feel free**.

One may experience anxiety.

One may start making plans again.

One may want recognition or fear losing someone.

This does not prove that freedom, conceived as a possession, has been lost.

Perhaps no object called “my final freedom” ever had to be held on to in the first place.

And we need not hold on to that thought either.

## There is no need even to stop searching

It may seem that a command now follows: stop searching.

Stop wanting.

Stop hoping.

Give up your goals, and you will see how simple everything is.

But such a command immediately restores the structure it promises to destroy.

Now a person has a special goal: to be without goals.

A special task: to do nothing for the sake of a task.

A special ideal: to have no ideals.

And every new interest begins to look like a threat to the state supposedly attained.

Weightlessness, therefore, is not the opposite of searching.

If someone is curious about philosophy, they can explore it.

If they want to write a book, they can write one.

If they want to earn money, grow a garden, care for loved ones, or travel, all these things remain possible and meaningful within particular experience.

The absence of a mandatory ultimate purpose does not turn particular purposes into mistakes.

This is not about refusing every practical support. We use a chair to sit, a railing to avoid falling, and agreements to live together.

The point is that **none of these supports needs to become the final justification of the phenomenon itself**.

One may hold on to the railing.

One need not hold on to the idea that without it existence itself loses its right to be.

## What if things become difficult again?

This is a powerful objection to all talk of freedom.

Suppose someone reads a philosophical essay and experiences extraordinary lightness, then encounters bereavement, illness, humiliation, or fear the next day.

Where has their weightlessness gone?

If we answer, “You haven't understood deeply enough,” we have produced another system of spiritual evaluation.

If we say, “A truly free person never feels burdened,” we dismiss genuine human suffering.

If we promise that correct thinking will abolish pain, we make a false promise.

But weightlessness as a philosophical possibility is not a permanent pleasant emotion.

It does not demand uninterrupted ease.

Experiencing may include heaviness, fear, resistance, and pain. In particular circumstances, people need care, treatment, support, time, and sometimes protection from danger.

None of this is refuted by the sentence “Everything is one phenomenon.”

And none of it establishes that the phenomenon as a whole has some deficiency which a person must repair by attaining a higher form of themselves.

One may once again feel that one is falling, and that feeling will be what is actually experienced.

**Weightlessness does not require the imagined fall to stop. It allows the image of falling not to be taken as the final description of existence.**

## The tree beneath which nothing has to be done

Imagine someone who comes to a tree.

They may sit beneath it because they are tired.

Because the weather is warm.

Because they feel like sitting.

Or without any particular reason they would care to name.

They hear leaves moving. See light between the branches. Remember unfinished tasks.

But they do not have to focus on their breath.

They do not have to experience unity with the world.

They do not have to understand that the tree does not exist as an independent object.

They do not have to feel gratitude, joy, beauty, or peace.

If they want to stand up, they can.

If they want to stay, they can.

If a thought about life's meaning arrives, they can think about it.

If no thought arrives, they need not interpret that as a spiritual achievement.

Sitting beneath a tree does not prove monophenomenalism. It is simply one possible human experience.

Yet something can be recognized in the scene that a philosophical formula struggles to express.

Once, an ordinary moment seemed obliged to become something else: an experience that would transform a person, a memory that would last, a step toward a goal, a confirmation that the time spent living had been worthwhile.

Now the moment need not be burdened with that demand.

It does not have to justify its existence.

And the person is not required to extract even pleasure from it.

## Not hiding from life behind philosophy

Someone may object that weightlessness is merely a convenient name for withdrawing from life.

A person is afraid to choose, love, or act, and finds an argument that allows withdrawal to be called freedom.

That certainly can happen.

Any philosophy can be used to excuse inaction. Monophenomenalism has no immunity to this criticism.

But the possibility of living without a mandatory ultimate purpose is not equivalent to refusing particular actions.

If someone matters to you, caring for them does not need cosmic justification.

If work matters, you can work.

If you need to protect yourself, protecting yourself may be the sensible thing to do.

If you do not want to act, that desire can also be examined—without a duty to turn it immediately into a virtue.

Weightlessness does not say that actions have no consequences.

It does not say that how we treat others makes no difference.

It does not erase distinctions between love and violence, help and indifference.

It simply does not turn a particular human preference into the final measure of the entire phenomenon.

**Not needing a justification for life is not the same as not needing medicine, friends, work, or care.**

These are different questions.

## Not a new nature of the phenomenon

The metaphor of weightlessness can lead to another mistake.

We may be tempted to say: the phenomenon itself is free, weightless, infinite, invulnerable, or beyond time.

But in doing so, we create another hidden object endowed with beautiful properties.

The Working Map accompanying *Model of the Phenomenon* explicitly warns against this substitution.

Not ascribing a property to the phenomenon is not the same as establishing the opposite property.

Not introducing a temporal axis as an independent foundation does not prove that the phenomenon is timeless by nature.

Not introducing a separate owner does not mean declaring that the phenomenon metaphysically “has no owner” as though this were a new intrinsic characteristic.

And weightlessness is not a new entity or a permanent attribute of the world.

It is a metaphor for a way of not imposing mandatory conditions on the phenomenon that we previously regarded as unavoidable.

The metaphor can help make a question visible.

It must not turn into another assertion about the secret nature of everything that exists.

## But don't we need at least one certainty?

At this point, someone may say:

All right. No ultimate goal is required. No final affirmation is required. No separate fundamental owner is required.

But surely we at least need to be certain that all this is true?

Can one find peace without having definitively proven monophenomenalism to oneself?

Here philosophy encounters its own limits once more.

The starting position of monophenomenalism is not a scientifically established law and is not proven by the mere fact that experiencing is present.

The model's definitions allow certain connections to be examined, but consistency among those definitions does not make every proposed interpretation an obligatory truth.

We can find an error in an argument.

We can propose another position.

We can remain unconvinced by the five theses.

If weightlessness depends upon complete certainty that one philosophy is correct, it becomes yet another possession that must be protected from doubt.

But the possibility of not turning every distinction into a final verdict does not require all philosophical disputes to be declared settled.

In this sense, even the model itself need not be clutched like a lifebuoy.

It can be investigated, applied, challenged, and refined.

Philosophy does not have to become the last thing a person grabs on to.

## Holding on to nothing

At first, someone tried to become strong enough to hold the world together.

Then wise enough to hold themselves together.

Then free enough never to hold on to anything again.

But even the last formula can become a new assignment.

For “I must hold on to nothing” is still the necessity of maintaining a particular image of oneself.

So the expression “holding on to nothing” is not meant as a command.

It cannot be made into an exercise with a compulsory outcome.

One can hold on.

One can let go.

One can try again to hold what is slipping away.

All these distinctions are possible within experience.

But none has to become a condition without which the phenomenon itself would be deficient.

Weightlessness, then, is not a reward for finally letting go.

Nor is it the emptiness left after everything dear has disappeared.

It is the absence of a need to turn life into a continuous test of whether one has managed to justify it.

**There is no need to stop holding on. Nor is there a need to maintain the absence of support.**

And one day even this sentence may be forgotten.

## Where there is no longer an examination

In the evening, someone may write another page of a philosophical essay.

They may close their laptop.

They may meet a friend.

They may sit beneath a tree and simply sit.

Not because they have finally achieved ultimate freedom.

Not because they know a secret hidden from others.

Not because every action now possesses unconditional value.

They may enjoy writing.

They may enjoy sitting.

Or they may feel bored, anxious, cold—and then leave.

None of this has to prove that life is being lived correctly.

In *After the Übermensch*, we asked why life should have to pass even the highest test of affirmation.

In *Nihilism without a Verdict*, we asked why the absence of ultimate meaning should count as a deficiency.

Here the question grows quieter.

If existence needs no final evaluation, what exactly must we hold on to so that it can continue to be what it is?

There is no need to answer.

One may search for an answer if one wishes.

One may stop searching.

There is no hidden destination we must reach before everything finally becomes the phenomenon.

There is no separate observer obliged to step outside experiencing and certify its existence.

There is no requirement to become a person who needs nothing ever again.

People, trees, thoughts, tasks, losses, meetings, and the differences among them remain.

And so does the possibility of not turning every one of them into an examination of existence as a whole.

**Many things. Many distinctions. One phenomenon.**

The words may remain.

They may be forgotten.

There is no need even to feel weightless.

## Foundations and limits of this essay

*Weightlessness* is an image and a philosophical exploration, not a new formulation of the five original theses, a description of a proven property of the phenomenon, or a technique for attaining a psychological state.

The inquiry draws on *Monophenomenalism* and *Model of the Phenomenon*, particularly their treatments of unity, distinction, concepts, the “I,” purpose, freedom, and liberation. The cautions against assigning either positive or negative metaphysical properties to the phenomenon are consistent with the *Working Map*. Links to the original works appear below the article.

The essay continues [*After the Übermensch*](/yavlenie/en/after-ubermensch/) and [*Nihilism without a Verdict*](/yavlenie/en/nihilism-without-verdict/), but does not require agreement with them. The claim that ultimate evaluation is not mandatory is a philosophical move within the proposed approach; it is not an automatic logical consequence of the single thesis of the phenomenon's unity.
