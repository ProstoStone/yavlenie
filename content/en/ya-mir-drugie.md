I, World, Others: The Problem That Never Was
Hume, Kant, other minds, and monophenomenalism: how a distinction comes to be treated as an independent foundation.
Author's essay
2026-11-06

## Where Is All of This?

There is a person.

There is a world.

There are other people.

Each person has consciousness. Experiences arise in consciousness. Experiences belong to the person, and the person is in the world.

Nothing unusual seems to have been said.

But let us look more closely.

So far, these are distinctions: person, world, consciousness, experiencing, other people. None of them yet requires an independent original foundation.

But a familiar explanation often takes another step without noticing it: what has been distinguished is treated not merely as content of experience, but as an independent foundation of existence.

Within such an explanation, a person becomes a separate owner of experiencing, the world an independently existing reality beyond experiencing, and other people owners of other, independently existing experiences.

**This is no longer merely a distinction. It is an additional assumption about the independence of what has been distinguished.**

Only then does the demand arise to explain how these foundations are related.

How does a person perceive a world if that world exists independently? How does consciousness know what lies beyond it? Why do experiences belong to one person rather than another? How can we establish the existence of other minds? What unites distinct perceptions into a single experience? Why is there something rather than nothing?

These are enormous questions. Philosophical systems and centuries of argument have grown from them, along with ideas about what a human being can know and must be.

But why did we begin in this way?

**Where is all of this?**

Not the location of a body, a house, or a tree. Where are the independent foundations of existence that we have already assumed?

Where is the observer, existing separately from experiencing, to whom experiencing must be presented?

Where is the independent “outside” whose existence was assumed in opposition to the “inside”?

Where are the other experiences, established as separate from what is?

We can point to a person, a tree, a thought about another mind, a memory. All these are distinguished. But where has it been shown that every such distinction represents an independent foundation of existence?

**We accepted division—and now we are trying to explain what we divided.**

Before seeking connections between supposedly separate foundations, perhaps we should examine where their assumed independence came from.

## The Abyss We Learned Not to Notice

A person looks at the world.

Many explanations start here. Yet this short sentence already presupposes two sides: the person here, the world there, with perception between them.

Another division follows. One person has an inner experience; other people have their own. One person cannot directly verify everything another person experiences, so the problem of other minds arises.

Then another difficulty appears: if perceptions are distinct, what makes them perceptions of the same person? What unites them in consciousness? What preserves personal identity through change?

Now the connections need to be explained. But the independence of what needs connecting has not been established.

We first assumed person and world as two independent foundations. We then introduced other independent consciousnesses. Next we demanded an explanation of how relations between them are possible. Eventually these relations became some of philosophy's greatest problems.

One accepted division gives rise to further questions, whose answers often call for additional foundations. The problems multiply, although their complexity does not establish that the original division was necessary.

**Distinction is not division of existence itself.**

“I” differs from “world.” “Mine” differs from “another's.” “Inside” differs from “outside.” These are meaningful distinctions.

But why must they denote independent original foundations instead of ways in which experiencing is distinguished?

If no such foundation has been established, the philosophical abyss may belong to a description we adopted, rather than to what exists.

Another beginning becomes possible.

Do not seek to assemble the accepted separations like puzzle pieces. **Do not accept distinctions as original foundations. Do not cut a painting into pieces along the outlines of the figures depicted in it.**

**The distinctions remain, but unexperienced independent existence is not introduced behind them.**

## Without an Original Division

This is not an account of a time before people, the world, or consciousness, nor of a state once possessed and then lost.

It is a philosophical starting point without the prior acceptance of independent domains of existence.

What is there?

Experiencing.

Not “my experiencing,” because “my” already assumes an “I” to whom it belongs.

Not “experiencing of the world,” which already establishes a distinction between experiencing and world.

Not “experiencing inside consciousness,” which already imagines consciousness as a container.

**Experiencing.**

World, body, thought, memory, another person, doubt, and questioning can all be distinguished. Yet none has thereby been established as an independent foundation of experiencing itself.

Monophenomenalism does not seek one further explanation for an existence already divided. If independent foundations of the distinctions have not been established, there is no necessity to begin with such a plurality.

The starting position is different:

**Everything is one phenomenon.**

This is not the conclusion that independently existing things have somehow been fused. Their independence was never accepted in the first place.

Nor is it a return to something that existed before the world. No “before” is required.

Monophenomenalism does not begin with a person studying their own consciousness. The person is itself distinguished within the phenomenon.

Nor does it begin with an observer standing before everything that exists. The observer, too, is a distinction.

**One phenomenon does not need someone to establish its unity.**

Why adopt this starting point? The possibility of asking the question does not make the habitual division necessary. An alternative asserting independent original foundations must show why those foundations are independent. Different words, objects, experiences, and descriptions do not supply such a proof by themselves.

Monophenomenalism accepts one phenomenon as its starting philosophical position, without introducing a second independent foundation. It does not claim to have proved all other ontologies logically impossible.

But it is a definite and radically different starting point from the usual division of person, consciousness, and world.

What was previously treated as an independent foundation is considered as a distinction within experience—without the need to posit independent existence behind that distinction. The necessity of each additional foundation can be examined without assuming some other independent foundation as its measure. There was no prior obligation to divide the phenomenon.

## Five Theses

*Model of the Phenomenon* expresses this way of considering what is in five theses:

1. Everything is one phenomenon.
2. The phenomenon is sensory experiencing.
3. A distinction within sensory experiencing is a concept.
4. Concepts structure the phenomenon into experience.
5. Experience is the phenomenon structured through concepts.

These five statements must not be turned into five successive events.

There is no phenomenon waiting for experiencing to arrive, no separate agent who subsequently distinguishes it, no concepts arriving from outside to process unformed material, and no finished product of this supposed operation.

The theses concern the phenomenon as considered, not a process that manufactures it.

Distinction is not an observer's action. It is experiencing as distinguished. A concept is not necessarily a word or idea inside someone's head: in the model, it is a system of distinctions.

Experience is not a collection of objects in consciousness. It is the phenomenon structured through concepts.

“I,” “world,” and “others” therefore are not three independent entities from which experiencing is produced.

**They are distinctions in the phenomenon.**

Do not replace this with a different theory. The phenomenon is not a vast box filled with little consciousnesses, a cosmic subject possessing human experiences, a hidden substance producing people, or an empty space containing things.

Each would add something the starting position does not require.

There is no separate phenomenon behind experiencing, no separate owner behind “I,” and no independent foundation behind the word “one.”

Even “one” is a concept through which the starting position is expressed.

## Who Experiences?

“I experience.”

This ordinary statement already distinguishes “I” and “experiencing.” The further thought comes easily: experiencing belongs to the “I.”

But what is this owner?

Consciousness? Personality? Subject? An inner observer?

Where is this observer found separately from experiencing?

If it appears as a thought, image, or feeling of presence, that too is experiencing. If another observer is assumed behind it, the question returns: who experiences that observer?

Who am I? The body? Thoughts? Memory? The one who notices thoughts? The one who realizes that thoughts are being noticed? And who realizes that last thing?

Every proposed “I” is once again distinguished in experiencing.

**Experiencing is experiencing by the very fact that it is.**

It does not need to be experienced a second time in order to become experiencing. No last observer is required to confirm it.

And the missing owner need not be replaced with a grander one.

Not “I am the only experiencer.” Not “all people are one experiencer.” Not “the universe observes itself” as a newly compulsory entity.

Each would once again introduce someone to whom the phenomenon supposedly belongs.

But the model requires no such owner.

**There is the phenomenon. The distinction “I experience” is already it.**

## Where Did the Others Come From?

“I” is determined through “not-I,” one's own through what is not one's own, inner through outer, self through world.

This is not a story about other people being created from the self over time. It concerns how distinctions determine one another.

“Another” is not a second foundation appearing beside the first. It is a determination within experience structured as “I” and “not-I.”

But the traditional formulation takes another step.

There is me. There are other people. Each must therefore have an independently existing experience. Since my experience is supposedly directly available to me, I must now find out whether their experiences exist.

The problem of other minds has been created.

But its initial division was adopted before its foundation was examined.

**Where did “my experience” come from as a separate domain of existence?**

The distinction “I” does not establish it.

**Where did “another's experience” come from as a second independent phenomenon?**

The distinction of another person does not establish it either.

There is experiencing in which faces, words, actions, reports of pain, and thoughts about another's mind are distinguished. But these distinctions do not establish an independent second phenomenon behind them.

And there is no first separate phenomenon that can finally be called mine.

This is crucial. Otherwise monophenomenalism would merely say that only my consciousness exists and everyone else is inside it. But “my consciousness,” “others,” and “inside” are themselves distinctions.

**One separate consciousness does not replace a multitude. The initial independence of consciousnesses as foundations of existence is not accepted.**

The question “Are there other minds?” can itself be distinguished in the one phenomenon. It has meaning in experience structured through “I,” “other,” “mind,” “existence,” and “knowledge.” But the question does not establish a second independent phenomenon.

In the original position there are not two independently existing consciousnesses whose relation needs explaining.

There is no compulsory problem of how to join them.

**There is nothing to join.**

## Why This Is Not Just a Game of Words

“You have called people distinctions. What does that explain?”

Renaming alone explains nothing. But a concept in the model is not an arbitrary label. It is a system of distinctions through which the relevant content of experience is determinate.

A person is not distinguished by a name alone. There are body, face, voice, actions, memories, relationships, history, conversations, and expectations. Some relations survive changes; others are confirmed or disproved. New knowledge appears.

These are meaningful structures of experience, open to investigation.

But their meaning does not prove an independent original foundation behind them that produces experiencing.

A biography is not an entity from which memories are manufactured. Biography, memory, body, and person are distinguished in the phenomenon.

And these distinctions are not isolated parts awaiting assembly. They are determinate through their relations. Their systematic character is not a further machine operating on experiencing.

Thus “What makes someone the same person after thirty years?” may be considered through memory, bodily continuity, documents, causation, and whatever gives that question content.

There is no need first to assume an independent owner of the entire biography and then discover how that owner survived.

Personal identity is already a question within a particular conceptual structure. It does not discover a compulsory separate being behind the phenomenon.

## Hume Came to the Very Edge

David Hume made an exceptionally bold move.

Looking within experience for an unchanging “I,” he found no such impression. He encountered perceptions—feelings, thoughts, pleasures, suffering—but no separate, constant impression of their supposed owner.

That brings him close to our question: why must there be an independent owner of experiencing at all?

Hume did not accept the traditional permanent self without examination.

But another difficulty followed. In his account, perceptions could be treated as distinct existences. If they are distinct, what unites them into one person? Why is a sequence of perceptions counted as one consciousness? How does personal identity arise?

Hume investigated resemblance, causal relations, and memory.

Later, in the Appendix to the *Treatise*, he admitted dissatisfaction with his own explanation: he could not reconcile the distinctness of perceptions with the need to explain their union.

This is not a weakness to ridicule. It is the honesty of a thinker who went to the limit of his starting assumptions.

Monophenomenalism begins otherwise.

**If perceptions are not originally accepted as independently existing foundations, the problem of their original union does not arise.**

This is not a secret mechanism of connection, not a new force gluing perceptions together, not another substance replacing memory.

The model does not require the prior establishment of independent existences from which one experience must be constructed.

**Hume sought a connection where his adopted formulation already involved distinctness. Monophenomenalism does not accept that distinctness as an original foundation.**

Memory and biography remain open to investigation in experience. They do not require the phenomenon to acquire unity from something else.

## Kant Approached from the Other Side

Kant appears here only as a historical parallel that helps clarify monophenomenalism's own starting point. This is neither an exposition of Kant's philosophy nor a dispute with it; the purpose is not to speak for Kant, refute him, or answer the questions he posed. **Neither Kant's concepts nor solutions to his problems are required by the five theses of *Model of the Phenomenon*.**

Immanuel Kant saw another aspect of this immense difficulty.

A mere list of perceptions does not explain coherent knowledge. How can representations concern one object? What makes unity of consciousness possible? How does objective judgment differ from an accidental sequence of impressions?

Kant developed the transcendental unity of apperception: the possibility that “I think” accompanies representations united in cognition.

He was not positing a little observer inside the head. Kant himself criticized treating the unity of thinking as proof of a simple soul-substance.

He came close to distinguishing the content of experience from unwarranted claims about an independent entity behind it.

But his investigation asks a specific question:

**What conditions are necessary for coherent human experience and objective cognition to be possible?**

Monophenomenalism does not need to deny the content of this question.

But its starting position is different:

**There is one phenomenon.**

“I,” “think,” “representation,” “unity of consciousness,” and “objective cognition” are distinctions determining the relevant experience.

Kant studies conditions for particular relations between representations. The model does not make such conditions independent foundations of the phenomenon.

This is not an automatic refutation of Kant. Nor did Kant simply propose two physical worlds under the names appearance and thing in itself.

But an exact question can be asked:

**Why must a necessary condition of a certain way of knowing become a necessary foundation of existence itself?**

A necessity demonstrated for particular judgments matters within that inquiry. To turn it into a necessity of the phenomenon as such requires another argument.

Kant sought what coherent cognition could not do without.

Monophenomenalism does not begin with a requirement to explain the existence of the phenomenon through the conditions of cognition.

Not because cognition is false.

Because **cognition is itself distinguished in the phenomenon, not posited in advance as an independent foundation of its existence**.

## Where the Problems Come From

“I” and “world” are distinguished in experience. A question arises about how one reaches the other.

“One's own” and “another's” are distinguished. A question arises about establishing someone else's experiencing.

Cause and effect, and sequences of events, are distinguished. Questions arise about the necessity of causal connection.

Beginning and end are distinguished. Questions arise about the first beginning of existence itself.

A person and life's meaning are distinguished. A demand arises for an ultimate goal without which existence would supposedly lack justification.

A present self and a possible future self are distinguished. A demand arises to become someone else before existence can count as sufficient.

These questions differ in their premises, arguments, and histories. Not all of them arise from the same assumptions.

But a recurring transition can be examined:

**What is distinguished is accepted as an independent foundation. Relations between the accepted foundations then become compulsory problems.**

Solutions may require still more foundations, which then require explanation themselves.

This complexity is not inherently a defect. Science and philosophy can describe particular relations with extraordinary precision.

But **precision within an adopted theory does not prove the necessity of all its initial foundations**.

The question is not only “How does this work?” but “What was accepted before the explanation began?”

Monophenomenalism does not add another link to an endless chain or search for a final cause behind all the others.

Its starting position contains no independently established second foundation beside the phenomenon.

**And therefore it contains no compulsory task of connecting independent beginnings that were never adopted.**

## What If a Foundation Is Discovered?

Suppose new knowledge emerges: previously unknown life, new ways of studying consciousness, evidence of worlds no one had suspected.

Such discoveries may overturn familiar explanations and reveal wholly new relations.

In experience, new distinctions are present.

But something discovered does not thereby become a second independent phenomenon beyond every phenomenon.

**Everything that is discovered is discovered in experience.**

This does not prove that any independent existence beyond experiencing must be logically impossible. Such a universal negative conclusion has not been derived.

But monophenomenalism does not begin by refuting infinitely many hypothetical foundations.

It begins with one phenomenon.

New findings can change the content distinguished in experience without automatically requiring abandonment of that starting position.

If the claim is that new findings prove an independent foundation of the phenomenon itself, that additional conclusion must be justified.

Discovering something new is not the same as proving another independent beginning.

**To discover a new distinction is not to discover a new original foundation.**

## Nothing Has Disappeared

In this position the familiar “I” is no longer the original owner of all experiencing. The world is not established as a second independent foundation. Other people are not assumed to be original owners of separate phenomena.

But what has happened to experiencing?

Nothing has been removed.

Colors, sounds, thoughts, pain, conversations, memories, and distinctions between people have not vanished. Ordinary explanations can retain their content in the circumstances where they apply.

What has changed is the philosophical status of the familiar picture.

Previously, a person was an original possessor of experience. Now the person itself is distinguished in experience.

Previously, consciousness was a place where experiencing occurred. Now “consciousness,” “place,” “inside,” and “outside” are distinctions of the phenomenon.

Previously, the task was to unite independent experiences. Now their independent existence is not accepted at the outset.

This is an immense shift in philosophical consideration, not a physical event transforming the world.

The model does not require its reader to undergo a special state. Understanding can differ, experiencing can change or remain familiar. Neither outcome is a compulsory verification or refutation of unity.

**The phenomenon does not become one when the model is adopted. It is what it is regardless of the way it is distinguished and described.**

That is its starting thesis.

## Even Unity Must Not Become an Entity

One final trap remains.

After abandoning a separate “I,” one might announce: “My true self is the whole phenomenon.”

But that reinstates a larger “I.”

“All people are one consciousness” creates another consciousness to which experiencing is supposed to belong.

A hidden substance beneath all distinctions would make the phenomenon into a thing and distinctions into its properties.

Treating unity as a higher state introduces an achiever, a path, conditions, and a result.

All these are again conceptual distinctions.

The model requires no added owner, hidden substance, absolute center, or special experience confirming its correctness.

**Even “I am the phenomenon” is a way of distinguishing what is.**

It does not establish a cosmic self.

“Unity,” “one,” and “monophenomenalism” are concepts too, not names for a final entity behind experiencing.

The model itself is a concept within what it describes. It cannot stand beyond the phenomenon as an external judge of existence.

Nor need it stand beyond the phenomenon to be a definite philosophical position.

## There Is Nothing to Join

Return to the beginning.

A person. A world. Other people. Each with a consciousness of their own.

We began with those claims and saw how many independent foundations they can silently presuppose.

Questions followed about the relation of person and world, personal identity, other minds, and the conditions of knowledge.

Hume investigated one deep difficulty of distinctness; Kant investigated necessary conditions of unity in cognition. Their work shows how substantial philosophical questions can be within particular systems.

But monophenomenalism does not begin with those systems.

Not with a person.

Not with a world independent of that person.

Not with many consciousnesses waiting to be joined.

Not with one consciousness owning everything.

**Everything is one phenomenon.**

“I,” “world,” “others,” memory, cognition, questions, doubt, and even the statement of unity are distinguished in it.

These distinctions need not be destroyed.

Nor need they be accepted as independent foundations of existence.

“How do I connect my consciousness to another's?” already presupposes mine and another's as separate origins.

“How does an observer reach the world?” presupposes an observer and world as separate independent sides.

“How does the phenomenon become one?” presupposes a division that must be overcome.

**None of these divisions had to be accepted to begin with everything as one phenomenon.**

We have not discovered a secret mechanism connecting independent existences, found a final observer, or produced a definitive theory of how consciousnesses receive experiences.

Nor have we eliminated the distinctions making up experience.

What certain descriptions added is no longer accepted as an original necessity.

The phenomenon has not become different, purer, or closer to perfection. It has not acquired freedom as a new property.

**There is nothing to join.**

Not because every philosophical problem has received a universal answer.

But because the starting position contained no independent parts of the phenomenon requiring union.

There was one phenomenon. And it remains what it is, including every distinction through which our questions arise.

**Everything is one phenomenon.**
