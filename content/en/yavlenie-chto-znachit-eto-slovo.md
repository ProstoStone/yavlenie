Phenomenon: what do we mean by the word?
An explanation of how the term is used in monophenomenalism.
Explainer
2026-11-14

Being is a phenomenon.

In monophenomenalism, being and phenomenon are not two objects placed on either side of an equals sign. The word “phenomenon” designates what is, without first defining it as matter, consciousness, subject, object, or the result of some fundamental principle at work.

This article explains a chosen use of the word. It is neither a dictionary entry nor a universal definition for every philosophical tradition. To understand the position, we must not silently substitute a different meaning for this one.

## What the word designates

“An unexplained phenomenon occurred.” We do not yet know its nature or causes, but we have already indicated that something happened. “Weather phenomenon” narrows the subject without yet telling us whether we are speaking of rain, a rainbow, or a tornado.

The English word “phenomenon” is used here to translate the Russian “явление” (yavlenie). In ordinary Russian, that word has several meanings: an event, a process, an occurrence, or an appearance. In philosophy, it can also acquire more specific meanings, including the manifestation of an underlying essence or an object as it appears in experience. We are not combining these meanings into a doctrine. We are specifying our own usage: **“phenomenon” indicates that something is, occurs, or appears in some way, without deciding in advance what that something is.**

This use does not require us to posit an underlying essence that manifests itself or a separate observer before whom it appears. The grammar of “something appears to someone” does not by itself establish two independent foundations. Nor does the word “appeared” explain what came about or how.

The word is not empty: it lets us speak of “something” as distinct from complete absence. But by itself it does not explain the nature of that “something.”

## The mirage is real as an experience; the lake may not be

A person sees water in the distance, approaches, and discovers a mirage. There was no lake. Yet the visual experience did not thereby become nothing: the explanation of what happened changed.

The same distinction holds between a conversation in a dream and a conversation that took place while awake. The dream occurred, but that does not establish that the other person actually spoke those words.

Someone hears a noise behind a door and assumes that a person is walking there. Later it turns out that a pipe was creaking. There was a noise; there were no footsteps. Acknowledging what happened does not mean accepting the first explanation.

**To acknowledge a phenomenon is not to accept its description as true or the object represented in it as existing.**

Thus, expressions such as “I imagined it,” “an error of perception,” or “a hallucination” do not erase the experience. They allow us to characterize it more accurately. The distinction between a correct explanation and a mistaken one remains.

## No additional phenomenon behind experiencing

The breadth of the term can invite a misleading picture: behind a sound, a pain, or a thought there is a formless foundation taking on different appearances.

The Model of the Phenomenon does not introduce such a foundation. “Phenomenon” does not name a substance, a container, or an additional object attached to experiencing. A sound does not first have to become a phenomenon. Pain does not need a separate bearer called “the phenomenon.”

**The indeterminacy of a word is not a property of a formless entity.**

The word “something” tells us nothing about the form of what is occurring. But failing to describe a form does not mean that we have discovered something formless. “We do not yet know what this is” and “we have established that it has no determinate character” are different claims.

That is why the phrase “without a description, the phenomenon does not exist” needs qualification. Without further description, the word does not tell us exactly what is being considered. What is happening need not wait to be named. The model does not posit another, “pure” phenomenon existing separately behind a particular experience.

## Why the model speaks of experiencing

So far, the discussion has concerned our choice of word. We now turn to how that word is used in the Model of the Phenomenon.

Its second thesis states:

**“The phenomenon is sensory experiencing.”**

This is a thesis of the model, not a dictionary definition. Here, sensory experiencing includes sensations, emotions, thoughts, and states. It does not mean only an intense feeling or a signal from one of the five senses.

Phenomenon and experiencing are not two independent things that must somehow be connected. Nor does experiencing require a separate owner situated beyond it. The “I” remains distinguishable within experience; there is no requirement to eliminate it or turn it into an independent foundation of what occurs.

Within the model, the expression “an unexperienced phenomenon” contradicts the adopted definition. But “not experienced by me at this moment” is a different claim: it does not imply “does not exist.” Other people, the past, and events known through traces and testimony are not ruled out by the absence of direct observation.

Every justification available to us is presented in experience. This fact alone does not prove that an independent reality is impossible. The model openly adopts its identification of phenomenon with experiencing; it does not derive that identification from the meaning of the word.

## “Before description” does not mean “before distinctions”

We can call something a phenomenon before explaining it. But that does not mean that a formless material existed first and was subsequently cut into things by concepts.

In the model, a distinction within sensory experiencing is a concept. A concept is not limited to a word or a deliberate act of reasoning: a color can be distinguished without a spoken name, and pain without a verbal explanation.

Thus, “before description” means without an explanation already assumed, not a special moment before every distinction. **Experience is the phenomenon itself, structured through concepts, not the product of concepts acting upon separate raw material.**

The word “phenomenon” also belongs to this description. We can begin our exposition with it, but it does not become a first concept that generates all others. The model explicitly states: “There is no first concept” (§ 1.2.6). The beginning of a text is not the beginning of existence.

## One phenomenon does not erase many distinctions

One further clarification is needed: the word “phenomenon” and the statement “the phenomenon is one” are not the same claim.

In everyday speech, rain, a rainbow, and a memory are different phenomena. This usage remains valid: it lets us distinguish, count, and investigate events.

Monophenomenalism’s starting position — **“Everything that exists is a phenomenon. The phenomenon is one.”** — addresses a different question. It does not assemble many objects into one enormous object. Within this position, distinctions are not taken as already established independent first principles that must then be connected.

Thus, “one” does not mean “the same.” Declining to treat distinctions as independent first principles does not deny differences among people, things, and experiences.

Unity is not proved by the fact that the same word can be applied to everything. A shared name alone settles nothing about the structure of what exists. We must distinguish three steps: choosing a designation, adopting a philosophical position, and defining the terms of the model.

**The word “phenomenon” designates what is. Monophenomenalism affirms its unity. The Model of the Phenomenon considers it as sensory experiencing structured through concepts.**

None of these steps introduces an additional thing behind what occurs. That is the purpose of the initial word: to speak of what exists without replacing it in advance with a particular explanation.
