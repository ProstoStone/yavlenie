Monophenomenalism in plain language
One phenomenon, many differences: how Model of the Phenomenon works and why it changes familiar questions.
Introduction
2026-11-04

Look around you.

Some things seem close and others far away. There are sounds, colors, and touches. A memory of yesterday is not like the cup on the table. Another person is not the same as your thought about them. Pain is not the same as the word “pain.”

Differences are everywhere. Sometimes everything we call life seems to depend on them.

And then comes an unusual philosophical statement:

**Everything is one phenomenon.**

The immediate objection is obvious: how can there be one when there are so many?

But monophenomenalism is not asking us to close our eyes to differences. Its question is different: **why must differences imply several independently existing foundations?**

Perhaps describing a multitude of things does not require adding a second world behind what is already there.

This thought, by itself, does not free anyone from fear or prove the structure of the universe. But it changes how we ask familiar questions—about the self, consciousness, reality, choice, and meaning.

Let us explore this without mysticism, compulsory belief, or special states one is supposedly required to attain.

## What does “everything is one phenomenon” mean?

We normally begin describing the world with separate things.

Here is a table. Here is a person. Here is a tree. Here is a thought about the tree. Here is a memory. We distinguish them and then try to discover how they relate to one another and what exists independently of what.

**Monophenomenalism adopts a starting position: everything that exists is one phenomenon.** This is not merely advice to suspend judgment about a second world. It is a definite philosophical position. The limits of inquiry available through experiencing motivate examining it, but do not by themselves prove that all other positions are impossible.

This does not mean a table and a tree are identical.

It does not mean that one person is another person.

And it does not mean that every object is part of some gigantic invisible object called “the phenomenon.”

“One” here does not count identical objects. It expresses a refusal to begin inquiry by assuming two independently given realms: the phenomenon, and then some ultimate reality behind it, already taken to be its source or owner.

Differences do not disappear. They are precisely what remains to be investigated.

We can still speak about people, events, objects, the brain, stars, and history. But none of these concepts automatically has to become a separate explanatory foundation *outside* everything under consideration.

**Unity does not abolish differences. It removes the need to explain differences by introducing a second, independent world.**

And this is a philosophical position, not a newly discovered law of physics.

## Why this is not proof that the external world is absent

There is an obvious objection.

We perceive things. Does that mean only perception exists, while things beyond perception do not?

No. That conclusion would be far too quick.

Our assertions about reality, and the ways we test them, are available to us within experience. This gives us a reason to examine the role of experiencing in our descriptions of the world.

But the fact that an assertion about an object is given in experience **does not logically entail** that the object cannot exist independently of a particular experience.

Monophenomenalism does not prove the absence of the external world. It proposes that we not treat an independently existing external source as a compulsory starting assumption unless the need for that assumption has been argued for.

This is an important difference.

“Not required to assume” is not the same as “proved not to exist.”

There is no need to reject science or call matter an illusion. Physics, biology, and medicine retain their subjects and methods. Philosophy merely asks what foundational commitments are already built into our ways of describing those subjects.

## The position and the model are not the same thing

This approach has two connected but different works.

**Monophenomenalism** states the starting philosophical position: everything that exists is considered one phenomenon. It discusses why one might adopt that position, where the available arguments run out, and what remains open.

**Model of the Phenomenon** offers a specific way of working with the position. It uses the terms “sensory experiencing,” “distinction,” “concept,” and “experience” and explores what happens when familiar philosophical problems are consistently considered through them.

We must not overlook the distinction.

One can discuss monophenomenalism without agreeing with every definition in *Model of the Phenomenon*.

And one cannot regard every interesting implication of the model as automatically proven by the single starting thesis of unity.

The position establishes a point of departure.

The model provides a vocabulary and a method of investigation.

Conclusions depend on how consistently the adopted definitions are used.

## The five theses of Model of the Phenomenon

The central structure of the model is expressed in five theses. Here they are unchanged:

1. Everything is one phenomenon.
2. The phenomenon is sensory experiencing.
3. A distinction within sensory experiencing is a concept.
4. Concepts structure the phenomenon into experience.
5. Experience is the phenomenon structured through concepts.

At first they may look like a description of a process: first a phenomenon appears, then someone experiences it, distinguishes it, creates concepts, and ends up with experience.

But that is precisely what they **do not** describe.

There are no five stages in manufacturing reality.

There is no separate observer behind the phenomenon, performing operations upon it one after another.

There is no originally shapeless material to which a conceptual grid is later applied.

The theses use different words to distinguish aspects of what the model considers as one phenomenon.

And the final thesis brings us back to the first: experience is not a second product manufactured from the phenomenon, but the phenomenon itself considered through conceptual structuring.

Even reading these sentences, understanding their words, and possibly disagreeing with them are themselves available as experience. The model does not give its reader a place outside everything being described, from which they could survey it in its entirety.

## What is sensory experiencing?

The word “sensory” can easily be understood too narrowly.

It may sound as though only the five senses are meant: sight, hearing, taste, smell, and touch.

But in *Model of the Phenomenon*, sensory experiencing includes more than sensations. It also encompasses emotions, thoughts, memories, and states.

Imagine hearing the sound of rain and remembering a walk.

The sound and the memory are distinct. One may seem external and the other internal. Yet in the experience available to us there are the sound, the memory, and the difference between them.

This is not a claim that your thoughts create the rain.

Nor is it a claim that a memory is physically made of the same material as a sound wave.

The model is not building a physical theory of rainfall or brain function. It investigates **how both are distinguished within experience**.

The ordinary explanation of rain in terms of atmospheric processes remains useful and testable. It does not disappear because the explanation itself is also accessible within human experience.

## A concept is not just a thought or a word

This is one of the most important points.

In everyday language, a concept is an idea, a theory, or a plan. Concepts may seem to exist only in the mind and to require words.

In *Model of the Phenomenon*, the term is broader.

**A concept is a distinction, understood as a system of distinctions.**

Imagine a white cup on a dark table.

The cup differs from the table in color, form, boundary, and presumed use. We can distinguish near from far, smooth from rough, cup from non-cup.

But we do not have to pronounce the word “cup” silently before the difference can be present.

And we need not introduce a separate little observer behind experiencing who attaches labels to what appears.

Here a distinction is not necessarily the deliberate act “I distinguish.” It describes how experiencing is present with differences.

So when the model calls a cup a concept, it **does not** claim that a cup is merely a word, a thought, or an invented object.

Still less does it suggest that pain will vanish if we call it a concept.

Pain can really hurt.

A person can really need help.

“Concept” is not another word for “illusion.”

It is a way of investigating the determinacy of experiencing without giving every distinction its own independent entity behind the phenomenon.

## Why “structuring” does not mean creating the world

The fourth thesis says: “Concepts structure the phenomenon into experience.”

One might imagine that concepts are tools picked up and applied to some raw material.

But that would raise further questions: who is the craftsperson, where is the raw material, and what happens before the tools are applied?

The model does not introduce such a scene.

“Structuring” does not name a manufacturing sequence. It describes how distinctions are present as experience.

Consider a melody.

We can distinguish separate notes, pitch, duration, rhythm, and the beginning and end of a phrase. That does not mean that a separate hidden melody exists somewhere, with notes subsequently glued onto it.

The melody is heard through distinguishable relations.

This is only an analogy, not proof of the model's theses.

The point is that **distinctions do not require a further independent object that must join the distinguished parts together**.

We need not imagine a phenomenon without differences, then conceptual processing, and finally a finished experience.

These are three ways of describing, not three worlds.

## What happens to the familiar “I”?

Now we approach a question that often makes philosophy intensely personal.

Who experiences all this?

The ordinary answer seems obvious: “I do.”

I see a tree. I hear a sound. I remember yesterday. I decide what to do next.

These are perfectly good statements in everyday life. They allow us to communicate, make plans, and hold one another accountable.

But the philosophical question can be made more precise.

Once the body, memory, sensation, the thought “this is me,” attention, and the feeling of presence have all been distinguished, do we require **yet another independent owner** of them all?

Within *Model of the Phenomenon*, the “I” is considered a distinction, not an independent distinguisher.

This does not mean that people do not exist.

It does not mean that everyone is identical or that one cannot distinguish one's own body from someone else's.

It does not mean that the absence of other minds has been proven.

It means that, to describe experiencing, the model does not take as a necessary starting foundation some additional entity existing in its own right behind the body, memory, thoughts, and other distinctions.

The question “Who is observing?” then turns out to be more complicated than it first appears.

We find the experience of observing and the distinction of an observer. But this does not by itself make an independent observer outside everything experienced a necessary assumption.

**Not needing a separate owner is not the same as making the human being disappear.**

## Why differences remain when the phenomenon is one

If everything is one, why do I not feel someone else's pain?

Why does one person know something another does not?

Why is my hand not the tree outside my neighbor's house?

These are serious questions. We cannot answer them simply by saying, “Because everything is one.”

In the model, differences between “mine” and “someone else's,” “here” and “there,” “my memory” and “another person's account” remain differences within experience.

The thesis of unity does not erase them.

But the existence of differences does not automatically establish that several independent metaphysical foundations are needed in order for those differences to exist.

We can distinguish several rooms in one house without declaring that every room is the same room.

The analogy has limits: the phenomenon is not pictured as a vast house containing everything. It only illustrates that **unity and the identity of every part are different claims**.

And this approach has open problems.

The thesis of a single phenomenon does not, on its own, explain the detailed organization of human perspectives, differences in memory, access to information, or the workings of consciousness. These require further arguments and inquiry.

Changing the way a problem is posed must not be presented as solving every detail of the problem.

## Freedom without a second chooser

Imagine an ordinary choice: to go for a walk or stay at home.

There is the weather, tiredness, plans, desires, and memories. There is hesitation. Sometimes there is a distinct feeling: “I am the one making this decision.”

All of these differences are real as describable experience.

But where is the independent chooser who must first separate from everything happening and then freely permit it to happen?

If such a chooser need not be assumed, the question of freedom changes.

We can continue to talk about ordinary freedom—rights, opportunities, coercion, choices, and consequences. These differences do not become less important.

But “absolute freedom” cannot simply be an unlimited version of freedom of choice.

In the strict vocabulary of the model, it too remains a concept, not a new property that the phenomenon must acquire.

Showing that there is no need to posit an independent fundamental prisoner is not promising a life without limitations.

Nor is it a demand that a person rid themselves of every desire.

**If liberation becomes an obligatory goal, the need to liberate oneself becomes another limiting distinction.**

One may choose, hesitate, fear, make mistakes, and change things. None of this requires introducing a second chooser behind what happens.

## Purpose, meaning, and error

A similar question arises with the meaning of life.

We can identify a goal: finishing a book, meeting a friend, treating an illness, building a house.

Within experience, we can distinguish the desired result, the movement toward it, success, failure, and the revision of an intention.

In *Model of the Phenomenon*, purpose and meaning are examined through such conceptual relations, especially direction toward a result.

But if individual actions have goals, does it follow that the phenomenon itself must have one ultimate goal?

Not necessarily.

To say “Life is meaningless because it has no final purpose,” we must first explain why it ought to have one.

Otherwise, the absence of a higher goal quietly becomes a violation of a requirement no one has established.

Something similar happens with mistakes.

A person can make an error in a calculation, miss a train, harm someone, or break a promise. Such errors have consequences in practical life and call for attention.

But to declare the phenomenon itself mistaken, we would need a criterion with which it was supposedly obliged to comply as a whole.

Where would that criterion come from?

The model lets us ask this without abolishing human standards, learning, or responsibility.

**Not everything that can be evaluated within experience becomes an ultimate measure of the phenomenon itself.**

## What the model does not promise

This philosophical approach can become dangerously attractive: we may want to turn it at once into a universal explanation of everything.

There is no separate owner of experiencing, so immortality has been proven.

Everything is one, so no real harm can be done.

Purpose is a concept, so nothing should ever be done.

The external world is considered within experience, so the external world does not exist.

But none of these leaps follows automatically from the starting position.

Biological death remains a fact within human descriptions of life. *Model of the Phenomenon* examines the concept of an ending, but does not prove that individual memory or personal consciousness continues after death.

Pain does not become imaginary.

Relationships, responsibility, medical care, laws, and scientific explanations do not disappear.

Nor does the phenomenon turn into an immortal, free, timeless, or omnipotent entity. Assigning such properties to it without separate grounds would repeat the same mistake as assuming a hidden second world.

Most importantly, **monophenomenalism does not demand a special experience to verify its truth**.

There is no need to attain enlightenment.

No need to stop thinking.

No need to trust a teacher.

No need to accept a philosophical claim merely because it promises peace.

One may consider the arguments, test their consistency, and disagree.

## How to test whether this way of asking questions is useful

Instead of solving every problem with one sentence, let us work through a particular example.

Someone loses their job and thinks, “I'm worthless.”

We can distinguish at least an event, memories of it, anxiety about the future, an evaluation of oneself, and an assumed standard the person believes they must meet.

Losing a job can be a serious real-world problem. It affects money, relationships, and opportunities.

But the conclusion “I'm worthless” is not a necessary logical consequence of being dismissed.

To reach it, one must add a criterion: “A person has value only if they achieve a certain kind of success.”

We can investigate where that criterion comes from, what it leads to, and how it relates to other values.

This does not guarantee that anxiety will disappear. Philosophy does not replace support, rest, practical decisions, or professional help.

But it can help identify **which additional demand turns one event into a final verdict on oneself**.

A similar form of inquiry can be applied to fear, freedom, responsibility, meaning, or the distinction between “me” and “the world.”

It does not prescribe a ready-made outcome. It makes certain usually unnoticed assumptions visible.

## The model also applies to itself

One final point is particularly important.

The sentence “Everything is one phenomenon” is itself given as a distinction.

The text of *Model of the Phenomenon*, its reader, doubt, agreement, and the attempt to refute it do not stand outside what it describes.

That does not automatically make the model true.

On the contrary, it prevents the model from quietly assigning itself a privileged position.

If every assertion is considered within experience, then assertions about experience must also remain open to examination.

We can ask whether the five theses are mutually consistent.

We can ask whether certain concepts reintroduce a hidden entity.

We can ask whether a specific conclusion truly follows from adopted definitions, or merely sounds appealing.

We can even reject the starting position.

The model does not require leaving the phenomenon in order to examine the phenomenon. But it does not receive proof merely by describing its own reading.

**The consistency of a philosophy is tested not by how impressive it sounds, but by the precision of its distinctions and arguments.**

## Where to begin in practice

There is no need to memorize the terminology or immediately accept all the proposed implications.

It is enough to begin with a few questions.

When I say “I,” what exactly is being distinguished, and is another owner needed behind the distinction?

When I say “reality,” what have I already accepted as its foundation?

When I say “freedom,” what am I contrasting it with?

When I say “mistake,” what standard makes it a mistake?

When I say “the meaning of life,” why am I assuming that existence as a whole must have a purpose assigned to it?

These questions do not forbid familiar answers. They help us see what the answers rest on.

In monophenomenalism, there is no need to become someone else first in order to gain the right to investigate what happens.

You need neither a special state nor a new world hidden behind this one.

There is what happens. There are differences. There are questions that arise within it.

**Differences don't require another world. And examining them doesn't require another observer.**

You do not even have to agree with that sentence.

It, too, is part of what can be examined.
