Monophenomenalism in Plain Language
One phenomenon, five theses, and the difference between a position and a model.
Introduction
2026-11-03

Monophenomenalism proposes considering everything that exists as one phenomenon. “One” doesn't mean all things are identical, or that there is one enormous person dreaming the world. The starting position introduces no separate, independent foundation behind the phenomenon.

This is a philosophical position, not an established scientific fact. That every assertion and test available to us is presented within experiencing motivates considering it, but doesn't by itself prove that everything that exists is identical with experiencing.

## The Position and the Model

*Monophenomenalism* states the starting position and discusses the limits of its arguments and its relation to other philosophical approaches. *Model of the Phenomenon* offers a specific way of considering things through experiencing, distinction, concept, and experience. We can discuss the position without automatically accepting all the model's definitions.

The five theses of *Model of the Phenomenon*:

1. Everything is one phenomenon.
2. The phenomenon is sensory experiencing.
3. A distinction within sensory experiencing is a concept.
4. Concepts structure the phenomenon into experience.
5. Experience is the phenomenon structured through concepts.

These aren't five steps in manufacturing experience. A shapeless experiencing doesn't appear first and then get processed by an observer. The terms distinguish what the text considers without turning it into separate entities or stages.

## What “Concept” Means Here

In everyday language, a concept is an idea or a theory. In the model, its meaning is broader: a concept is a system of distinctions. Green, pain, and a thought about pain can be considered through distinctions without becoming identical. Pain isn't reduced to the word “pain,” and saying “green” doesn't color experiencing.

So “it's a concept” doesn't, by itself, mean “it doesn't exist.” Nor does it prove that a problem is solved. We need to show the particular distinctions and steps in the reasoning.

## What Remains in Everyday Life

People, relationships, actions, research, and consequences aren't abolished. A broken kettle can be repaired. Someone in pain can be helped. Philosophical inquiry doesn't require translating every everyday conversation into special terminology.

One practical possibility is to examine how particular events become a final story about yourself and the world. Losing a job and concluding “I'm worthless,” for example, are connected in experiencing, but one doesn't prove the other. This is an interpretation, not a promise of a psychological effect.
