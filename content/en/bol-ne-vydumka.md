Pain Doesn't Become Imaginary
Why calling pain a “concept” doesn't make the pain disappear—and why making distinctions doesn't turn the world into an illusion.
Essay
2026-11-03

You accidentally touch a hot mug. Your hand jerks back; you've burned your finger. A minute later, you can explain what happened: “I burned myself. It hurts.” An hour later, you might complain about it to a friend. By tomorrow, you may have almost forgotten it.

Each of these moments involves something different: heat, pain, the movement of your hand, a memory, a story. It's tempting to assume that a philosophy that calls these things concepts must regard them as unreal—as though pain exists only because you know the word “pain.”

But that doesn't follow. In *Model of the Phenomenon*, the word “concept” has an unusual meaning. And misunderstanding that meaning can lead you astray before you've even begun exploring the position.

## Not a Word Placed on Top of a Sensation

In everyday speech, a concept is usually an idea someone has invented or learned: the concept of a state, of happiness, or of a new app. So it's easy to conclude that if pain is a concept, it must be nothing more than a thought about pain.

But in *Model of the Phenomenon*, a concept is **a system of distinctions**. This doesn't mean that a shapeless sensation comes first, then the mind finds a name for it, and only afterward does the experience of pain appear. Nor does it require a separate observer inside us, processing sensations.

Pain differs from warmth. A burn differs from the memory of a burn. A sudden touch differs from the uneasy anticipation of one. These aren't merely items on a list of words: each distinction has its own felt content.

To say that pain is conceptually distinguished is not to replace pain with a description of it. If you're in pain, no philosophical explanation makes it hurt any less. And if someone near you is in pain, talking about concepts is no substitute for helping them.

## The World Doesn't Disappear When We Notice Distinctions

Consider an ordinary traffic light. Red is distinguished from green; stopping from moving; an intersection from the road leading to it. Rules, habits, actions, and dangers are connected with these distinctions.

We can say that the traffic light, the road, and the driver are considered through particular distinctions. But it doesn't follow that the car is unreal, that a collision is imaginary, or that you can safely step into traffic.

Quite the opposite: distinctions matter precisely because they have content in experience. Color, speed, the sound of brakes, and fear are not the same thing. The fact that they don't require several independent foundations doesn't turn them into nothing.

The boundaries between “mine” and “someone else's,” “before” and “after,” “success” and “mistake” can also be examined. But examining a boundary isn't the same as erasing everything distinguished on either side of it.

## And Who Does It All Belong To?

The everyday phrase “my finger hurts” is useful and perfectly understandable. It lets us speak to a doctor, ask for help, or explain what happened. There's no need to ban it or replace it with special philosophical language.

Still, we can take a closer look at what is being called “mine”: a body, a finger, pain, memory, a name, a person's history. All of these are distinguishable. But does the experiencing of pain also require us to assume a separate owner existing behind everything on that list?

*Model of the Phenomenon* doesn't require such an owner as a fundamental entity. Nor does it ask us to declare people imaginary. A person in pain doesn't become any less important because we're examining what the word “I” means.

There's an important difference between these two claims: **“No separate fundamental owner of experiencing is required”** and **“No one can be harmed.”** The second does not follow from the first. Actions and consequences, help and care, and responsibility remain part of the world we live in.

## A Concept Doesn't Have to Destroy What It Distinguishes

Sometimes philosophical explanations sound as if their purpose were to unmask the familiar world. Discover that everything is experiencing, and suddenly you're supposed to stop trusting your eyes, your feelings, your relationships, or your memories.

But there's another way to approach it.

You can drink hot coffee, enjoy seeing someone, miss a person, or dread an uncomfortable conversation. You can examine each experience, noticing the distinctions through which it becomes definite, without demanding that the coffee, the joy, the longing, or the fear disappear.

You can make a mistake in a calculation and correct it. You can argue about what's true. You can examine the model itself, question it, and find weaknesses in it. “It's a concept” isn't a universal answer that ends the conversation. It opens up the next question: what exactly is being distinguished, and what conclusion genuinely follows from that?

That's why *Model of the Phenomenon* shouldn't be read as a declaration that the world is unreal. It offers a way of considering the world without first introducing a separate foundation behind the phenomenon. It's a philosophical position you can agree or disagree with, not a magic formula that cancels everyday experience.

Pain remains pain. Red remains red. Human life doesn't become a stage set just because we try to understand more precisely the words we use to describe it.

**To distinguish something is not to destroy what is distinguished.**
