There Are No Mistakes
Why a mistaken action doesn't turn your whole life into a mistake.
Essay
2026-11-03

You sent the wrong message. Wasted money. Stayed where you should have left. Left somewhere you might have stayed.

And suddenly this isn't about one action anymore. In your head, an entire biography of a defective person begins to unfold.

“I ruined everything.”

One word deserves attention here: **everything**.

What exactly has been ruined? A conversation? An agreement? The result of your work? Or has your very existence somehow become a failure?

## Where Is the Standard for a Mistake?

A calculation can be wrong. If a structure collapses because of it, philosophical reasoning won't erase the consequences. If you've hurt someone, what happened matters, as does what you can do now. Mistakes in specific activities exist in relation to conditions, aims, knowledge, and agreements.

But where is the standard by which an entire life becomes a mistake?

We often imagine a correct version of our life running alongside the one we've lived. In that version, we chose the right career in time. Said the right words. Didn't lose someone. Didn't waste years. This version, meanwhile, is the spoiled copy.

We compare what is happening with that image and call the difference proof of our defeat.

Yet this imagined life is available to us precisely as an imagined life. We know its fortunate turns because we put them there. We can leave its difficulties unwritten. It can hardly lose a fair comparison: it's made out of what we currently lack.

That doesn't prove our earlier decisions were the best ones. Sometimes a decision was bad. Sometimes we really missed an opportunity, and regret has grounds. But a bad decision doesn't make an entire life wrong.

“There are no mistakes” questions that sweeping verdict. In the language of *Model of the Phenomenon*, an error is considered through a particular inconsistency among concepts. This doesn't turn being mistaken into a property of the whole phenomenon.

## Repair Without a Verdict

You don't have to justify what happened by saying “it had to happen” or “it's all for the best.” We may not know whether it is for the best. Some losses are never compensated. You don't have to turn them into lessons before allowing yourself to go on living.

What happened is there. It can be examined, mourned, and repaired where repair is possible. None of this requires the additional conclusion: “Therefore, I myself am a mistake.”

Responsibility is concrete. Acknowledge what you did. Hear how it affected someone else. Repay a debt. Redo the work. Ask for forgiveness, knowing that no one owes it to you.

Self-condemnation may accompany all of this, but it replaces none of these actions. Years of inner punishment don't, by themselves, return what another person lost.

Nor must you immediately get rid of regret. “Accept your past properly” can easily become another reason to blame yourself.

You can regret something and keep living. You can correct an action without declaring a person defective. You can remain unsure how another life would have turned out.

The next thing you try may go well or badly. Something may need fixing.

But you don't have to retake an exam for the right to exist every time.
