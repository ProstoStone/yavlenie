There Are No Mistakes
Where does “wrong” come from if the phenomenon itself has no standard it must meet?
Essay
2026-11-03

A cup lies shattered on the floor. A minute ago it was standing on the table. Now there are shards, spilled coffee, a wet tablecloth. Someone says, “I made a mistake. I should have put it farther from the edge.”

It seems obvious that a mistake has occurred. But where, exactly, is it?

In the shards? In the movement of a hand? In the sound of breaking glass? In the regret? If the person had wanted to get rid of an old cup and deliberately smashed it, the very same outcome might be called a success. If the cup was a favorite, a loss. If it was part of a performance, a spectacular finale.

The shards are still shards. What has changed is **what we compare the event against**.

This is not wordplay. It leads to a question we seldom notice ourselves asking: can a mistake exist in its own right, apart from the standpoint and standards by which something is called a mistake? And can it be not merely a person who makes a mistake in a particular task, but existence itself—the phenomenon as such?

## To Make a Mistake, You First Need a Standard

You can get an answer wrong in a calculation. Make a losing move in chess. Say something in a conversation that you later regret. But none of these events carries around a separate substance called “mistake.”

To identify a mistake, we need distinctions: what the task was, which rule applies, what result was expected, what actually happened, and where a mismatch was found. In arithmetic, an answer is assessed according to the rules of arithmetic. In chess, a move is judged by the game's rules and the goal being pursued. In a relationship, words can be evaluated in terms of trust, promises, intentions, and another person's feelings.

This does not mean that someone consciously invents the rule every time. Many standards are learned so deeply that they seem like properties of things themselves. We inherit language, upbringing, habits, laws, and ideas of good and bad. Sometimes a standard is already at work before we even notice it. But an unnoticed standard is still a standard.

Remove the condition, and the word “mistake” loses its definite meaning. Under one condition, what happened fails to fit; under another, it fits. This does not make the outcomes identical. It shows that **being a mistake belongs to a relationship between distinguished conditions, not to the event as a stamp it bears in itself**.

If you write “two plus two equals five,” that is a mistake in ordinary arithmetic. Calling the mistake conceptual does not make the answer correct. It means seeing exactly where its incorrectness lies: within a particular system of distinctions, not as a crack in the fabric of existence.

## What Rule Is the Phenomenon Itself Supposed to Follow?

Now we can take the step that makes this question worth asking.

To call the broken cup a mistake, we need a criterion: “The cup should have remained intact.” To call a decision a mistake, we need some way to evaluate it. But by what rule is **the whole phenomenon** supposed to unfold? What could we compare it with in its entirety and then declare, “Here is where existence happened incorrectly”?

We can imagine an ideal world where cups never break, loved ones never die, and people never hurt each other. We can long for such a world, make plans, and work to reduce suffering. But the picture of how things “should have been” also appears in experiencing—as a thought, a wish, a comparison, a project. It does not thereby become an independent blueprint lying somewhere outside the phenomenon, entitled to demand an accounting from it.

In *Model of the Phenomenon*, everything is one phenomenon, and a distinction within sensory experiencing is a concept. Concepts structure the phenomenon into experience. Mistake, rule, correspondence, violation, cause, effect, “it could have been otherwise”—these, too, are distinctions within experience. We cannot carve out a special exemption for the concept of a mistake and place it outside everything it is meant to judge.

So **“the phenomenon itself makes no mistakes”** does not mean that we have inspected every event and simply failed to find any. It means something more radical: the phenomenon has no external, nonconceptual yardstick by which it could fail to be what it is. It is not sitting an examination to earn the right to exist as it does.

Mistakes are distinguished *within the phenomenon*. But **the phenomenon itself cannot be a mistake**.

This conclusion belongs to the philosophical position being proposed; it is not a proof of that position from universally accepted facts. The position is open to disagreement. Yet if everything is considered as one phenomenon, the idea of an absolute mistake is no longer something we can meaningfully identify.

## “I Should Have Done It Differently”—Where Is That “Differently”?

One of the heaviest thoughts a person can carry is also one of the simplest: “If only I'd acted differently back then.”

If only I hadn't said it. If only I had. If only I'd left sooner. If only I'd stayed. If only I'd understood in time. In that thought, the present meets an imagined fork in the road, and we begin to judge the life that happened by the life that did not.

But the “other life” is not given to us as a second reality that actually took place. It is here now—as a memory, a supposition, a fantasy, a calculation. In the imagined version, we can alter one decision while leaving everything else conveniently familiar. In actual experiencing, we have no access to a completed duplicate of the world against which to run a fair comparison.

This does not forbid us to consider alternatives. If a bridge collapsed because of an error in calculation, investigating the failure matters if we want the next bridge to stand. If we hurt someone, we can try to understand which words caused pain. Counterfactual scenarios are useful, sometimes indispensable.

But a scenario's usefulness does not make it a supreme court over the fact of existence. “I would have preferred something else” and “what happened had no right to happen” are different claims. The first is a distinction within experience. The second would require a law binding the phenomenon as a whole.

Nor do we need to say, “Everything happened exactly as it did because it couldn't possibly have happened otherwise.” That merely installs causal necessity as another ultimate foundation. We need neither justify the past by appealing to fate nor prove it was the best of all possibilities. **What happened does not have to have been necessary, right, or useful in order to have happened.**

## Who, Exactly, Makes the Mistake?

“I made a mistake” is a perfectly ordinary human sentence. It helps us acknowledge an action, explain a decision, apologize, or redo a piece of work. There is no reason to ban it from the language.

But look more closely at the “I” in that sentence. There is a body, memory, an intention, fear, an action, a later evaluation. The “I who acted” and the “I who now condemns the action” are distinguished within experiencing. For a mistake to be distinguished between them, we do not need to add a further owner, separate from all of this, standing behind the world and directing events infallibly.

We can call ourselves agents, guilty, responsible, careless. Those words have recognizable conditions of use. But **a separate agent as an independent foundation** does not spring into existence merely because the sentence has the subject “I.”

Here it is easy to make the opposite substitution: if there is no separate owner, perhaps nobody acts and nobody can be held responsible. But that confuses levels again. Responsibility, promises, harm, repair, and trust remain. They do not need an ontological owner beyond the phenomenon in order to be meaningful distinctions in life.

## But What About Consequences? Surely They're Real

Yes. The cup broke. A shard can cut your finger. Someone else may be angry. A poor decision can be followed by the loss of money, work, or a relationship. None of this should be denied for the sake of the elegant phrase “there are no mistakes.”

But consider what we call a *consequence*. We distinguish one event from another, arrange them in time, and establish a connection: “This happened because of that.” That is how we describe, investigate, predict, and act. Such descriptions can be more or less accurate. They can save lives.

Yet in *Model of the Phenomenon*, time and causality are not introduced as an independent framework behind experiencing. **Outside concepts, there are no “consequences” as special things waiting for us beyond the phenomenon.** There is the phenomenon, within which we distinguish an action, pain, loss, the following day, a causal connection, an attempt to make things right. None of this is an illusion or a nothing. But none of it falls outside the phenomenon to become an independent verdict upon it.

Saying “there are no consequences outside concepts” does not mean “you can jump off a roof and nothing will happen.” Jumping, falling, injury, the fear of loved ones, medical treatment—these are precisely the distinctions we take seriously in experience. What makes them “consequences of a mistake,” however, is a particular set of conceptual connections. That label is not inscribed on the phenomenon from outside.

A consequence therefore does not prove that existence made a mistake. It can be excruciating, irreversible, terrifying. **This argument does nothing to diminish pain. But pain is not thereby evidence that existence itself is fundamentally wrong.**

## So Why Are We Afraid of Making Mistakes?

We are rarely afraid of nothing more than a wrong number or an unfortunate remark. We fear what they supposedly establish about us once and for all. “I'll ruin everything.” “I can't be trusted.” “I'll miss my only chance.” “My life will turn out to have been wrong.”

Between an action and its evaluation, an entire courtroom grows. There is a defendant, an ideal image of oneself, a verdict, and an imagined correct biography one must spend a lifetime trying to catch up with.

But everyone in this courtroom is a distinction within experiencing. There is no observation post outside the phenomenon from which someone compares your life against a final, authoritative version of how it was supposed to go.

In this sense, **there is no reason to fear a mistake as an absolute event: there is nowhere for such a mistake to occur**. You may fear pain, rejection, loss, punishment, death. Fear may arise, and that is not itself a mistake. You can take precautions, learn, and ask for help. But there is no additional danger hovering over all of this—the danger that your very existence might turn out to have been lived incorrectly.

Even the demand “stop being afraid, because there are no mistakes” would introduce a new standard of correctness and produce a new “mistake”: being afraid when you were supposed to be free. There is no need for that. Fear, too, is not a failure of the phenomenon.

## What If Someone Does Something Cruel?

This needs to be said plainly: the absence of an absolute mistake **does not render cruelty harmless or dishonesty honest**.

When one person hurts another, suffering, action, intention, betrayal of trust, protection, restitution, and punishment can all be distinguished. Depending on the ethical, legal, and personal standards at work, we call an act cruel, criminal, or wrong. These distinctions carry weight in life: people protect one another, stop violence, and establish responsibility.

You cannot refute someone's suffering by declaring it “conceptual.” But neither can suffering be smuggled into an argument as proof of an external law requiring the phenomenon as a whole never to contain such an event.

Monophenomenalism does not ask us to be indifferent. It simply does not introduce an additional absolute court beyond all human relationships, separate from the distinctions through which those relationships are experienced. We can judge actions without claiming that existence itself has produced a defective version of reality.

## The Question of Mistakes Applies to This Argument, Too

Suppose you tell me, “This is wrong. Your reasoning contains a logical mistake.”

That is a meaningful objection. We can examine whether the conclusions follow from the premises, whether terms have been confused, whether the starting thesis has been made to say something it does not say. “There are no mistakes” does not cancel that examination or protect the model from criticism. On the contrary, it invites us to specify **which rule is at issue and exactly where a mismatch occurs**.

If a mistake is found, it can be corrected. Nothing like a mistake of the phenomenon itself will have occurred: there will have been a distinguished argument, an objection, an inconsistency, and a correction. Even the concept of a mistake is not itself a mistake simply because it is a concept.

This may be the most surprising turn of all: the statement “there are no mistakes” does not take the word “mistake” away from us. It puts the word back where it belongs.

## You Can Simply Pick Up the Pieces

The cup is still broken. The coffee needs wiping up. If it belonged to someone else, you can apologize and buy a replacement. If you cut your finger, you can treat the wound. You need not bless what happened, insist it was meant to be, or invent a story about how the loss was secretly beneficial.

Nor must you turn this understanding into an achievement. Tomorrow you may say “I made a mistake” again, feel upset, reconsider a decision. None of that will refute the point. It will be life, within which goals, results, regrets, and repairs are distinguished—and within which there is no extra examination that life itself must pass.

**Mistakes exist wherever conditions are distinguished by which something counts as a mistake. Within the phenomenon there are those conditions, their violations, experiences, and consequences. But the phenomenon itself has no condition it is obliged to fulfill. There is no mandatory blueprint against which it could fail.**

And that is why there are no mistakes.
