How a gradient becomes a picture
A field of color, contrast, and one picture: a visual introduction to distinctions.
Illustration
2026-11-03

Imagine a large sheet with no stripes or drawn objects. Color covers its whole surface: blue emerges in one corner, red and pink in another, while green, yellow, and violet patches spread below. They don't radiate from a single point or stand apart behind lines. One shades gently into another. This isn't a rainbow with predefined bands, but a whole field of shades containing many gradual transitions.

{{figure:1}}

It's easy to notice a greener area and a yellower one. There's a difference, but there needn't be one definitive line with only green on one side and only yellow on the other. The transition can be gradual, and its boundary depends on which areas we consider and how we compare them. In one place the difference seems barely perceptible; in another, distinct. The colors themselves needn't consist of ready-made, originally separate pieces.

## Neighbors and contrast

Now imagine several fragments taken from different places on the same sheet. One is deep blue, another green, a third yellow, a fourth reddish. Broad areas of gradual transition may have stood between them on the large sheet. When the fragments sit next to each other, those transitions no longer lie between them. Clear edges, noticeable combinations, and contrast appear.

{{figure:2}}

The scissors in this example belong only to the imagination. They help show how clearly parts are distinguished when placed in a new relationship. But a concept in *Model of the Phenomenon* isn't scissors, an action, or a separate mechanism cutting up some colorless raw material. It is defined as a system of distinctions. The image of cut-out sections illustrates **comparison**, not the production of experiencing.

Contrast is particularly noticeable when areas far apart in color sit together. Blue beside red, or dark beside light, can stand out more than two similar shades. This isn't about good and bad. Red and blue aren't arguing, and neither is better than the other. They are simply distinguishable in different ways within a particular combination.

There's another feature. The same green area can look different surrounded by burgundy and surrounded by yellow-green. The color of the inner square stays exactly the same, while its prominence and its perceived relationship to the background change.

{{figure:3}}

Here we can see why a color name alone doesn't describe the whole picture. What matters is both the chosen shade and its place among others. An area is set apart from its neighbors and determined in relation to them at the same time. This is a visual image of the mutual determination of distinctions, although the color example doesn't prove philosophical theses.

## From a sheet of color to an image

The same imagined sections can form a landscape. Blue and turquoise fragments become sky and water; green ones become hills; a yellow circle is distinguished as the sun; warm shades form a path. The sheet of gradual patches contained no ready-made “hills” or “sun,” yet these are clearly distinguishable in the new composition. A meaningful image emerges in which space, lighting, distance, nearness, and remoteness can be recognized.

{{figure:4}}

The picture doesn't become less real *as an image* because we see how its colors relate. On the contrary, color distinctions make it this particular image. We can remember the single sheet without declaring every fragment a separate ultimate foundation. Unity doesn't mean gray uniformity, and diversity doesn't require independent foundations.

The image of the sheet and the assembled picture has a limit. It doesn't follow that an undistinguished phenomenon actually existed first, that someone cut pieces from it, and that a world was then assembled. Cutting, rearranging, and assembling are actions within the illustration. **Experience in the model isn't the result of such a process.**

## From colors to experiencing

Similar comparisons can be noticed in everyday experience without colored paper. Someone sits on the steps holding a cup of coffee. The warm cup is distinguished against cool air; the taste of coffee among other sensations; tiredness in relation to movement, rest, expectations, and what came before. Calm may be distinguished, or anxiety. Words such as “warmth,” “tiredness,” “joy,” and “anxiety” don't replace what's happening with separate objects. They allow us to talk about distinctions with content within experiencing.

Anxiety doesn't thereby become something bad by the very constitution of the phenomenon, or joy something good by a universal rule. Evaluations also belong to particular experience and can matter enormously to a person. The analogy doesn't abolish evaluations, pain, or joy. It allows us to examine how each experiencing is distinguished and in relation to what. A single situation can include many relationships that one name cannot exhaust.

Strong color contrast often makes an image more expressive. This is a useful prompt for considering differences in experience, not a law of psychology. We cannot conclude that every feeling grows stronger as its surroundings become more contrasting, or that all feelings lie along one continuous spectrum. Their particular content cannot be deduced from the design of our picture.

Images of white light or silence may bring distinguishability and indistinguishability to mind. Yet white light doesn't prove a hidden colorless foundation, and silence isn't necessarily the result of all sounds canceling each other out. These too are distinguishable when discussed or experienced. The illustration has no stage at which every distinction has literally been removed, leaving “pure experiencing.”

The gradient and the picture composed of its imagined sections help us consider a simple but easily overlooked thought: **the definiteness of what is experienced doesn't require independent cuts in the phenomenon, and the mutual determination of distinctions doesn't empty them of content**. The world doesn't become empty because it can be considered through distinctions, just as an image doesn't disappear when we understand its color relationships.

> To see a picture as one, you don't have to erase the image. To distinguish its colors, you don't have to treat them as separate worlds.
