A glossary of Model of the Phenomenon
Clear explanations of key terms, with examples, distinctions in meaning, and the limits of philosophical conclusions.
Reference

Ordinary words sometimes become surprisingly difficult when we use them to describe experiencing itself. “I see a tree,” “this is a real thing,” “I made a decision,” “my life has meaning”—these phrases rarely need explaining in everyday conversation. The philosophical question begins when we ask what we have already assumed by saying them.

*Model of the Phenomenon* offers a particular vocabulary for investigating those assumptions. It is not an attempt to replace every familiar word with a new one. It is a way of noticing distinctions that usually go unquestioned.

**The most important reading rule:** when the model calls something a “concept,” it does not mean that the thing is imaginary. When the model does not introduce an independent foundation, it does not mean that the absence of such a foundation has been proven. And when it speaks of one phenomenon, it does not erase differences among people, events, and experiences.

This is not a universal dictionary of philosophy. It explains terms *within Model of the Phenomenon*, distinguishing its technical language from the broader starting position of *Monophenomenalism* when necessary. The examples are there to clarify the ideas, not to prove the five theses.

The English terminology matters: *experiencing* translates «переживание», while *experience* translates «опыт». *Sensory experiencing* is «чувственное переживание». These are not interchangeable labels in the model.

## Where the model begins: five theses

The five foundational theses are quoted unchanged and in their original order:

1. Everything is one phenomenon.
2. The phenomenon is sensory experiencing.
3. A distinction within sensory experiencing is a concept.
4. Concepts structure the phenomenon into experience.
5. Experience is the phenomenon structured through concepts.

They do not describe five successive actions. We need not imagine shapeless material appearing first, an invisible observer then imposing concepts upon it, and experience being produced at the end. All five formulations describe what is being considered as one phenomenon, through different distinctions.

The fifth thesis does not announce a new product: it returns us to the phenomenon of the first. Even reading the model itself, understanding it, and doubting it are accessible as experience.

## Phenomenon

**In the model:** that which is, and is as sensory experiencing.

Imagine hearing rain, seeing light in a window, and thinking about tomorrow. We distinguish a sound, a color, a thought, and an idea of time. The model is not asking us to locate an additional hidden thing behind these, called “the phenomenon,” which somehow produces them all.

“Phenomenon” is not the name of a gigantic object. The term lets us consider what is already there without adding another independent entity at the beginning of the explanation.

**Do not confuse:** in *Monophenomenalism*, the word is used in a starting statement about everything that exists. That is a philosophical position; the presence of experiencing alone does not strictly prove that everything which exists is identical with experiencing.

## One

**In the starting position:** everything that exists is one phenomenon. This is not merely advice to refrain from hypothesizing a second world: it is an adopted philosophical claim. No separately established second foundation is introduced. The fact that all available tests take place within experiencing motivates the position, but does not by itself prove it.

“One” does not mean all things have one shape, that all people are one person, or that many sensations merge into an indistinguishable mass. Hot differs from cold, yesterday from today, one's own action from another's.

Imagine a conversation: two voices are heard as two voices even though the conversation is described as one happening. This is only an analogy, but it helps distinguish unity from sameness.

**Important:** “one” is not a number assigned to an item on a list of objects, nor the name of a universal consciousness. Not needing to posit an independent second foundation does not prove that such a foundation is impossible.

## Sensory experiencing

**In the model:** everything experienced, including sensations, emotions, thoughts, and states.

The name can be misleading: it may sound as if only the five senses—sight, hearing, touch, taste, and smell—are meant. But remembering, worrying, imagining tomorrow, and thinking “I doubt” also count as experiencing in the model.

When you feel cold and remember a previous winter, the cold and the memory are different. The model does not make them one physical process or erase the difference between them. It investigates them as distinguishable within experiencing.

**Do not confuse:** experiencing does not necessarily mean consciously thinking *about* an experience. Nor does the term itself establish a separate owner standing behind everything experienced.

## Distinction

**In the model:** the way sensory experiencing is as distinguished.

A white cup on a dark table is distinguished by color, boundary, and form. The sound of rain differs from a voice. Pain differs from a memory of pain. These differences need not arise only after somebody has silently named them.

A “distinction,” then, is not a separate action performed by someone who first looks upon an utterly empty surface and then draws lines across it. It concerns the determinate ways experiencing is considered to be present.

**Do not confuse:** making a distinction does not prove that the things distinguished are identical or that differences have no real consequences. Nor does the distinction demand an additional independent “distinguisher” as its ultimate foundation.

## Concept

**In the model:** a system of distinctions. This is broader than the everyday word “idea.”

In ordinary speech, a concept may mean a plan, a theory, or a thought. Here the term concerns distinction as such, not only its verbal description.

Green can be called green, but the word “green” does not produce its greenness. A person can feel pain without forming the sentence “I am in pain.” Pain, the name for pain, and a thought about pain are therefore not the same distinction, although all can be considered through the model's concepts.

**Do not confuse:** “this is a concept” does not mean “it is only in someone's head,” “it is an illusion,” “it does not exist,” or “nothing needs to be done about it.” Calling pain a concept does not eliminate pain or the need to help someone who is hurting.

## A system of distinctions

Why define a concept as a system rather than a single simple label?

Because a particular “this” is distinguished in relation to “not this,” alongside something else, through a boundary, a relation, or a direction. When you say “far,” “near” and “far,” place and distance are already distinguished. When you say “now,” “before,” “after,” and “now” are distinguished.

But this does not imply a rigid scheme in which there is one first, fundamental concept from which all the others grow like branches. The model establishes neither a compulsory tree nor a primary element nor a chronological order in which distinctions arise.

**A useful check:** ask what a particular distinction is related to, and whether you are quietly introducing an independent object behind the relations you are already describing.

## Structuring

**In the model:** how concepts are as experience.

The word can sound like a manufacturing process: first comes the “raw material” of experiencing, then someone processes it with concepts, and finally a world of objects is produced.

The model assumes no such factory.

Imagine a melody: notes, rhythm, repetition, pauses, and the beginning and ending of a musical phrase can all be distinguished. We need not assume an additional hidden melody existing independently of these relations in order to describe what is heard as a melody.

This is only an illustration. The philosophical point is that **structuring does not name a causal operation, a stage in time, or the production of a new phenomenon out of an earlier one**.

## Experience

**In the model:** the phenomenon structured through concepts.

In everyday speech, “experience” may refer to knowledge or skills acquired over time: “I have years of experience.” In philosophy it can refer to observations or what somebody has lived through.

Here the term is precisely defined by the fifth thesis.

When you see a cup, distinguish its shape, remember who owns it, and wonder whether the tea inside is hot, all this can be considered as experience. But experience does not become a second object manufactured by someone out of a separate phenomenon.

**Do not confuse:** *experience* and *experiencing* are not two independent worlds in this model. The distinction between the English terms is particularly important: *experiencing* corresponds to «переживание», and *experience* to «опыт».

## “Is” and givenness

The word “is” sounds simple, yet it can quietly import an entire ontology.

In the expression “the phenomenon is,” it need not identify an object with a predetermined collection of properties, located in some special space or time. In the model's language, it is a way of not looking away from what is already given in the course of examination.

The fact that experiencing is given as a starting point for inquiry does not automatically prove the entirety of monophenomenalism. One may acknowledge experiencing while rejecting the thesis that everything which exists is one phenomenon.

**The crucial distinction:** a starting philosophical position, the model's definitions, and arguments offered in their support have different roles. They must not be substituted for one another.

## The “I” and the subject

“I see,” “I remember,” “this belongs to me”—these are necessary and intelligible expressions in everyday life. A person differs from other people and has a body, a history, a name, and relationships.

But what exactly does “I” designate when made into a philosophical question?

Within experience we can distinguish the body, memory, thoughts about oneself, the feeling of presence, and the difference between “mine” and “someone else's.” The model allows us to investigate these **without assuming an additional independent owner in advance**, supposedly located behind them.

This does not make a person imaginary or all people identical. It means that the “I,” in this analysis, is a distinction, not a necessarily independent entity producing distinctions.

**For example:** “I changed my mind” works perfectly well in daily life. The philosophical question is only whether a second, unchanging thinker must be assumed behind the change of mind.

## Observation and consciousness

It may seem obvious that if something is observed, there must be an observer serving as the independent foundation of observation.

But in experience we can distinguish seeing, attention, the thought “I am observing,” the body, and the idea of an observer. The model does not require us to add a separate metaphysical entity to these at the outset in order to explain the presence of distinctions.

“Consciousness,” viewed in this way, need not be an invisible container for experiences or the creator of the world. It becomes a subject for conceptual inquiry.

**Limit of the conclusion:** refusing to posit a hidden observer does not solve every question about the brain, the mechanisms of perception, or other minds. Those questions remain open to scientific and philosophical investigation.

## The external world and matter

If everything we say about the world is accessible through experience, does that mean the external world does not exist?

No.

When you see a tree, you can scientifically investigate its growth, its cells, and its interactions with light. Such explanations remain useful, testable, and significant.

Monophenomenalism directs our attention elsewhere: every justification available to us—observation, experiment, logical inference—is formulated and assessed within experience. This **does not entail** that an object existing independently of a particular experience has been proved not to exist.

**Reading rule:** “the independent external world is not introduced as a compulsory starting foundation” is not equivalent to “the external world has been proved absent.” The model does not require us to declare matter imaginary.

## Reality and illusion

In a dream, you may see a street and, upon waking, realize it was a dream. In ordinary life, you can mistake a reflection for an object or misjudge a distance.

The distinction between real and illusory matters: it helps us test observations and correct errors.

But the distinction “reality versus illusion” itself involves criteria: what counts as verification, which observations we compare, and what we expect to discover.

The model examines these criteria as conceptual relations within experience. It **does not** conclude that the real and the imagined are equivalent in every respect or that checking anything is unnecessary.

Conversely, calling something real does not by itself establish an independent ultimate entity beyond all our ways of testing it.

## Time and space

In ordinary life, we readily distinguish “yesterday,” “now,” and “tomorrow,” “here” and “there.” Clocks help us make appointments, and maps help us find our way. These distinctions work.

Within the model, time and space are examined as ways of structuring experience: through “before and after,” “nearer and farther,” succession, position, and movement.

This is not a claim that clocks have stopped, distances have vanished, or the laws of physics no longer hold.

There is a subtler danger: refusing to make time a fundamental property of the phenomenon can be mistakenly transformed into the claim “the phenomenon is timeless by nature.” But **timelessness** would then become another property ascribed to the phenomenon without justification.

More precisely: the model does not treat a temporal axis as a necessary independent foundation of the phenomenon itself. Temporal descriptions remain applicable where they are useful.

## Cause and effect

A kettle heats water. Rain makes clothes wet. Medicine affects someone's condition. Causal explanations help us act, test expectations, and prevent harm.

The model does not ask us to abandon such explanations.

Instead it asks: when we say “cause” and “effect,” which distinctions are already involved—“earlier and later,” “condition and outcome,” “what would have happened otherwise”?

In this vocabulary causality is considered within experience, not as a separately established law that must govern the phenomenon *from outside*.

**Do not confuse:** not introducing absolute causal necessity as a starting foundation does not prove that the phenomenon possesses the property of being “uncaused.” Nor does it abolish the causes investigated by science and everyday practice.

## Purpose, meaning, and value

Imagine going to buy bread. The goal is clear: to obtain bread. If the shop is closed, the outcome does not match your intention. You can change your plan or go home.

In the model, purpose is examined through direction toward a result, and meaning also through such direction. Value is distinguished in relation to preference: what matters, to whom, and compared with what.

But the fact that a particular action has a goal does not entail that **the entire phenomenon must have an ultimate purpose**.

And if no ultimate purpose is found, this does not prove that existence is deficient. To conclude that something is missing, we must establish why existence was obliged to satisfy that particular goal.

**Do not confuse:** freedom from mandatory meaning does not prohibit goals, care, love, or curiosity. It only refuses to make any of them the ultimate condition of everything that exists.

## Choice, will, and freedom

We may choose between walking and working, freely agree to meet someone, or experience coercion. These are real distinctions in human life.

The model examines how a chooser, alternatives, desires, actions, and consequences are distinguished within experience. It does not assume without argument a separate “I” residing outside everything happening and deciding from an independent standpoint.

Ordinary freedom is distinguished in relation to restrictions: freedom of movement relative to prohibition, freedom of choice relative to the lack of choice.

But “absolute freedom,” in the model, is not a superpower allowing us to do anything whatsoever. It, too, is a concept, not a special property of the phenomenon or a prize to be earned.

**Important:** not needing an independent doer does not eliminate rights, obligations, practical responsibility, or the consequences of action.

## Error, truth, and consistency

When a calculation is wrong, a train departs earlier than expected, or a prediction fails, an error can be established in relation to rules, measurements, and results.

In the model, error is examined through the inconsistency of concepts: expectation and outcome, rule and action, what was assumed and what was observed.

So the statement “error is a concept” **does not** mean “errors never occur.” It means that we distinguish an error through a criterion against which something is compared.

Consistency also needs careful specification. Logical noncontradiction, agreement with observations, and a felt sense of harmony are different things.

Even perfect internal consistency among the model's formulations would not prove its starting philosophical position to be uniquely true.

## Responsibility and other people

When somebody breaks a promise, causes harm, or helps someone, it matters. Within experience we distinguish participants, deeds, consequences, and accepted standards of evaluation.

The model allows responsibility to be considered through these distinctions without making one participant the absolute first cause of everything that happens.

It does not follow that “nobody is responsible for anything.” In practical life, responsibility remains important for agreements, care, law, and shared decisions.

**The precise boundary:** not positing a fundamentally independent owner of the phenomenon does not mean other people, their experiences, or their legitimate claims upon one another cease to exist.

## Death, endings, and absence

The death of a person is a real event in medical, biographical, and social descriptions. It involves loss and consequences for those who remain alive.

The model lets us examine the concepts of an ending, a beginning, loss, and nonbeing separately. To speak of the end of a particular biography is not yet to prove the end of the phenomenon itself.

But the converse matters equally: the fact that we cannot directly experience our own absence as a state does not establish personal immortality or the continuation of memory after death.

The model does not promise the survival of the familiar “I,” establish what happens after death, or replace knowledge of human mortality.

**Distinguish:** the limit of a particular human life and a claim about an absolute limit of the entire phenomenon. Neither automatically establishes the other.

## A property and its negation

This is one of the most important safeguards against misreading the model.

It is easy to say, “The phenomenon need not exist in time,” and quietly turn this into “the phenomenon is eternal and timeless.”

But the second statement already attributes a special nature to it.

The same happens with “outside space,” “without a cause,” “without a subject,” or “absolutely free,” if such phrases are understood as a list of properties belonging to a mysterious entity.

**The rule is symmetrical:** we must not ascribe positive properties to the phenomenon without grounds, but neither may we declare their opposites—timelessness, spacelessness, absolute absence of causality—to be proven.

It is more precise to speak about the limits of *our description*: the model does not adopt time, space, causality, or a separate owner as compulsory independent foundations. This does not reveal a hidden nature of the phenomenon, not even a “nature of having no properties.”

## Self-application

There is one question we must ask the model itself: what happens to the model if everything is considered one phenomenon?

Its text, the reader, understanding, disagreement, and doubt are also distinguished within experience. The theory cannot quietly be moved outside what it aims to describe and declared the sole external observer.

But self-application is not automatic proof.

The fact that an objection can be described in the model's terms does not make the objection false. And the fact that the model can include its own reading within its subject matter does not confirm its initial theses.

**A useful question:** are we mistaking the consistency of the model's language for a proof of independent truth about the world?

## Monophenomenalism, the model, and its implications

Finally, three levels of inquiry should be kept distinct.

**The starting position:** *Monophenomenalism* proposes considering everything that exists as one phenomenon. This is a philosophical starting point, not a scientifically established fact.

**The model's definitions:** *Model of the Phenomenon* fixes five theses and technical meanings for “experiencing,” “distinction,” “concept,” and “experience.”

**Applications and interpretations:** inquiries into consciousness, freedom, death, meaning, and ethics explore what can be said when those definitions are adopted. They do not all follow with equal logical force from the single thesis of unity.

One may accept the starting position without agreeing with a particular application of the model. One may also criticize the starting position itself.

The value of this glossary lies in keeping these levels separate and not mistaking an elegant conclusion for a proven fact.

## How to use this glossary

You do not have to read every entry in sequence or memorize every term. Begin with a question that genuinely interests you.

If you are wondering “Who am I?”, start with the “I,” subject, and distinction. If you are interested in freedom, read about choice, causality, and responsibility. If meaning interests you, read about purpose, value, and endings.

As you read, keep three simple questions in mind: **what exactly is being distinguished? What additional foundation is being quietly assumed? Does the conclusion actually follow from the adopted definitions?**

A glossary should not replace a living question with a ready-made answer.

It should help us ask the question more precisely.
