The “I” does not need to be eliminated
Why accepting monophenomenalism requires neither changing the everyday “I” nor eliminating or deliberately preserving it.
Essay
2026-11-12

Monophenomenalism does not require eliminating the “I,” weakening it, dissolving it, or replacing it with some supposedly more correct way of experiencing. Nor does it require the opposite: keeping the “I” unchanged. The question is not what the “I” should become, but why it should be treated as an independent foundation of what exists in the first place.

## Not a foundation does not mean a mistake

The “I” is one of the most familiar and useful distinctions. It is bound up with a name, memory, a body, actions, plans, and relationships with other people. We say, “I remember,” “I decided,” “I am in pain,” or “I am responsible for this.” There is nothing in such expressions that monophenomenalism requires us to abandon.

But the everyday need to speak and act in the first person is not the same as a need to recognize a separate “I” as a precondition for experiencing to exist. We can distinguish ourselves from another person, our own decision from someone else’s action, without turning that distinction into two independent foundations of being.

Monophenomenalism begins with the statement: “Everything that exists is a phenomenon. The phenomenon is one.” The Model of the Phenomenon is one possible tool for inquiry within this position. It introduces additional definitions: the phenomenon is sensory experiencing, and a distinction within sensory experiencing is a concept. Here a concept is not necessarily a verbal thought, still less an error. It is distinction itself, through which “I,” “another,” “body,” “thought,” “observer,” and much else can be distinguished within experience.

That the “I” can be distinguished in experience does not mean it is invented or ought to disappear. Nor does it mean there must be another owner of all experiencing, independent of the phenomenon, hidden behind it. A distinction needs no such additional foundation in order to remain meaningful.

## The trap of elimination

It is easy to take an unnoticed step here: if the “I” is not an independent first principle, then we must stop experiencing it. We must stop saying “I” inwardly, stop feeling like the center of events, and attain a state without an observer.

But this turns philosophical understanding into a project of changing experience. A new condition appears: as long as the “I” is felt, something is supposedly unfinished. Experiencing is then monitored for signs that it is sufficiently free of the “I.” Yet the one doing the checking, the wish to change oneself, and the assessment of the result are themselves part of what is experienced.

Monophenomenalism prescribes no such work. A weaker sense of “I” does not prove its starting proposition, and the return of that sense in full force does not refute it. An unusual state may prompt inquiry, but it does not, by itself, settle a question of philosophical truth.

## Neither eliminate nor hold unchanged

How someone experiences themselves can vary. At times the “I” stands out as a clear center of events; at others, a person is absorbed in an activity and scarcely thinks about themselves. Sometimes a sense of being an observer arises; sometimes the usual point of reference loses its definition. All this can be investigated. But none of these states needs to be granted the privilege of being the final truth about how experiencing ought to be.

Allowing the “I” a place in experience does not mean committing to keep it unchanged. Monophenomenalism does not demand that the everyday “I” be changed or eliminated. It may remain, change, recede from attention, or reappear—without any special philosophical instruction.

What changes, above all, is its status within an explanation: the “I” is not taken as an independent first principle. This is not an instruction to weaken the sense of self. Someone can still experience themselves distinctly as a person without treating that experience as proof of a separate owner of the phenomenon.

Nor must the “I” be relocated to a new point of support: “my true self is the observer,” “I am all experiencing,” or “I am the phenomenon itself.” If such words once again assume an unchanging owner or container of what happens, the old premise has simply acquired a new name. The unity of the phenomenon does not mean that the personal “I” has expanded to encompass everything that exists.

This also applies to understanding itself. We can discuss the phenomenon while using the word “I.” We can notice changes in our sense of self. We may also notice no such changes at all. Adopting the starting position does not oblige anyone to experience themselves differently.

## Everyday life remains everyday life

In ordinary life, the “I” serves practical purposes. People recognize their homes, remember promises, make agreements, care for loved ones, and take responsibility for the consequences of their decisions. Distinctions between people, their interests, and their actions do not become dispensable just because they are not taken as independent first principles.

This also matters when someone genuinely wants to change something about themselves: a habit, an attitude, a behavior, or a way of dealing with pain. Such changes can have understandable causes and consequences. But they do not require first declaring the very experiencing of “I” to be a philosophical error. Nor does recognizing everything as a phenomenon provide a reason to refuse help or abandon effort.

Declining to treat the “I” as an independent owner of the phenomenon does not turn a person into a helpless spectator. Decisions, actions, and seeking help are also part of what happens; they do not need to occur outside it to affect subsequent events. “Everything is a phenomenon” does not mean “my actions make no difference.”

The absence of a mandatory transformation of the “I” does not prohibit change. A goal of changing a habit or learning something does not contradict the position. It simply does not follow from the position that any particular state of the “I” must be attained in order to accept monophenomenalism or justify one's existence.

## Freedom without a prerequisite

The same applies to absolute freedom. If it is treated as a reward for the disappearance of the “I,” one must first achieve the correct state and then maintain it. Freedom would depend on succeeding at a special practice.

On this reading, absolute freedom is neither a special feeling nor a new property attributed to the phenomenon. It means there is no mandatory task a person must complete to justify existence itself. No change in experiencing is required for this. Such freedom does not mean human omnipotence, the abolition of circumstances, or freedom from consequences, and it does not promise uninterrupted calm. Wanting to change something, being unable to change it now, acting, doubting, and even searching for a point of support remain within what exists.

What matters most is that the very fact of experiencing oneself does not need to be corrected. The “I” need not be eliminated for the phenomenon to be one. One need not stop experiencing oneself as a person for a distinction to remain a distinction. One need not make the observer disappear in order not to treat it as an independent first principle. Nor must this understanding become a new demand: “Now I must stop searching and changing.”

The “I” may remain in experience. It needs neither justification nor elimination.

Accepting monophenomenalism does not require you to stop being yourself.
