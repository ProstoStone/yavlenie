Nihilism without a verdict
Why the absence of ultimate meaning does not mean that existence lacks something.
Essay
2026-11-04

Imagine someone who suddenly no longer understands what they are living for.

Only yesterday, things seemed reasonably clear. They needed to finish a project, earn recognition, secure their future, become a better person, find love, or leave something significant behind.

They did not necessarily believe in God or a grand destiny. An entirely ordinary life, too, may be organized around a promise: what is happening matters because it leads somewhere.

But one day the question “What for?” stopped receiving a convincing answer.

## When the answer to “What for?” disappears

Why pursue success if everything will eventually end?

Why create anything if what is created will disappear?

Why become better if there is no final measure by which one could determine that one has finally become what one ought to be?

And if, after death, there is no one left for whom any of it mattered, does life not turn out to be meaningless?

There is more than doubt in these questions. A particular understanding of existence is already at work.

Life is assumed to be going somewhere. What happens is supposed to produce a result capable of justifying it as a whole. Without that result, actions supposedly lose their significance and existence becomes futile.

When the expected goal disappears, a person discovers more than the absence of an answer.

They discover the absence of an answer they thought **had to exist**.

A negative conclusion follows.

Life is meaningless.

But before accepting it, we should ask:

**What exactly has been discovered—the absence of meaning, or the world's failure to meet our demand that it have meaning?**

These are not the same question.

## Nihilism is not a single doctrine

The word “nihilism” covers different philosophical positions.

One can deny objective morality, doubt the possibility of knowledge, reject the existence of independent values, or maintain that human life has no ultimate purpose.

These positions are related, but they are not identical.

Nor does nihilism necessarily entail despair. Someone may believe that life has no objectively assigned purpose and remain at peace with that. They may enjoy art, relationships, and inquiry without treating them as parts of a cosmic design.

It would therefore be a mistake to begin with the formula “nihilism is the conviction that everything is bad.”

This essay concerns a more specific variant: **existential nihilism insofar as the absence of ultimate meaning becomes a ground for a negative assessment of existence**.

Its reasoning might be expressed as follows:

> If existence has no objective ultimate purpose, then it is ultimately meaningless; and if it is meaningless, everything we do lacks final justification.

Not every nihilist would agree with this formulation. That matters. We are not examining every philosophy called nihilism, but a particular transition from the absence of an ultimate goal to the experience of meaninglessness.

Within that transition may lie an assumption that makes the negative conclusion unnecessary.

## An absence is not yet a deficiency

Imagine a room without a chair.

By itself, this does not mean that something is missing from the room.

If it is a dance studio, the absence of a chair may be perfectly appropriate. If someone enters hoping to sit down, they will find it inconvenient. If someone had promised to provide a chair, its absence becomes a broken promise.

The room has not changed. Nor has the absence.

But a deficiency arises only in a particular relation to an expectation, need, or intended use.

We may say, “There is no chair here.”

To say, “There ought to be a chair here, and there isn't,” we must add a criterion.

Now return to existence.

The proposition “No mandatory ultimate purpose of life has been discovered” does not yet contain the proposition “Life is deficient because it lacks a mandatory ultimate purpose.”

Between them stands an additional assumption:

**For existence to be sufficient, it must have an ultimate purpose.**

This is what turns an absence into a deficiency.

If the assumption goes unexamined, a person may mistake their own requirement for a property of the world.

First, an ultimate goal is appointed a necessary condition of existence. Then none is found. Finally, existence is declared inadequate.

But what does the absence of an answer prove when the question was assigned obligatory status in advance?

It may show that the requirement has not been met.

It does not yet show that the requirement was justified.

Here lies the first possibility of moving beyond negative nihilism.

There is no need to rush to invent a new meaning to fill the supposed void.

**We should first ask why the void was treated as damage at all.**

## The logic of the negative conclusion

To see the problem more clearly, let us separate the argument into propositions.

First: a human life has meaning only if there is an objective ultimate purpose it ought to fulfill.

Second: suppose no such purpose exists.

Third: life then lacks meaning **only in this deliberately narrow sense of the word**.

But if an ultimate purpose has not been disproven, only not established, the conclusion is different: **we have no grounds for claiming that this kind of meaning exists**. Not finding a purpose does not prove its absence.

Neither case establishes that life is bad, that it cannot be loved, or that there is nothing in it to enjoy.

But the decisive issue concerns the first.

**Why must an objective ultimate purpose be a necessary condition of meaning?**

And even if we accept the third proposition under a narrowly specified definition of “meaning,” another step is required.

Fourth: if life lacks that kind of meaning, this is bad; life is somehow not what it ought to be.

This reveals another requirement that nihilism may preserve unnoticed after dismantling the old foundations.

The world ought to have meaning.

The world has none.

Therefore, the world is not what it ought to be.

But where did this “ought” come from?

If the need for a final purpose was previously derived from divine intention, objective morality, or the structure of reality, rejecting those foundations leaves the requirement in need of a new justification.

Otherwise a paradox appears.

Nihilism rejects mandatory ultimate meaning while continuing to judge the world as though the presence of such meaning were still mandatory.

It abandons the answer while preserving the authority of the question.

**Meaning disappears, but the supposed necessity of meaning remains untouched.**

That is what produces the negative verdict.

## Nietzsche had already noticed this trap

It would be mistaken to attribute the argument just described directly to Nietzsche, as though we had discovered an objection he had never considered.

Nietzsche was among the most penetrating investigators of this crisis.

In his late notes on European nihilism, the devaluation of the highest values is connected with the failure of inherited categories of purpose, unity, and truth to perform the work assigned to them. The world appears to have lost value because it can no longer be measured by criteria once regarded as unconditional.

What matters here is not simply the content of the lost values, but the manner in which they were used.

A person first tries to measure everything that exists by some ultimate standard.

Then they discover that the standard does not fit.

And they conclude that the failure of the standard proves the failure of what is being measured.

But why?

**Perhaps what has been exposed is not the worthlessness of the world, but the inadequacy of the demand to evaluate the entire world in this way.**

Nietzsche himself comes remarkably close to this conclusion.

He does not simply mourn the loss of meaning. He examines the origins of the evaluations through which the world came to seem devalued. Nor can his positive philosophical project be reduced to a complaint that higher foundations are missing.

Our comparison should therefore not portray monophenomenalism as rescuing us from a philosophical error Nietzsche failed to notice.

The difference, if there is one, appears in the next step.

Nietzsche can ask about the revaluation of values, the affirmation of life, and forms of existence capable of enduring the loss of inherited foundations.

But another possibility can also be explored:

**What if the phenomenon does not even require a new system of values capable of affirming it once and for all?**

This is no objection to human creativity or evaluation.

It asks why evaluation should be the ultimate condition of existence in the first place.

*A note on sources:* Nietzsche's notes published after his death under the title *The Will to Power* do not constitute a completed book that he himself prepared for publication. They should not be read uncritically as his definitive system. Here they serve as evidence of his reflections on nihilism, not as a finished philosophical treatise.

## What do we mean by meaning?

The word “meaning” appears unified until we examine the different situations in which we use it.

When someone asks about the meaning of a sentence, they want to know what it says.

When they ask about the meaning of an action, they may be asking about its motive or intended result.

When they say a meeting was meaningful, they are not necessarily claiming that it fulfilled an objective purpose of the universe.

Yet when they ask about the meaning of life, these distinctions can become entangled.

Signification, purpose, value, and goal begin to sound like different names for a single absolute foundation.

But they are not the same.

A written text can have significance.

An action can have a cause and an intended result.

A relationship can have value for the people involved in it.

A project can have a goal.

None of this establishes that existence as a whole must have a single function comparable to the function of a tool.

A hammer is made for certain uses within human practices. It can be used successfully or unsuccessfully relative to those uses.

But to declare the entire phenomenon a tool, we would need to say who assigned it a function and by reference to what result it ought to be judged.

If that result, too, is distinguished within the phenomenon, it does not become an external foundation for the whole phenomenon.

If the result is instead located beyond the phenomenon, an additional entity or condition is being introduced and still requires justification.

In *Model of the Phenomenon*, meaning is considered through the conceptual distinction of direction toward a result, while value is understood in relation to preference.

This does not make meaning imaginary or incapable of changing human life.

On the contrary, people live within experiences structured by purposes, intentions, evaluations, preferences, and consequences.

But **a goal distinguished within experience need not be the ultimate purpose of the entire phenomenon**.

And the absence of such a purpose does not erase the significance already distinguished within human life.

## A finite life and the end of the phenomenon are different questions

A more difficult objection now emerges.

Suppose that no ultimate purpose is mandatory.

Human beings still die.

They grow older, lose loved ones, and encounter illness. One day an individual's life comes to an end. Some plans remain unfinished; some opportunities are never taken.

Does finitude not make everything futile?

Several propositions need to be distinguished.

First: a human life has a beginning and an end within biographical, medical, and social descriptions.

Second: a person can form the idea of their own death and of the loss of everything they associate with themselves.

Third: existence as such must have a final end, after which absolutely nothing remains.

These are not identical claims.

The first has real meaning within human practice. Death does not cease to be a medical fact, a loss to loved ones, or the end of a particular biography.

The second concerns an important part of human experience: we can think about and fear the loss of ourselves.

The third, however, requires separate examination.

In *Model of the Phenomenon*, death is examined as a conceptual distinction of an ending or a loss within experience. This does not mean that a person will continue living after death, that their memories will survive, or that personal immortality is guaranteed.

The model offers no such promises.

Instead, it makes another distinction: **the end of a particular life within the phenomenon cannot simply be equated with the end of the phenomenon itself without further grounds**.

We can conceive the absence of our body, memories, thoughts, and biography.

But to conceive the absolute absence of every phenomenon is already to employ the concept of absence as a distinction present in the experience being described.

The inability to experience one's own nonexistence directly does not prove that an individual life continues forever. This argument must not be used as a disguised guarantee of immortality.

Yet it also calls the opposite haste into question: why should the finitude of a biography automatically establish the final limit of the entire phenomenon?

Monophenomenalism does not require that transition.

This differs in principle from a familiar image of the nihilistic world: a brief human life without ultimate meaning, set within a reality imagined as moving inexorably toward final disappearance.

That image is possible. It is not a necessary definition of nihilism.

But it shows how finitude, purposelessness, and negative evaluation can reinforce one another until they seem like a single proposition.

*Model of the Phenomenon* need not accept the entire construction.

## Even finitude does not prove futility

We can go further and refuse to make the distinction between a person's end and the limit of the phenomenon our only argument against nihilism.

For the sake of argument, suppose that individual life does end once and for all and never returns.

Why should futility follow from that fact?

To call something futile is to relate what happened to a goal that was supposed to be achieved.

For example, someone spends years building a bridge, but it collapses before opening. If the purpose was to provide a crossing, we may say that the work failed to achieve its intended result.

This is not merely an emotional reaction. It is a distinction between a goal and an outcome.

But when someone says, “The whole life was futile because it ended,” what criterion are they using?

Was the purpose of life never to end?

Who established that purpose?

Unless it has first been introduced, an ending is not itself proof of failure.

One might reply that anything which eventually disappears has no eternal value.

Very well.

But why must value be eternal?

That is another independent demand.

“To have no eternal value” is not the same as “to have never had value.” **Joy does not have to last forever to matter.**

“An event has ended” is not the same as “the event never took place.”

And “nothing will remain forever” is not the same as “nothing ever mattered.”

Finitude is a distinction concerning a temporal boundary.

Futility is an evaluation of what happened in relation to a goal.

Confusion begins when the boundary is mistaken for a negative evaluation, as though the end itself passed judgment on everything that preceded it.

**But an ending is not a judge.**

## Why freedom from meaning goes beyond the right to invent meaning

When someone loses faith in a ready-made destiny, they are often offered a new answer.

No one has determined the meaning of your life?

Determine it yourself.

There is no higher purpose?

Invent your own.

The universe demands nothing of you?

Create something for which you yourself will want to live.

There is genuine freedom in this response.

One can choose directions, create values, and discover new forms of activity without waiting for instructions from elsewhere.

Yet it has a limit.

**If a person must create their own meaning, the necessity of meaning has not disappeared.**

Only its presumed source has changed.

Previously, meaning was to be received from God, nature, history, or a higher order.

Now one must manufacture it oneself.

But the requirement remains: life must obtain justification through a goal.

And what if someone does not wish to create meaning?

What if they discover no grand interest?

What if they have no desire to turn every day into a work of art?

Does this mean they have again failed to meet a necessary condition of existence?

Here the monophenomenalist approach opens a different question.

It is not necessary to create meaning in order to be free from its absence.

It is possible to stop treating meaning as mandatory.

Not because meaning is harmful.

Not because goals are illusions.

And not because life must be completely without purpose.

**But because neither the presence nor the absence of a goal is an ultimate condition of the phenomenon.**

A person can create a goal and later abandon it.

They can devote their whole life to it.

They can become fascinated by something unexpected.

They can want nothing in particular.

These ways of existing differ.

Yet none needs to become the final justification for everything that happens.

## Who exactly needs to be given meaning?

When someone says, “My life must have meaning,” this is a perfectly understandable human statement.

It distinguishes a person, their life, expectations, goals, a feeling of dissatisfaction, and an idea of an adequate foundation.

There is no need to deny the person or their experience.

But consider the construction itself.

Here is a life with all its events.

Here is an image of what that life ought to be.

Here is dissatisfaction with the discrepancy.

Where is the additional, independent owner to whom this entire life must present its justification?

In *Model of the Phenomenon*, the “I” is treated as a distinction, not as an independent distinguisher.

That does not mean the person is imaginary, that they have no body, history, or relationships.

It means that describing a person need not involve positing a hidden observer existing independently of everything experienced.

And if an independent owner of experience is not required, neither is an independent tribunal before which the phenomenon as a whole must justify itself.

One can experience meaninglessness.

One can search for meaning.

One can find meaning in love, work, art, or care.

But one need not assume that behind these experiences there stands a separate, fundamental subject to whom the world owes a final answer to “What for?”

The question of meaning remains distinguishable.

**Its existence simply does not prove that the entire phenomenon is obliged to answer it.**

## A freedom that need not become a state

Now comes a temptation to say:

“I have understood that meaning does not exist and is not needed. Therefore I am finally free.”

This sentence may quietly construct another prison.

If freedom depends upon understanding, misunderstanding becomes unfreedom.

If freedom requires the absence of desire, desire becomes an obstacle.

If a free person must avoid pursuing goals, any new goal becomes a mistake.

And then one must constantly monitor oneself.

Am I too attached?

Has a new meaning appeared?

Have I begun fearing loss again?

Do I want to become someone?

The absence of goals will itself have become a goal.

But the freedom discussed here is not a psychological state without striving.

It does not require a special calm, fearlessness, or permanent clarity.

In the strict terms of *Model of the Phenomenon*, even the expression “absolute freedom” remains a concept. It does not identify a hidden substance or a special property that the phenomenon must possess.

The radical point is not the attainment of a state free from all limits, but the absence of any need to posit an independent, fundamental prisoner of the phenomenon.

Freedom therefore needs neither recognition nor consent.

A person may begin searching for a destiny again.

They may experience despair.

They may believe their life is meaningless.

They may reject monophenomenalism.

None of this places them outside the phenomenon.

**Freedom from mandatory meaning does not even require giving up meanings.**

This is no longer simply a positive variation on nihilism.

It is the possibility of requiring neither positive nor negative nihilism as a final position.

## Does this turn everything into indifference?

Here we reach the most important practical objection.

If no goal is mandatory, why care about anyone?

If existence needs no ultimate justification, why not abandon responsibility?

If meaning is a conceptual distinction, do love, pain, injustice, and human suffering become insignificant?

No.

But this answer cannot be delivered by a comforting phrase alone.

We must distinguish two propositions.

First: no value must serve as an external foundation for the phenomenon as a whole.

Second: within human experience there are no meaningful values or consequences.

The second does not follow from the first.

When a child is in pain, that pain does not disappear merely because it is not an independent foundation of the universe.

When someone needs help, the need may be entirely real in their circumstances.

When a promise is broken, its consequences may matter profoundly for trust, relationships, and subsequent decisions.

One can take another person seriously without declaring care the ultimate law of the universe.

One can distinguish cruelty from help without treating them as eternal properties of the phenomenon as a whole.

One can acknowledge human responsibility without assuming an absolute owner of everything that happens.

That is precisely why rejecting mandatory ultimate meaning does not require the abandonment of ordinary human meanings.

Indeed, it does not prohibit assigning those meanings enormous importance.

But enormous importance is still not the same as metaphysical necessity.

This marks the boundary between freedom from final justification and indifference toward particular lives.

**The phenomenon requires no external goal. A person within experience may very well need water, medicine, support, or an answer to a specific question.**

One does not cancel the other.

## Is this merely a change in the meaning of “meaning”?

Imagine a critic saying:

“You have refuted nothing. You have simply changed what the word ‘meaning’ means. The nihilist claims there is no objective ultimate meaning. You agree, and then suggest we should not regard that as a problem.”

This is a strong objection.

In one respect, it is fair.

Monophenomenalism need not refute the narrow nihilistic assertion “There is no established objective purpose for human life.”

If no such purpose has been discovered, we can acknowledge that we have no grounds for relying on it. That does not prove its impossibility.

The disagreement concerns the next step.

The absence of a goal does not entail a requirement to evaluate the phenomenon negatively.

To make that conclusion binding, one needs an argument showing that the phenomenon ought to have an ultimate purpose.

That is the argument we are asking for.

If the critic replies, “Personally, I need my life to have meaning,” this describes a real human experience that should not be dismissed.

But a person's need for meaning and the objective necessity of meaning for all existence are different claims. **You don't need to know why the universe exists to know whom you want to embrace, whom you want to help, or what you want to do today.**

If the critic instead says, “I call any life without an ultimate purpose meaningless, but I do not consider such a life bad,” the central disagreement largely disappears.

We may accept the terminology without accepting a negative verdict.

The problem arises when a definition quietly becomes an accusation.

And the proposed position must be subject to equally careful scrutiny.

**The thesis “Everything is one phenomenon” is not an empirical proof that nihilism is false.**

It is the starting philosophical commitment of monophenomenalism, further developed in *Model of the Phenomenon*.

Moving from this thesis to an analysis of meaning requires additional definitions and arguments.

It permits objections, requires tests of consistency, and must not be presented as a universal logical theorem.

The force of the comparison is not that it prohibits nihilism.

It is that it reveals an assumption behind a negative conclusion which one need not accept.

## Nihilism needs no victor

Another paradox emerges here.

Philosophy often sets out to defeat nihilism.

A foundation must be found.

The value of life must be proven.

Meaning must be restored.

People must be reassured that everything happening is not futile.

But perhaps this struggle itself preserves the authority of the very thing from which one wants to be free.

As long as existence requires justification, we must search for it.

As long as the absence of a goal counts as a deficiency, we must invent a goal.

As long as finitude is declared a defeat, we must demand immortality or some other way to conquer the end.

But what if none of this is necessary?

Not because a final answer has been found.

Not because death has been abolished.

Not because suffering has disappeared.

And not because the world secretly turns out to have been arranged for our happiness.

But because the demand for ultimate justification is not a mandatory condition of the phenomenon.

In this sense, nihilism need not be defeated.

We can see that its negative verdict depended on an assumption it had not established.

There is then no need to appoint a new victor.

We cannot conclude that the world has finally acquired ultimate meaning.

Nor need we conclude the opposite: that the absence of meaning is its ultimate truth, defining everything that exists.

We can simply stop demanding that the entire world be an answer to a question which is itself distinguished within the world.

**Nihilism searched for meaning and found its absence. But the absence of meaning does not have to become the meaning of absence.**

## A world that owes no justification

Imagine an evening.

Someone leaves their home and walks along a street. The air is cold. Lights shine in the windows. A bus passes, voices can be heard, and leaves move in the wind.

They are not obliged to find beauty in it.

They are not obliged to be mindful of the present moment.

They are not obliged to feel grateful.

They are not obliged to turn the walk into an exercise in inner liberation.

They may think about work.

They may remember someone.

They may stop beside a tree or sit on a bench.

They may feel nothing particularly remarkable.

And what happens need not become evidence for any philosophy.

Once, this person might have searched for an answer in such a walk.

Perhaps they wanted to know their life was not futile. They wanted to see enough, accomplish enough, leave enough behind. They wanted time to justify itself for passing.

But now a different question is possible.

Why must a walk justify time?

Why must a tree evoke eternity?

Why must an evening become a memory that can be preserved?

Why must the person derive even pleasure from what is happening?

They may experience pleasure.

They may strive for something.

They may make this evening the beginning of a new story.

But none of these possibilities is a necessary condition of the evening itself.

**What happens need not become something else.**

This is no demand to renounce the future.

Futures, goals, dreams, and fears remain distinguishable within experience.

The point is that the phenomenon needs no separate future that must eventually justify what is.

We can recall the nihilistic question once more:

“What if everything ends, and nothing remains?”

For an individual human life, this question can be painfully concrete. Monophenomenalism promises no personal immortality and does not erase the fact of loss.

But the presumed limit of a biography does not become the limit of the entire phenomenon without further grounds.

More importantly, even a finite life need not produce an eternal result in order to avoid violating some supreme requirement.

Nothing must be invented to replace lost meaning.

Nothing must be taken on faith in order to escape nihilism.

There is no need to become someone who never again asks “What for?”

The question may return.

Desires, fears, plans, and disappointments may arise.

None of this places what happens outside what is.

Nihilism becomes a verdict when the absence of mandatory meaning is treated as failure to meet a mandatory demand for meaning.

But if the demand itself has not been established, the verdict lacks a foundation.

Another conclusion becomes possible.

Not “Life is meaningless, therefore everything is futile.”

And not “Life is beautiful because I have found it a new meaning.”

But something far more radical:

**Life requires no ultimate meaning in order to be life. We can love, create, rest, and rejoice without having to justify existence as a whole through any of them.**

We may give meaning to anything.

We may fail to find it.

We may search.

We may stop searching.

And none of this determines whether the phenomenon will be what it is.

The absence of a final foundation does not turn existence into a fall.

It allows us to see that **the necessity of a foothold was never established**.

And if there is no need to hold on, perhaps there is no need to fall.
