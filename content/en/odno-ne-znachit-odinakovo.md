One doesn't mean the same
Why one phenomenon doesn't turn people, feelings, and things into identical copies.
Essay
2026-11-03

It's raining in the city. At a bus stop, someone slips their phone inside their jacket. Someone else is laughing. Another person watches the road in silence. Across the street, a customer holds a warm cup of coffee. A bus slows at the traffic lights. For one person, it's an ordinary Tuesday. For another, it's a day they've been waiting for. For someone else, it's the day bad news arrives.

Everything is different. Which is why the claim that “everything is one phenomenon” can sound so strange. Are rain, laughter, loss, and joy supposed to be the same thing? Are the people at the bus stop really one person looking at themselves from different angles?

No. That picture gets in the way of what monophenomenalism is proposing.

## One is not another thing

We're used to counting objects. Three cups on a table. Two people in a room. Ten cars on a street. When someone says all of this is “one,” it's natural to imagine one enormous thing containing everything else.

But in monophenomenalism, unity doesn't mean there's a giant object somewhere, a universal mind, or a hidden observer watching everything happen. It isn't a proposal to replace lots of small entities with one very large entity.

The starting point is different: everything that exists is considered as one phenomenon, without first introducing an independent foundation beyond it. This is a philosophical position, not a result of counting objects and not something proved merely by noticing that we experience things.

Differences don't disappear. Without them, there would be nothing to describe.

## Different things remain different

One person hears laughter; another reads an upsetting message. Hearing laughter is not the same as reading a message. Tiredness doesn't turn into joy. A hot cup doesn't become cold. And calling everything one phenomenon doesn't make someone else's pain your own.

We can still distinguish people, their histories, relationships, memories, possibilities, and actions. There's no need to claim that everyone experiences the same thing, that anyone can read everyone else's thoughts, or that helping someone is no different from hurting them.

Unity here isn't the claim that everything is identical. It's the refusal to assume, at the outset, that these distinctions require separate fundamental worlds or independent owners of experiencing.

In everyday life, that may sound like a tiny adjustment. In philosophy, it changes the question. Instead of searching for an invisible second foundation that somehow connects an experiencer to what is experienced, we can examine the distinctions themselves, as they are available to us.

## No scissors, no assembly line

*Model of the Phenomenon* sets out five theses that consider experiencing, distinctions, concepts, and experience together. In its specific terminology, a concept is a system of distinctions. It isn't necessarily a word or a conscious thought.

It's tempting to picture a colorless material existing first, a mind cutting it into pieces, and a world assembled afterward. But that picture brings in a separate maker, separate tools, and a sequence of construction. The model requires none of these.

The red you see isn't produced by saying the word “red.” Pain doesn't wait for someone to explain it before it hurts. A distinction isn't a knife, and a concept isn't a worker turning raw material into experience.

Even when we speak of “parts,” “wholes,” and “boundaries,” those words need not refer to independent cuts in the phenomenon itself. We can use them without turning a way of describing things into the workings of a hidden machine.

## And life goes on

The person at the bus stop can still miss the bus. Someone in the café can still burn their hand. Two people can argue, make up, or walk past each other without ever meeting. Differences don't become stage scenery, and the consequences of actions don't vanish.

One phenomenon doesn't mean that another person's suffering stops mattering. Nor does it require treating people as characters who needn't be taken seriously. Examining the idea of a separate owner of experience doesn't erase people or our concern for them.

That's what's unusual about this perspective: it doesn't ask us to remove anything from life before calling it one. We don't need to erase colors, feelings, or faces. We don't need to make the world calmer, prettier, or simpler than it is experienced.

It's still raining. The bus pulls in, its doors open, some people get on, and others stay under the shelter. All of it can be distinguished. None of it has to be identical.

**One phenomenon doesn't mean a world without differences. It offers a way to consider differences without turning each of them into a separate ultimate foundation.**
