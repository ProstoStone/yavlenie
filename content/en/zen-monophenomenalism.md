Zen and Monophenomenalism: A Study and Philosophical Comparison
Awakening, no-thought, koans, and practice: Zen on its own terms and in comparison with the Model of the Phenomenon.

Zen opens a challenging conversation about awakening, no-thought, practice, and ordinary life. Monophenomenalism begins with a different assertion: “Everything that exists is the phenomenon. The phenomenon is one.” Can these questions be considered together without turning one philosophy into a translation of the other?

The study therefore has two parts. The first examines Chan and Zen on their own Buddhist foundations: their history, texts, practice, and internal disagreements. The second independently introduces Roman Dudnikov’s monophenomenalism and Model of the Phenomenon before developing a comparison. Similarity between expressions is not taken as proof that their meanings are identical.

## Part I. Zen (Chan): awakening, practice, and everyday life

### Introduction. The subject of the study

Zen is often presented through a few familiar images: a monk sitting silently; a teacher answering a question with an unexpected phrase; a koan that admits no ordinary logical solution; someone “simply living in the present.” None of these images is entirely false. Together, however, they can obscure the main point: Zen is neither an isolated calming technique nor a teaching that thinking is harmful. It is a historically changing Buddhist tradition with its own texts, transmission lineages, rules, disputes, practices, and philosophical questions.

To understand Zen on its own terms, consider first its traditional texts, then the history of their formation and institutions, and subsequently the philosophical questions. A historically established event, a later traditional narrative, a normative instruction, and a modern philosophical interpretation will not be treated as the same thing.

The central tension running through this study is: **if awakening is not the acquisition of a new entity, why is practice given such importance?** The different voices of Chan and Zen do not give one answer. Each deserves to be heard without its differences being erased in advance. [[1](#ref-1), [2](#ref-2)]

### 1. Chan, Zen, Seon, and Thien: one family, many forms

Chinese *chan* (禪, *chán*) derives from Sanskrit *dhyana*, meaning meditative concentration. Japanese *zen*, Korean *seon*, and Vietnamese *thiền* name related traditions that developed through the spread of Chinese Buddhism. These words do not name a single unchanging doctrine repeated identically by every monastery in East Asia.

Chan took shape in China from the early medieval period onward, interacting with Buddhist schools, Chinese linguistic and cultural forms, monastic organisations, and local ways of discussing the path. Japanese Zen later developed new disciplines, forms of transmission, and philosophical formulations. Korean and Vietnamese traditions should likewise not be reduced to local copies of Japanese experience. [[1](#ref-1)]

The converse matters too: meditation existed in Buddhism long before Chan. Meditation alone therefore does not explain Chan’s distinctiveness. Ways of speaking about awakening, exchanges between teachers and students, Dharma transmission, and the understanding of everyday conduct as a site of practice all became important.

### 2. Zen remains Mahayana Buddhism

Chan grows out of Mahayana Buddhism; it is not a separate religion that suddenly declared every Buddhist teaching unnecessary. Behind its conversations and exercises stand the Four Noble Truths, not-self, conditionality, emptiness, wisdom, bodhisattva vows, and compassion. This does not mean that every teacher recites the whole set of doctrines on every occasion. A concise Chan response can make sense precisely because its listener already inhabits the language, practice, and discipline of Buddhism.

The familiar formula about a “special transmission outside the scriptures” and “not relying on words and letters” was important to Chan’s traditional self-description, especially in developed genealogies and during the Song period. It cannot be declared either the unquestionable words of Bodhidharma or an ancient rule operating identically in every Chan community. The formula’s historical emergence and circulation are more complex than its traditional attribution. Alongside it existed sutra reading, commentaries, public instruction, recorded sayings, ritual life, and monastic regulations. The image of being “outside the scriptures” was itself transmitted within an extensive written tradition. [[1](#ref-1), [10](#ref-10)]

The formula’s substantive meaning is better sought in the distinction between knowing about the Way and realising the Way. Someone can recount a sutra faultlessly while using it for self-assertion, competition for status, or the maintenance of familiar attachments. Denying the sufficiency of book learning is nevertheless not the same as denying the significance of texts. Chan history shows that dialogue, commentary, imagery, and verse can participate in Buddhist education.

The language of Buddha-nature is connected with this. Buddhist texts sometimes speak positively of awakening as present or available. But these expressions do not automatically turn Mahayana emptiness into a doctrine of an eternal universal Mind. Their meanings must be examined within different schools, texts, and practices rather than through one convenient modern definition.

### 3. Bodhidharma: early tradition and the question of sources

In traditional genealogy, Bodhidharma appears as the first Chinese patriarch, continuing the Indian transmission from the Buddha. Stories of his exchange with the emperor, nine years facing a wall, and patriarchal succession profoundly influenced Zen’s self-understanding. They deserve study as religious narratives, but need not be reliable records of early history. [[1](#ref-1), [3](#ref-3)]

The corpus associated with Bodhidharma includes the Treatise on the Two Entrances and Four Practices (二入四行論). Entrance through principle is connected with understanding the Dharma; entrance through practice concerns a particular relationship to suffering, circumstances, the desire for reward, and action. The text is especially interesting because it places a way of life alongside insight: intellectual and spiritual understanding does not replace practical realisation.

The treatise’s authorship and proximity to the historical Bodhidharma’s teaching nevertheless remain disputed. Scholars connect it with the early Chinese Buddhist environment and debate its layers, authorship, and editing. Atsushi Ibuki, in particular, has argued against taking it as a direct record of an Indian monk’s teaching. The “two entrances” here therefore characterise an early tradition associated with Bodhidharma, rather than a historically verified personal programme of Bodhidharma himself. [[17](#ref-17)]

A substantive question follows: why does a teaching that so strongly emphasises direct understanding also value patience, nonattachment, accordance with the Dharma, and a particular way of life? Reducing all Chan history to a later slogan about “suddenness” cannot answer it.

### 4. Huineng, the Platform Sutra, and sudden awakening

Tradition presents Huineng (638–713) as the sixth patriarch. The Platform Sutra is a central Chan work, but it cannot be read as a verbatim record of Huineng’s speech that remained unchanged for centuries. Different recensions exist; the influential later version translated by John McRae for BDK belongs to a longer textual history. The story of rival heirs and verses about a mirror expresses not only doctrine but also controversy over the legitimacy of transmission. [[2](#ref-2), [4](#ref-4), [5](#ref-5)]

The opposition between a “gradual Northern school” and a “sudden Southern school” was also a historical means of constructing identity. John McRae shows why the picture of two entirely homogeneous camps is misleading. A student labelled “gradual” did not necessarily consider awakening a mechanical result of accumulated effort; an advocate of “suddenness” did not necessarily reject training, meditation, and discipline.

Sudden awakening points to the qualitative character of insight: what previously seemed an object to be sought can be seen differently rather than acquired like a new thing. Yet the path leading to it and life afterward remain questions. If previous ignorance has been removed, why do habitual reactions arise? If wisdom is already available, what does learning mean? The Platform Sutra offers not an abstract cancellation of the path but a distinctive way of bringing wisdom, concentration, and practice together in action. [[2](#ref-2), [5](#ref-5)]

This answer should be read as the text’s position, rather than assumed in advance to be a philosophical theory of the structure of all experiencing. Comparison with another philosophy becomes appropriate only after this independent reconstruction.

### 5. No-thought, no-form, and nonabiding: their positive content

In the Platform Sutra, “no-thought” (無念, *wunian*), “no-form,” and “nonabiding” are not three demands to destroy parts of human life. They are interconnected directions of Buddhist training. The familiar explanation of no-thought allows thoughts to arise while insisting on not being caught by what is encountered. The absence of thought is not dullness or the suspension of conscious activity. Conversely, a stream of reasoning does not by itself obstruct practice; what matters is how it is held and what is given authority. [[2](#ref-2), [5](#ref-5)]

No-form does not declare the world of objects an empty stage set. Forms are seen and distinguished, but are not held as self-sufficient supports compelling appropriation, rejection, or constant defence of a familiar position. Nonabiding addresses this habit of fixation: an event arises, and it can be cared about and remembered, without necessarily being made an unchanging foundation of personal worth, fear, or desire.

Imagine a conversation in which someone hears an unpleasant remark. They understand the words, can assess whether they are true, and can respond to the substance. Yet between hearing and responding, the entire construction “I have been defined forever,” “I must win,” or “otherwise everything will collapse” need not arise. This is a contemporary illustration of a possible meaning of nonabiding, not an instruction or a literal quotation from Huineng. It shows why the issue is neither amnesia, insensitivity, nor a prohibition on thinking, but openness to what happens without an already fixed entanglement.

This goes beyond the popular psychological formula “do not identify with your thoughts.” For Huineng, the stakes also concern perceived conditions, cognition and action, and the very understanding of wisdom and concentration. Meditative practice does not remove someone from the everyday world: it must remain effective in encountering that world.

A substantial philosophical question remains: how can thought useful for action be distinguished from clinging when both appear within the same mental activity? The tradition answers not only through definitions but through examination in conduct and practice. No-thought therefore has cognitive, practical, and ethical dimensions; reducing it to the removal of a single self-image would be too narrow.

### 6. Sudden awakening and sustained practice

“Sudden” primarily concerns the character of insight, not necessarily the biography of someone without preparation. A person may read, sit, and observe discipline for decades before suddenly seeing something not reducible to the sum of their training steps. Sudden awakening can also be followed by a deepening transformation of conduct. These possibilities appear in different combinations rather than belonging to two sealed camps. [[2](#ref-2)]

The strongest question is philosophical rather than chronological: not “How many minutes until awakening?” but “What can be prepared if the content of awakening is not manufactured through training like an external object?” Some Chan lineages describe practice as the expression of an already available Buddha-nature; others emphasise the transformation of deluded habits and the force of a decisive breakthrough.

“No need to produce enlightenment as a thing” must be distinguished from “every action is already wise.” The second does not follow from the first. Awakening and its expression in conduct are not always treated as simple synonyms within the tradition.

### 7. Mazu: “mind is Buddha” and “neither mind nor Buddha”

Mazu Daoyi (709–788) is a central figure in Hongzhou Chan. His teachings survive through recorded-sayings traditions and later literary treatments. The short expressions attributed to him must therefore be examined both as doctrinal statements and as actions within particular teaching encounters. [[6](#ref-6)]

“Mind is Buddha” can address a student seeking absolute truth as something remote, external, and unattainable. Here the Buddhist nature of awakening concerns the student’s actual life and knowing, rather than an alien object. But when “mind” itself becomes a fixed idol, the further response “neither mind nor Buddha” cuts off a new attachment. This does not mean that any mutually exclusive formulas are equally true: their work depends on the direction of the particular instruction.

Mario Poceski shows that the Hongzhou school was not a gathering of spontaneous rejecters of Buddhism. Its rhetoric developed against a background of monastic culture, Mahayana texts, and long-standing debates about mind, Buddha-nature, and awakening. In this setting, “mind is Buddha” is not a ready-made doctrine of cosmic substance; “neither mind nor Buddha” does not dismiss awakening’s significance. [[6](#ref-6)]

The use of affirmation and negation is especially important. Words can open understanding and subsequently become an obstacle when repeated as a final formula. A later instruction need not correct an earlier one as false; it may address a different attachment. Yet this contextuality itself requires careful interpretation, or it becomes a simple excuse for anything a teacher says.

### 8. “Ordinary mind is the Way”: not seeking the extraordinary elsewhere

“Ordinary mind is the Way” appears in the tradition associated with Mazu and in the famous exchange between Nanquan and Zhaozhou, case 19 of the Wumenguan. Without specific textual investigation, these occurrences cannot be reduced to one original author and one historical remark. Their lines of meaning are nevertheless comparable: the Way is not described as an exotic object reached by leaving ordinary life behind. [[6](#ref-6), [8](#ref-8)]

For Mazu, “ordinary” is connected with abandoning the attempt to manufacture a special state through a contrived attitude. In Nanquan’s exchange, deliberately trying to approach the Way can itself become a departure from it. This does not mean that hunger, anger, envy, care, and compassion always express wisdom equally. The Buddhist context requires attention to the quality of action, harm caused, and attachment.

Someone making soup, getting a child ready for school, or answering a difficult question need not first move into a sacred realm. This does not make every way of cooking or communicating right. “Ordinary mind” invites a relinquishment of the assumption that awakening must take an exclusively foreign and exceptional form; it does not declare arbitrary conduct sufficient.

The instruction’s force lies in this combination of ordinariness and demandingness. Rather than occupying a separate sacred space, the path concerns how one eats, speaks, hears, works, and responds. The difficulty is not proving that ordinary activities are “spiritual,” but understanding which attachments turn them into blind repetition.

### 9. Buddha-nature: possession, realisation, and existence

The terminology of Buddha-nature encompasses historically connected but not fully coincident expressions: Sanskrit *buddha-dhatu* (Buddha element or realm), *tathagatagarbha*, and East Asian *foxing* and *bussho*. In particular contexts they name related ideas; their meanings, metaphors, and doctrinal functions nevertheless change between texts. [[16](#ref-16), [19](#ref-19)]

One function of positive Buddha-nature language is to affirm the possibility of awakening: a person need not imagine the Way as the creation of an entirely alien entity. Buddhist schools nevertheless reconcile this language with anatta and emptiness differently. Some interpretations emphasise potential or the manner of actual awakening; others speak much more confidently of a deep, present reality. Buddha-nature debates concern not only human beings but also insentient things and all dharmas, particularly in Tiantai and some other East Asian discussions. No single formula is binding on all Buddhism.

Dogen makes the question more radical than simply “Does a being have Buddha-nature?” In the Bussho fascicle, he works with the traditional statement that all sentient beings possess Buddha-nature and reinterprets it: they not only *have* it; on a particular reading, “all existence” itself *is* Buddha-nature. This reorients the relationship between bearer and quality rather than merely expanding the list of beings assigned another property. Rein Raud’s research and translations of the fascicle emphasise this work with language, existence, and manifestation. [[16](#ref-16)]

Two opposite mistakes are easy here. One turns Buddha-nature into an eternal thing secretly belonging to everyone. The other translates this unusual text in advance into an abstract modern ontology, forgetting that Dogen is guiding monastic students along the Buddhist Way. His concerns include realisation, practice, and impermanence; Buddha-nature cannot be reduced to the psychological self-appraisal “everything is already inside me.”

A substantive problem emerges: can awakening be spoken of as original without making it a hidden substance, and what place then remains for suffering, ignorance, learning, and action? This is not a contradiction resolved by one beautiful phrase. Different sources work through it differently.

### 10. Gong’an and koan: case, interpretation, and exercise

Chinese *gong’an* (公案), Japanese *koan*, is commonly translated as “public case.” Its history involves records of teachers’ conversations and deeds, commentaries, verses, and monastic practice. A koan is not simply an illogical phrase that creates confusion. It is a complex way of working with a Buddhist case: doctrinal interpretation, literary work, model of an appropriate response, and material for the discipline of attention can all be involved. [[7](#ref-7)]

Robert Sharf draws attention to koans as part of a developed culture of interpretation and public engagement with precedents, rather than merely a method for destroying a student’s logical capacity. A response sometimes shifts the question’s familiar framing. But it can also rearrange emphases within a Buddhist topic, invoke other stories, and require understanding of the tradition’s technical language. “The koan destroys the question” is therefore only one possible description of its effect.

At least three dimensions can be distinguished. As a historical and literary document, a koan calls for dating, textual comparison, and interpretation. As religious instruction, it offers a direction of Buddhist understanding. As an exercise, it can become the focus of concentrated practice whose meaning exceeds a final philological account. These dimensions need neither be conflated nor declared false because one does not perform another’s functions.

### 11. Zhaozhou’s dog and “mu”: question, negation, and work with a phrase

In the first case of the Wumenguan (無門關, The Gateless Barrier, 1228), a monk asks Zhaozhou whether a dog has Buddha-nature and receives the answer 無: *wu* in Chinese, *mu* in Japanese. In ordinary language it carries a negative meaning, but the koan’s subsequent history cannot be reduced either to a grammar lesson or to an abstract paradox. The case relies on the wider Buddhist context of Buddha-nature while confronting it with a concrete question. [[7](#ref-7), [8](#ref-8), [10](#ref-10)]

Other versions of Zhaozhou’s exchange are known, including an affirmative answer. They show that the later koan results from selection, editing, and specialised use of an earlier case, rather than providing a transparent window onto a single historically established episode. In the Wumenguan, Wumen makes this particular “mu” the central barrier with which practitioners must engage with their whole being. A single negation becomes an exercise, not a ready-made thesis that dogs lack Buddha-nature.

It would be too easy to say that “mu” merely cancels the yes/no pair. Such a description has already decided the koan’s meaning. In traditional practice, persistence of questioning, the way the word is used, and the distinction between a genuine response and a memorised commentary matter. This is more layered than a logical puzzle about the compatibility of two statements.

The koan itself does not license abandoning logic everywhere. Historians can check manuscripts, translators the meaning of 無, and philosophers the consistency of an interpretation. Practice requires another kind of participation, but does not prove every arbitrary phrase.

### 12. Paradox, language, and verification

Chan texts include responses that appear contradictory when treated as reports of physical facts. Their task may be to disrupt an expected conversational script: the student anticipates a certain kind of answer, while the teacher reveals the limitations of the question itself. Such a pedagogical move does not make every contradiction equally true.

Three questions must be distinguished: did the teacher historically say this; what does the utterance do within its genre and situation; and is a possible philosophical interpretation of it true? Neither the first nor the second establishes the third without an argument. This boundary matters especially for modern readers drawn to striking sayings detached from their original context.

Rigorous examination is not opposed to respect for Chan. On the contrary, a tradition that developed complex commentaries, disputes, and training criteria cannot respectfully be described as rejecting every meaningful distinction between successful and unsuccessful understanding.

### 13. Dahui and investigating the key phrase

In Song Chan, Dahui Zonggao (1089–1163) developed *kanhua Chan*: work with a case’s key phrase, or *huatou*. This practice holds a question not to collect explanations but to break through habitual ways of reasoning and clinging to solutions. Dahui criticised what he saw as a dangerous fascination with inactive tranquillity. [[10](#ref-10)]

His polemical portrayal of opponents should not, however, be taken automatically as an accurate historical description of all their meditation. Dahui participated in particular disputes about training, authority, and awakening. “Silence” and “illumination” can mean something quite different from the passivity he opposed.

A real tension can be identified here: when does attentive unhurriedness disclose wisdom, and when does it become a habit of avoiding difficult questions? Both approaches acknowledge this question, although they answer it and describe the effort required differently.

### 14. Hongzhi and silent illumination

Hongzhi Zhengjue (1091–1157) is associated with the Caodong lineage and *mozhao*, “silent illumination.” Reading Dahui’s criticism alone might suggest an immobile, self-hypnotic condition in which thought and attention are nearly absent. Morten Schlütter’s research shows how much this picture owes to polemics between lineages. [[10](#ref-10)]

“Silent” need not mean impoverished, and “illumination” need not mean a sudden effect acquired after prolonged immobility. The expression can concern a clear, attentive presence that does not split attention from what happens through a rigid intention to obtain a result. This does not cancel historical methods of training and monastic exercises.

The difference from kanhua should be described as a difference in pedagogical emphasis and the schools’ self-understanding, rather than a choice between thinking and not thinking. Both are concerned with liberating understanding and with avoiding the transformation of a method into a new attachment.

### 15. Japanese Zen: Soto and Rinzai

Japanese Soto and Rinzai are often opposed as “just sitting” and “koans.” This scheme can help an initial introduction, but quickly becomes a caricature. Rinzai includes disciplined seated meditation, while Soto has a rich tradition of studying koans and commentaries. They differ in training methods, institutional histories, the teacher’s role, and emphases in understanding practice. [[11](#ref-11)]

Japanese Zen does not merely reproduce Chinese Chan. Its historical schools formed within Japanese religious and political life; their differences cannot be projected backward onto all Chinese history. When a contemporary school describes “its” meditation, it is useful to ask whether this is a contemporary normative account, a historical characterisation, or a philosophical thesis.

Both Soto and Rinzai can speak of wisdom and compassion. This is no reason to deny actual differences between their methods, pace of training, or language of enlightenment in advance.

### 16. Dogen: practice-realisation rather than producing a reward

At the opening of Fukan Zazengi, Dogen asks an exceptionally clear question: if the Way is originally perfect and present, why effort and sitting? He then does not eliminate practice but instructs it in detail: how to sit, work with the body and attention, and relate to results. He deliberately maintains the tension between original presence and exercise. [[12](#ref-12)]

*Shusho itto* (修証一等, the inseparability of practice and realisation) rejects a scheme in which practice is an external means gradually producing enlightenment as a separate result. Realisation is not postponed: it is expressed in practice itself. This is not the identity of two already finished things, but the unity of their Buddhist meaning. The approach also unfolds in Bendowa, where Dogen discusses sitting and the realisation of the Way. Dogen therefore does not say that because enlightenment is not “produced,” practice can be abandoned. On the contrary, wholehearted sitting becomes particularly important. [[21](#ref-21)]

Within Buddhist language, this approach has positive content. Practice is no longer reduced to an investment in future reward; it participates in what Dogen regards as the Buddha Way. Consider care for the world: if concern for another is expressed in concrete help, helping is not merely a method for subsequently acquiring care. This example does not prove Dogen’s doctrine. It illustrates how an action can be a realisation rather than merely an instrument for an external goal.

*Shikantaza*, “just sitting,” is a concrete discipline. It guarantees no psychological pleasantness and cannot be generalised into “do nothing” for all human life. Its meaning is determined by its Buddhist context, not by an outward posture alone. [[9](#ref-9), [11](#ref-11), [12](#ref-12)]

### 17. Thinking of not-thinking

In the widely circulated version of Fukan Zazengi, Dogen uses a formula from a dialogue about sitting: thinking of not-thinking. The answer to how this is possible is *hishiryo*. Accurate reading requires three distinct expressions: *shiryo* (思量, “thinking”), *fushiryo* (不思量, “not-thinking,” negating thinking), and *hishiryo* (非思量, often rendered “nonthinking” in English, in contrast to “not-thinking” for *fushiryo*). Russian translations of the latter two can sound identical, although the original negations differ. These are not three stages to be traversed in sequence, but a challenging formula addressed to the manner of sitting. [[9](#ref-9), [12](#ref-12)]

It proposes neither the removal of every thought nor a new theoretical classification of consciousness. One should neither deliberately manufacture the right thoughts nor turn their absence into a trophy. Dogen speaks of zazen as practical realisation in which attention is not governed by grasping at arising reasoning or pursuing results.

A textual detail matters. In the surviving signed Tenpuku version of Fukan Zazengi, an instruction to notice arising thoughts and let them go appears instead of this threefold formula. That instruction connects awareness of a thought with its disappearance, rather than with forcible suppression. The difference between versions is noted in an official Soto explanation. Hishiryo should therefore be associated with the widely circulated version, not presented as an unchanged element of every early version. [[20](#ref-20)]

This also reveals the limit of a modern philosophical paraphrase. The content of thinking of not-thinking unfolds within the discipline of zazen; it is not established by an unusual logic of negations alone.

### 18. Genjokoan: studying and forgetting the self

In Genjokoan, Dogen connects studying the Buddha Way with studying oneself, forgetting oneself, and being verified by the myriad dharmas. This is a sequence within Buddhist training, not an instruction to erase memory, a personal name, or the human capacity to care for others. [[11](#ref-11), [13](#ref-13)]

First, the text directs a person towards the very manner of following the Way rather than an abstract theory of someone else’s states. Next, forgetting oneself disrupts a familiar perspective in which the world merely supplies a background to one’s own project of attainment. Finally, verification by the myriad dharmas restores the significance of what is encountered, teaches, and acts. Dogen’s text moves from enclosed self-seeking towards engaging with the world as the Buddhist Way.

This can also be interpreted as relinquishing the assumption that “I” is a permanent centre or owner of everything happening. But this should be marked as a modern interpretation: Dogen does not use monophenomenalism’s terminology or address independent first principles in its formulation. His discourse belongs to the study of Dharma, practice, and the realisation of wisdom.

This makes the passage deeper, not shallower. Forgetting oneself in a Buddhist context does not mean becoming an impersonal void. It concerns how learning becomes not a project of accumulating merit for a separate “I,” but a way in which the world itself and other dharmas participate in realising the Way.

### 19. Uji: existence and time as an event of the Way

In the Uji fascicle of Shobogenzo, Dogen rereads an ordinary expression that can mean “at one time” or “at a certain time,” separating it into a relationship between existence and time. Some translations have established the title “Being-time.” This title requires care: it already embodies a translator’s philosophical decision, rather than being a simple neutral equivalent. [[14](#ref-14)]

The text does not present time as an empty external container into which separate things are subsequently placed. Concrete beings, situations, and events are considered in their temporal actuality. This is not a crude denial of past or future: Dogen uses traditional temporal distinctions while rethinking their relationship to Buddhist realisation. Time becomes neither advice to “live for today” nor an endless atom of the present.

Raji C. Steineck argues that modern commentators often transfer Dogen from a religious and practical genre into a philosophical system, thereby changing his original question. In particular, *u* can be translated differently as “being,” “what is,” or “existence,” while *ji* can be read as one “time” or concrete “times.” Steineck emphasises that Dogen urges his audience to practise and study the Way; the text was not written as a response to modern debates on the ontology of time. The researcher nevertheless allows philosophical dialogue with Dogen when the transformation of meaning is recognised and justified. [[14](#ref-14)]

This does not prohibit philosophical comparison. It makes comparison responsible: first reconstruct what time means in religious instruction, then separately ask what a modern theory might contribute. One thing is already clear: Uji investigates not only temporal experiences but how existence, change, and practice are understood within the Buddha Way.

### 20. Everyday life, ethics, and historical responsibility

Chan and Zen bring Buddhist training into eating, working, conversation, caring for the sick, monastic discipline, and relationships. This is part of the positive content of “everyday life is a site of practice.” Discipline, vows, compassion, and the bodhisattva path are not accidental later restrictions imposed on a “free” Zen; they are important parts of the tradition. Christopher Ives’s study shows the variety of ways ethics has functioned in Zen schools. [[15](#ref-15)]

History nevertheless gives no reason to regard spiritual authority as a guarantee of good conduct. Brian Victoria documented and critically discussed the support given to Japanese military expansion by a number of influential Zen figures and institutions in the twentieth century. Assessments of his broader conclusions remain debated, but the serious historical problems themselves require discussion of responsibility, violence, and institutional power. [[18](#ref-18)]

Stories of teachers’ shouts, blows, and unpredictable answers belong to particular literary and monastic settings. Their presence in tradition does not give contemporary teachers a moral right to harm students. Likewise, belonging to a transmission lineage does not make every statement historically accurate or every decision infallible.

An open question is especially important here: if awakening means overcoming ignorance, how can its expression in care be verified rather than merely recognised in a charismatic manner of speaking? The tradition has requirements and criteria, but history itself shows that their application needs continuing examination.

### 21. Zen beyond its popular image

The modern image of Zen often associates it with an aesthetic of simplicity, silence, an uncluttered room, tea ceremony, and stress reduction. Some associations have historical grounds. But they do not represent the full diversity of monastic life, religious rituals, relationships to karma, Buddhist vows, and complex philosophical texts.

“Zen acknowledges neither words nor reasoning” requires particular care. As a historical claim it is false; as polemical instruction it can point to the limitations of exclusively verbal confidence; as a universal philosophical thesis it requires an argument and a precise specification of its subject.

Zen is not “Western psychological minimalism before the West.” It can be intelligible to a contemporary person, but accessibility does not justify removing its historical Mahayana, bodhisattva paths, ritual, and social forms of life.

### 22. What the independent study reveals

Chan’s substantive core cannot be reduced to prohibitions on thinking and reading. It is an intense engagement with how the Buddhist Way is realised directly in life. Huineng’s no-thought describes engagement with conditions without being caught by them. “Ordinary mind” in the Hongzhou tradition and the tradition associated with Nanquan brings the Way back from an exotic goal to everyday activity. Koans make cases objects of interpretation and practice. Dahui and Hongzhi organise work with consciousness differently. Dogen makes the inseparability of practice and realisation a concern of sitting and language themselves.

These answers are not identical, and centuries of historical transformation stand behind them. Yet the positive content of a question important to the comparison is now visible: can awakening avoid becoming an alien object without devaluing life, learning, the body, events, ethics, and effort?

Zen answers from within Buddhism. It needs neither to be dissolved into general psychology nor proved through another philosophical system. Its real strength is clearer when positive instructions are read in their original setting and retain their internal tensions.

### 23. Questions to bring to the comparison

The study identifies several connected questions. First, how is no-thought compatible with clear understanding and distinction without turning distinction into attachment? Second, when does “seeing one’s nature” reveal the Buddhist meaning of awakening, and when does it encourage reifying what supposedly must be found? Third, what does practice realise if the Way is not a product of an initial deficiency? Fourth, how can a koan be approached without destroying either its practical meaning or the criteria of historical and philosophical examination?

These are joined by Dogen’s questions about forgetting oneself, Buddha-nature, and being-time; the difficulties of everyday conduct and ethics; and, finally, why Buddhist freedom from attachment is connected with a path rather than reduced to simply relinquishing the need for attainment.

A comparison with monophenomenalism’s independent starting position is now possible. It will not answer “Who said the same thing first?” It should show where directions of inquiry genuinely converge and where the foundations, or even the subject of the question, change.

## Part II. Zen considered through monophenomenalism

### 1. Two starting points

There is a reason the comparison begins only after an independent account of Zen. If Huineng’s no-thought were declared from the outset to mean the model’s concept, Buddha-nature to mean the phenomenon, and awakening to mean absolute freedom, the inquiry would be circular: another tradition would yield only what had already been put into its description. Neither Zen’s history nor a critical examination of monophenomenalism would have room to emerge.

Comparison requires distinguishing starting points, meanings of terms, and human implications. It compares ways of posing questions, not words that sound alike. Where the traditions genuinely approach one another, that similarity deserves recognition; where foundations and purposes change, the difference must be preserved.

Roman Valentinovich Dudnikov’s philosophy appears here as an independent interlocutor, not a judge of Buddhist tradition. The inquiry must remain open to objections from both sides.

### 2. The starting position: one phenomenon and five theses

Monophenomenalism states its starting position directly: “Everything that exists is the phenomenon. The phenomenon is one.” This is a positive philosophical assertion. It is not strictly deduced from a proof that other ontologies are impossible, nor is it merely a refusal to discuss the external. Unity here does not mean one separate thing, one cosmic person, or a homogeneous mass without differences. A separate, independently established first principle is not accepted as a necessary second pole of existence. The question of other ontologies is not declared logically closed. [[B](#ref-B)]

The Model of the Phenomenon is **one possible conceptual tool of inquiry within monophenomenalism**. Accepting the starting philosophical position does not require accepting this particular model. The model specifies a way of considering experiencing and experience through five theses, whose wording is preserved:

1. Everything is one phenomenon.
2. The phenomenon is sensory experiencing.
3. A distinction within sensory experiencing is a concept.
4. Concepts structure the phenomenon into experience.
5. Experience is the phenomenon structured through concepts.

Sensory experiencing includes not only sensations through the senses but also thoughts, emotions, and states. Experiencing and experience are distinguished: experience is the phenomenon structured through concepts. A concept need not be a word or an intellectual theory; it designates a system of distinctions. A distinction is not an act by a hidden “distinguisher.” It exists as a way of experiencing. The five theses do not depict a sequential factory: first an allegedly empty phenomenon, then a subject, then manufactured experience. They are different aspects of considering what already exists. The model itself is a concept within the phenomenon, not an engine that creates it. [[A](#ref-A)]

Things, people, history, actions, bodily pain, joy, and scientific testing exist: all are distinguished and have content. But being distinguished does not entail that every distinction must be recognised as a fundamental independent principle. Monophenomenalism does not require existence first to be divided into independent realms so that a bridge can then be sought between them. This is the meaning of “Nothing to connect”: there is no obligation first to acknowledge independent fundamental principles and then solve the problem of connecting them. This philosophical move does not deny people or actual relationships.

The position is not deduced from Zen, and Zen does not prove the five theses. It is therefore important to distinguish conclusions from monophenomenalism’s starting position from those depending on the model’s additional concepts.

### 3. No-thought and distinction: an apparent contradiction

At first glance, a contradiction appears between the Platform Sutra and the model’s third thesis. Huineng teaches no-thought, while the model states that a distinction within sensory experiencing is a concept. Does Buddhist awakening then require conceptuality to cease, while the model describes all experiencing through conceptual distinction?

The apparent contradiction weakens when the subjects of these statements are clarified. In the Platform Sutra, no-thought concerns how a person lives and acts amid arising thoughts and circumstances. What matters is not being caught, the absence of fixed abiding, rather than a blankness in which words and events are no longer distinguished. In the model, meanwhile, a concept does not mean a harmful idea, psychological attachment, or belief system to be purified away. It concerns distinctions as such.

Imagine someone hearing a harsh word. Sound, meaning, speaker, personal reaction, and a possible reply are distinguished. In the model’s language, all of this is conceptually structured experience. But these distinctions do not entail compulsory clinging: what is heard need not become a final verdict on one’s identity, an occasion for attack, or the centre of a closed story built around one remark. Buddhist no-thought can describe such a way of remaining uncaught. The model need not regard distinctions as errors.

An important boundary remains. Huineng’s no-thought belongs to the Buddhist path of wisdom and liberation from ignorance. It has normative meaning: one relationship to events is preferable to another. The model’s third thesis alone does not establish a normative boundary between wisdom and ignorance, nonattachment and clinging. Further concepts, descriptions, and arguments are required. Monophenomenalism as a starting position, and the model as one instrument of inquiry, allow the question of clinging to be posed differently, but do not themselves determine Buddhist criteria of wisdom and ignorance.

There is a harder remainder. If a tradition speaks of nonconceptual knowledge or a distinctive wisdom, it should not immediately be translated into the model with the declaration “That is still a concept, so the Buddha was wrong.” In Indian Buddhist epistemology, particularly Dignaga and Dharmakirti, conceptual and nonconceptual cognition are distinguished by criteria that do not coincide with the model’s broad use of “concept.” One term therefore cannot simply replace the other. The disagreement may concern **classification** rather than experienced content. Establishing a real contradiction would require showing that both sides discuss the same thing in the same sense. [Section 14](#section-40) returns to this question.

A substantive question emerges: how can distinguished experiencing be the site both of attachment and of liberating action? A Buddhist answer will concern practice and wisdom. The model offers its own language for analysing distinctions, without deciding their quality on behalf of Buddhist tradition.

### 4. Buddha-nature and one phenomenon: a particularly risky affinity

It is tempting to find monophenomenalism’s position already expressed in Buddha-nature teachings. This is especially tempting in Dogen, where “all sentient beings have Buddha-nature” is reinterpreted as an intimate relationship between reality itself and Buddha-nature. One might want to say: “There it is: everything is one phenomenon.”

Precision is particularly necessary here. *Buddha-dhatu*, *tathagatagarbha*, and *bussho* have histories within Buddhist teachings about awakening, ignorance, impermanence, emptiness, and the Buddha Way. Dogen’s reworking of the language of possession concerns not only the ultimate question of what exists, but also how existence realises Buddhist practice. Buddha-nature is not the question of the absence of a second fundamental first principle in Dudnikov’s terminology. [[16](#ref-16), [19](#ref-19)]

Monophenomenalism introduces no hidden “nature of the phenomenon” to be discovered, attributes no innate perfection to the phenomenon, and does not make it the possessor of awakening. It begins with the positive position of one phenomenon without constructing another independent entity behind it. Calling this Buddha-nature would transfer Buddhist normative and soteriological commitments onto a position that does not contain them.

There is nevertheless an affinity in the direction of criticism. Both a reading of Dogen and monophenomenalism allow the familiar grammar “a being possesses a special secret property” to be questioned. Dogen moves beyond that grammar within Buddha-nature teaching. In the model, bearer and property are themselves distinctions within one phenomenon; no necessity to acknowledge the bearer as a separate first principle is established. These are **comparable moves against reification**, not identical ontologies.

A question that a Buddhist can legitimately put to monophenomenalism remains open: what does the positive “one” mean if it is neither a substance nor a single object? Why is this starting position not another fixed concept to which one can become attached? Monophenomenalism openly acknowledges “one” as a positive philosophical starting point rather than a proved consequence of the limits of knowledge. This answers a question about **status and the limits of justification**, but does not automatically resolve the Buddhist objection. The Madhyamaka question of whether a new independent ontological support is being introduced remains substantive and is also considered in [section 14](#section-40). [[B](#ref-B)]

### 5. Seeing one’s nature and the question of who sees

The Chan formula “seeing one’s own nature” (*kensho* / *jianxing*) can easily suggest a hidden subject that must discover a hidden object. Awakening then becomes a familiar scene: first an observer exists, then a special entity appears before them. But the Buddhist use of the formula, including its relationship to not-self and the critique of clinging, is more complex. “Seeing” can mean understanding and realising the Buddhist Way, not necessarily directly contemplating a spiritual object.

From the model’s position, the construction of the question can be investigated afresh. A person, a search, seeing, what is sought, instruction, and a change of understanding are distinguished. But distinguishing “seer” and “seen” does not prove two independent first principles that must subsequently meet. Dudnikov’s philosophy needs no separate supersubject to whom all experiencing belongs. [A, B]

This does not mean that historical Buddhists intended exactly this formulation. On the contrary, the Buddhist path connects seeing with liberation from a particular ignorance, practice, and ethical transformation. Analysing the words “seer” and “seen” alone gives the model no basis to conclude that the entire Buddhist task is automatically accomplished. It reveals where a fundamental pair need not be introduced; what counts as awakening in Zen is determined by the content of the relevant Buddhist teaching.

### 6. Ordinary mind and ordinary life: what is already here?

The formulas associated with Mazu and Nanquan are attractive because they remove the need to seek the Way in a separate extraordinary object. A substantive opportunity for comparison with monophenomenalism arises here: if everything is one phenomenon, no domain of ordinary events must first be excluded as “not real existence.” Cooking dinner, talking, working, and hearing a car are not obstacles to the phenomenon being the phenomenon.

But for Mazu, ordinary mind does not mean that every reaction is ethically sufficient. His language concerns particular practice and overcoming the tension of seeking. In the model, “everything is the phenomenon” does not distribute judgements between wise and unwise reactions. Kindness and pointless cruelty are both distinguished; their being distinguished within one phenomenon does not make them identical.

Consider someone hurrying, noticing a request for help, and stopping. The model’s language distinguishes haste, request, decision, actions, and consequences. All are the phenomenon structured into experience. A Zen question might be different: can action express wisdom and compassion without first manufacturing the image of a “properly enlightened person”? These answers can meet in life, yet monophenomenalist and Buddhist ways of evaluating what happens differ.

Ordinary life needs no metaphysical consecration to be what it is. The Buddhist question of wisdom in everyday action remains independent.

### 7. Koans, mu, and the framing of a question

The story of Zhaozhou’s dog is especially interesting for the way a ready-made pair of answers can hold a question within an established frame. The monk asks whether a dog has Buddha-nature. Wumen’s later treatment makes mu not merely a negation but a focus of practice. Other readings of a similar exchange yield different answers. The story encourages reconsideration of a familiar opposition’s meaning, but this does not exhaust its Buddhist tasks. [[7](#ref-7), [8](#ref-8), [10](#ref-10)]

Monophenomenalism likewise allows a question’s framing to be investigated before the expected answer is accepted. When a philosopher asks how to connect two independent consciousnesses, the grounds for already presenting them as independent principles can be examined. If they were first affirmed as fundamentally separate, the problem of connecting them may result from precisely that premise. “Nothing to connect” names this turn: not a denial of people, but a refusal to make a disputed separation a compulsory premise.

There is a strong analogy in **how a question is approached**: before solving the given problem, check whether an additional assumption created it. But it would be mistaken to claim that mu means “one phenomenon” or that every koan exposes only a false problem about independent first principles. A koan can perform disciplinary, philological, ritual, and interpretive functions not reducible to monophenomenalism’s ontological questions.

Changing the frame also does not replace checking facts. Refusing to accept two fundamental owners of experiencing does not remove the need to learn what a particular person feels, help them, and distinguish reliable testimony from error. Likewise, practising with mu does not relieve historians of identifying the textual version or translators of distinguishing meanings.

### 8. Sudden awakening: changed understanding without producing an entity

Many sudden-awakening stories show spiritual change described as something other than acquiring a missing object. Someone has long tried to obtain something, then sees the situation differently. An affinity with monophenomenalism’s human significance appears here: a changed way of considering things sometimes removes the weight of a task that seemed unavoidable a moment earlier.

But analogy is not identity. Buddhist awakening has a definite meaning within teachings about ignorance and liberation. Monophenomenalism promises no special awakening event and establishes no programme compulsory for everyone. Accepting its philosophical position is not the same as attaining Buddhist nirvana or completing a traditional Chan path. [A, B]

A change in perspective can be sudden: a premise that previously organised the entire problem stops being convincing. But the speed of change does not become a criterion of truth. A real error can be seen suddenly, and an equally sudden illusion can be accepted. Inner relief therefore does not prove the model’s philosophical truth, just as a student’s powerful experience does not remove the need for subsequent Buddhist examination of understanding and conduct.

The important point is not to turn understanding into another thing. If a change occurs, it cannot be reduced to possessing an object called “freedom” as personal property. It can nevertheless affect one’s relationship to life: a human dimension distinct from the logical status of the starting thesis.

### 9. Practice and nonattainment: the hardest point of comparison

Dogen poses a question that philosophical comparison must not evade: if the Way is originally perfect, why sit? Fukan Zazengi does not deny practice’s necessity; it changes the meaning of practice’s relationship to awakening. In shusho itto, practice and realisation are not two objects on different shelves. Practice can express what it realises. [[12](#ref-12), [21](#ref-21)]

Monophenomenalism poses the question differently. Its starting position contains no prescription to sit, contemplate, repeat mu, or achieve a particular state as a condition for everything being the phenomenon. One possible human reading of this position is the possibility of **not making liberation a compulsory task**. Within the model’s philosophy, a person need not first become someone exceptional for the phenomenon to be what it is.

The mistaken conclusion that practice is meaningless does not follow. Meditation, learning, therapy, physical work, and care are distinguished ways of living that can have their own purposes and consequences. The model simply establishes none as a necessary metaphysical transition to one phenomenon. This differs from Dogen, for whom practice has positive Buddhist significance even when not understood as producing a reward. **Zen itself, however, need not regard practice as connecting previously independent principles.** Dogen in particular need not introduce a metaphysical gap between practice and realisation. The difference concerns the status and meaning of the Buddhist path, not one approach acknowledging a fundamental split that the other removes.

The comparison can be stated precisely. For Dogen, “awakening need not be manufactured” is compatible with the requirement to practise: practice expresses Buddhist realisation. For monophenomenalism, “liberation need not first be attained” means the absence of a **general philosophical duty** to perform a special practice to justify existence. This neither denies practice’s usefulness nor claims that anyone is already enlightened in the Buddhist sense.

Someone who likes sitting quietly can sit. Someone who must care for a loved one can care. Someone facing a difficult practical problem is entitled to address it. None of these activities becomes secondary because what is “real” supposedly exists only in another special state. Yet an activity’s significance is not proved by one ontological formula either: its meaning and evaluation unfold within concrete experience.

### 10. Dogen: thinking of not-thinking and forgetting oneself

The hishiryo formula in Fukan Zazengi helps reveal the limits of the simple opposition “I think / I do not think.” Dogen instructs a manner of sitting identical neither to ordinary rational planning nor to attempts to suppress thought. The model can distinguish thought, an intention to stop it, and the experiencing of silence; none becomes external to the phenomenon. This nevertheless does not yet describe the quality of zazen as Buddhist practice.

Genjokoan is even more interesting. Dogen connects studying the Way with studying and forgetting oneself, and with verification by the myriad dharmas. “There is no separate owner” should not automatically be substituted here. It can serve as a **subsequent philosophical interpretation**, but does not belong to Dogen’s language. He neither formulates one phenomenon nor proves independent perspectives impossible.

In the model, “I” is a distinction within experience, not a necessary fundamental observer who creates experiencing. This does not imply the destruction of a person and their biography. Likewise, Dogen’s forgetting oneself does not mean amnesia. Both permit investigation of what happens when the familiar need to secure a separate centre no longer serves as a starting support. For Dogen, however, the theme develops towards the Buddha Way; for the model, towards analysing the phenomenon’s distinctions and the premises of philosophical questions.

A subtle danger lies in “disappears.” Saying “the self disappears” can falsely suggest that memory, life, relationships, and responsibility disappear. More precisely, **a particular fundamental assumption about an owner** ceases to be compulsory, while the distinction of a person and their concrete life remains. Dogen’s text has a different aim: Buddhist self-investigation is realised as forgetting oneself and verification by dharmas. Similarity should not be extended into literal identity.

### 11. Uji and time: different questions beneath similar words

In the Model of the Phenomenon, time is investigated as a concept structuring experience into distinctions of “earlier,” “now,” “later,” duration, and sequence. This is not a story in which physically existing time was created by a mental act, nor a claim that scientific measurement of time is useless. The model describes the status of time within the adopted way of considering the phenomenon. [[A](#ref-A)]

Dogen works differently in Uji. He rethinks existence and time through Buddhist instruction about concrete events and realisation. His account cannot be reduced to “time is a concept,” still less to a simple psychology of the present moment. Steineck argues that transforming uji into a modern theory of time can quietly change the text’s subject. Such a reading is nevertheless possible if the translation between contexts is recognised and justified. [[14](#ref-14)]

There is still a genuine shared question: why does “first there is an empty container of time, then events are placed in it” seem so natural? In the model, this picture is understood as a conceptual structure of experience. In Dogen, the relationship between time and concrete things is reconsidered through the Buddhist path. Both perspectives can examine the familiar container image, but rely on different claims and lead to different consequences.

An important limitation follows: “time is a concept” does not automatically imply that actual processes are reversible, that actions have no consequences, or that the past has the same empirical accessibility as a present sensation of pain. The conceptual status of a description does not deprive practical distinctions of force. Nor does a Buddhist understanding of time entitle anyone to say that history, karma, or conditioned consequences no longer matter.

### 12. Other people, pain, and ethics: freedom is not indifference

There is a risk in this comparison that must be avoided. Chan can speak of not-self and nonattachment. Monophenomenalism declines to introduce independent first principles. Superficially combining these moves can produce the false claim “Nobody exists, so nobody can be harmed.” This follows from neither approach when responsibly considered.

In the model, a person, their account, pain, body, request, care, and relationships are distinguished within one phenomenon. It is not established that these distinctions are independent fundamental grounds. But immediate pain and a report of it, sensation and memory, a false promise and help given, do not thereby become the same. Philosophical unity is not ethical indifference. [[A](#ref-A)]

If a loved one needs medical attention, that question does not disappear after reasoning about one phenomenon. Calling a doctor, making a concrete decision, and listening remain real tasks. Similarly, Zen discipline, vows, and compassion do not lose significance when clinging to the idea of a separate self ceases. The history of schools whose senior teachers supported militarism shows the danger of treating the rhetoric of nonduality as a ready-made moral justification. [[15](#ref-15), [18](#ref-18)]

The difference between traditions remains here too. In Buddhism, ethical action is connected with the bodhisattva path, karma, compassion, and liberation from dukkha. The model’s five theses contain no ready-made complete ethics. They offer a way to investigate how ethical relationships are present within experience, but what to do requires further substantive grounds and examination. This is no lack of radicalism: refusing to invent an additional first principle does not mean refusing responsibility for actions.

### 13. Absolute freedom: a crucial difference of purposes

A question especially important to monophenomenalism now arises: if there is no need first to create a separate hidden subject, seek an external foundation of the phenomenon, or prove experiencing’s right to exist, what remains of the need to “attain freedom”? Might the race for liberation itself become another way of declaring the present inadequate?

For monophenomenalism, this question is not answered by asserting a special substance called Freedom or attributing absolute freedom to the phenomenon as a property. The Model of the Phenomenon explicitly states that freedom is a concept distinguished through the limitations from which it is conceived; absolute freedom is neither given as a separate experienced quality nor a property of the phenomenon. The position’s positive human significance lies not in possessing a metaphysical treasure, but in seeing that the demand first to make an impossible absolute exit from what exists need not be accepted. [[A](#ref-A)]

Buddhist Zen poses a different question. It acknowledges ignorance, dukkha, attachment, a path, and awakening. Even when it speaks of original Buddha-nature or the inseparability of practice and realisation, the Buddhist meaning of the path remains. Reducing Zen to a call to learn nothing and strive for nothing would contradict numerous traditional texts and practices.

An affinity is possible in experiencing the unnecessary character of additional self-coercion. Someone may have spent their life thinking, “I must become completely different; otherwise I am not yet allowed to live.” In light of monophenomenalism, they may stop regarding this condition as compulsory. A Zen practitioner might undergo a similar shift while working with attachment to attainment. Neither tradition, however, guarantees immediate relief. Pain, loss, limitations, and responsibility are not cancelled.

This difference makes comparison substantive: Zen offers a path within which the goal should not be reified; monophenomenalism introduces no compulsory path as a condition for the phenomenon’s unity. One investigates how liberation is realised; the other permits the question of what grounds the demand first to become liberated before acknowledging one’s own life as meaningful. No winner need be chosen. It is enough to recognise clearly that the questions genuinely differ.

### 14. A critical examination of the comparison itself

Substantive comparison is not exhausted by successful analogies. It must withstand objections from both a Buddhist philosopher and a critical reader of monophenomenalism.

The first objection: if everything is one, why is this not simply another metaphysical concept to cling to? An answer must begin by acknowledging the claim’s status. Monophenomenalism proposes a positive starting assertion, not a deductively proved result. The model includes itself as a concept. This prevents description from turning itself into an external mechanism, but does not protect the position from substantive criticism. A Buddhist, especially a Madhyamaka thinker, is entitled to ask whether the positive “one” acquires an unnecessary ontological function. An automatic response that emptiness already confirms unity would be an invalid inference.

The second objection: if every distinction is a concept, what about Buddhist teachings on nonconceptual knowledge? Renaming cannot settle this dispute. The model’s concept encompasses the distinctions within experiencing, while certain Buddhist schools give “conceptual” narrower criteria. Substantive comparison requires an independent reconstruction of those criteria. A lack of literal agreement is not yet a contradiction, but neither should its absence be declared in advance.

The third objection: if practice is not compulsory for the phenomenon itself, does everything become ethically and humanly arbitrary? No, because the theses do not describe every normative ground of human life. Help, evidence, promises, and responsibility have distinguishable significance within experience; the model does not instruct anyone to disregard them. But it also contains no deduction of a universal ethical code. This is an honestly stated boundary, not a promise that five theses replace all moral philosophy.

The fourth objection: if liberation is not required, does that devalue the suffering of people seeking help? The absence of a compulsory metaphysical transition does not prohibit treatment, learning, meditation, or changing difficult circumstances. The position’s positive significance is precisely that practice can be chosen and meaningful without becoming a condition of the right to exist. Pain and the need for action remain acknowledged.

The fifth objection: does the proposed comparison replace Dogen with a modern philosophy? This is a real risk, argued by Steineck. The independent account of Zen is therefore separated from philosophical comparison, whose results are presented as **consideration through monophenomenalism using the model**, not as “what Dogen’s words truly mean.” Where Dogen speaks of dharmas, the Buddhist path, and realisation, they are not quietly replaced with concepts, unity, or absolute freedom. [[14](#ref-14)]

The sixth objection: does “nothing to connect” remove the problem of other minds merely through words? It removes a particular initial requirement to connect centres of ownership already accepted as fundamental. But questions about testimony, empathy, error, communication, and the justification for attributing feelings to another remain. In ordinary life they are concrete and important. The philosophical turn therefore changes the frame without replacing investigation of other people with an unconditional denial of their existence.

The seventh objection: can Zen in turn influence monophenomenalism? Yes, as a source of critical clarification: Zen reveals a difference between acknowledging a formulation and living in a certain way, between confidence in an explanation’s truth and the ability not to cling to it. This influence does not require adding Buddhist soteriology to the model. The question can be taken seriously without changing any of the five theses.

### 15. What remains after the comparison

Zen emerges as a complex Buddhist tradition developing, in different ways, the relationships between direct understanding and the path, thought and remaining uncaught, word and realisation, sitting and action. No-thought is particularly important in Huineng; Mazu brings the Way into ordinary life without making ordinariness an excuse for every reaction; koan teachers work with questions and their interpretation; Dogen explores the inseparability of practice and realisation, forgetting oneself, and the time of concrete Buddhist life.

Monophenomenalism is not their hidden common foundation. It offers another positive starting point: everything that exists is one phenomenon. As one possible instrument within this position, the Model of the Phenomenon allows distinctions in experiencing to be considered without positing a separate owner as a necessary fundamental principle. Differences need not become independent first principles; their independence is not established merely by naming them. A particular human significance opens here: there is no need first to postulate existence’s fundamental fragmentation and then demand that it be reassembled. This significance belongs to the adopted philosophical consideration, without claiming to be the necessary result of every spiritual practice.

Freedom from the compulsory demand to attain a special state is nevertheless not nirvana. It neither cancels suffering, makes everyone enlightened, nor devalues practice. Zen can be practised, the model philosophically investigated, and other forms of life and care pursued. The phenomenon’s unity alone does not demand the choice of one exclusive spiritual procedure.

The most fruitful result is therefore not “Zen and monophenomenalism are the same,” but the ability to preserve their difference while keeping conversation possible. Zen offers rich historical and practical material about discovering attachment to attainment and ready-made answers. Monophenomenalism proposes another starting point: must what is distinguished be accepted as independent first principles before the problem of connecting them is posed? These are two different lines of inquiry. Preserving their independence is precisely what makes the dialogue substantive.
