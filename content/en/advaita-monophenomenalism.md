Advaita Vedānta and Monophenomenalism: Two Starting Points
Nonduality, Ātman, consciousness, distinction, and freedom: what each philosophy claims and where their boundaries lie.
Comparative essay
2026-11-08

Nonduality can easily become an attractive general formula: everything is one, there is no separate “I,” freedom need not be attained. Yet similar words can express very different philosophical commitments. We cannot understand the relationship between two philosophies merely by renaming Brahman “the phenomenon” or Ātman “consciousness.”

We will first consider Advaita Vedānta on its own terms. Then we will set out monophenomenalism and *Model of the Phenomenon* without treating Advaita as the source of their claims. Only then can we identify where the two approaches genuinely meet and where the resemblance ends.

**This account of Advaita is our understanding and interpretation of its philosophy**, based on classical texts, commentaries, and modern scholarship. We aim to represent the tradition's central claims accurately, without presenting our reading as the only valid interpretation or claiming to speak for every school of Advaita. Disagreements about this reading should be kept distinct from the comparison that follows: in the second part we present a separate philosophical position on its own foundations.

## Advaita: What Nonduality Means

The Sanskrit word *advaita* means “nonduality,” literally “not two.” Classical Advaita Vedānta, particularly as articulated by Śaṅkara, holds that **Brahman is the only independent ultimate reality, and the true self, Ātman, is identical with Brahman**.

This does not mean that there is one enormous object in place of many smaller ones. Nor does it mean that an individual ego owns the universe. Differences among people, objects, and events remain meaningful in ordinary life, but they do not constitute a plurality of independent ultimate principles.

Advaita is at once a philosophy of being, a theory of knowledge, and a path of liberation. Its questions belong together: what truly exists, who knows, and why does a person take themselves to be a limited being?

## Why Advaita Turns to the Upaniṣads

For Śaṅkara, the Upaniṣads have a distinctive authority as a source of knowledge of Brahman. Reasoning is essential for clarifying their meaning, resolving apparent inconsistencies, and responding to objections. But the identity of Ātman and Brahman is not offered merely as a deduction from neutral premises that every philosopher must already accept.

This is a defining feature of the tradition, not a defect that can simply be repaired by adding another argument. Advaita's account of knowledge distinguishes ordinary perception of objects, reasoning, and the testimony of the Upaniṣads concerning that which is not one object among others.

Thus asking how Advaita justifies nonduality requires us to consider both Śaṅkara's arguments and the role of scriptural testimony. Replacing one with the other would alter the teaching we are trying to understand.

## Brahman and the Dependent Reality of the World

The *Chāndogya Upaniṣad* (6.1.4) offers the example of clay and the vessels made from it. A pot, bowl, and jar differ in name, shape, and use, yet as clay objects they do not exist independently of clay.

The example shows that **difference of form need not imply independence of existence**. Advaita applies this approach to the plurality of the world. Names and forms, *nāma-rūpa*, are determinate, but they have no ultimate reality independent of Brahman.

Brahman, however, must not be understood literally as cosmic clay. The material of a pot changes; Brahman is understood as unchanging. Advaita distinguishes ultimate reality from the dependent reality of the world. The latter is often called *mithyā*, which is not equivalent to sheer nonexistence.

Someone may mistake a rope for a snake, feel fear, and then correct the mistake. Advaita uses such an example to distinguish an experienced appearance from its ultimate status. This does not render all everyday knowledge equally false: within human life, inquiry, action, consequences, and the distinction between correct and mistaken judgments remain meaningful.

The term *māyā* is associated with the appearance of plurality in a nondual reality. Yet māyā must not become a second independent principle alongside Brahman. More detailed accounts of its status, along with developed schemes of multiple orders of reality, differ between Śaṅkara and later Advaitins.

Advaita is not necessarily committed to providing a physical mechanism by which Brahman produces the world. Accounts of creation can serve as preliminary instruction: from the ultimate standpoint, the production of a second, independently existing reality is not affirmed.

## Ātman, Consciousness, and the Witness

The body changes, thoughts arise and disappear, feelings give way to others, and a person's self-understanding changes as well. Advaita examines these changes and distinguishes what is known from the consciousness by which it is known.

Consciousness here is not another object discovered within the mind. If an inner state is discovered, it is already something known. Śaṅkara therefore treats consciousness as self-revealing, not requiring a second consciousness to certify the first.

The term *sākṣin*, witnessing consciousness, helps explain this. But the witness is not an inner person seated behind thoughts. Nor is the relationship between witness and witnessed the final truth of nonduality: ultimately Brahman does not stand opposite a second, independent object.

The *Chāndogya Upaniṣad* (6.8.7) gives this teaching its celebrated expression: **tat tvam asi — “You are That.”** It is not the identification of a personal biography with the cosmos. It asserts the identity of the true self with ultimate reality.

Two steps must be distinguished. The content of experiencing changes, while consciousness in Advaita is not reducible to any particular change. But that observation alone does not establish the identity of consciousness and Brahman without the other grounds of Advaita's teaching, above all the testimony of the Upaniṣads.

## Avidyā, Adhyāsa, and Bondage

If Ātman is always identical with Brahman, why does a person experience themselves as a limited agent?

Śaṅkara turns to *avidyā*, ignorance, and *adhyāsa*, erroneous superimposition. Features of body and mind are attributed to the true self; the self's consciousness is in turn attributed to the embodied mind as if it were independently its source.

Advaita does not deny bodily exhaustion or pain. It examines another move: why are changes in the body and mind taken as final definitions of what a person truly is?

This gives bondage its philosophical meaning. A person seeks lasting completeness through the results of action, possessions, and changing circumstances because they take their true nature to be limited.

A difficult question remains: what is the locus of ignorance if ultimate reality is nondual? Later Advaita schools offer different explanations. If the *jīva* is taken as the locus of ignorance, while individuality is explained through ignorance, a question arises about the logical interdependence of these explanations. But avidyā is held to be beginningless; the issue is not which came first in time.

If Brahman is understood as the ground of ignorance, this likewise does not mean that Brahman is actually mistaken or that ignorance is an ultimate property of Brahman.

We need not choose a final answer on behalf of a tradition whose own schools propose different accounts.

## Mokṣa: Liberation Through Knowledge

Advaita understands liberation, *mokṣa*, as the removal of ignorance about the true self, not the production of a new state of Ātman.

If Ātman is always identical with Brahman, it cannot truly lose its nature and later recover it. Knowledge does not create Brahman; it discloses what, according to the teaching, has always been real.

Classical Advaita therefore gives a place to instruction, reflection, contemplative assimilation, and ethical preparation. They matter for the student even though they do not produce ultimate reality.

The teaching also belongs to the context of *saṃsāra*, the cycle of birth and death associated with karma. At the empirical level, Advaita recognizes Īśvara, the Lord, as connected with cosmic order and the fruits of action, without making Īśvara a second independent principle alongside Brahman. Liberation cannot therefore be reduced to a single psychological adjustment in one's personal story.

The *Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad* examines waking, dreaming, and deep sleep, while *turīya* points to Ātman as not confined to these states. The mere absence of thoughts is not liberation: habitual ignorance may remain after deep sleep.

Gauḍapāda expresses nonduality in particularly radical terms in *Māṇḍūkya Kārikā* 2.32: ultimately there is neither a bound being, nor one striving for liberation, nor one who has been liberated. Instruction nevertheless retains its meaning for a student at the empirical level.

**Advaita teaches a liberation that reveals the true self never to have been bound.**

This outlines its own philosophical structure. Different accounts of ignorance, māyā, and liberating knowledge remain part of the tradition's internal inquiry. We need not settle all of them before turning to another philosophical position.

## Monophenomenalism Begins Elsewhere

Monophenomenalism states its independent starting proposition:

**Everything that exists is a phenomenon. The phenomenon is one.**

This is neither a translation of “Ātman is Brahman” nor a conclusion drawn from studying Advaita.

Unity here means that **everything that exists is taken as one phenomenon. Distinctions within it are not thereby turned into independent original foundations. Oneness does not mean sameness.**

A person, a world, thoughts, objects, and other people are distinguished. But their being distinguishable does not itself require each to possess an independent ground of existence that must subsequently be connected to the others.

The position is not strictly deduced from the bare fact that experiencing occurs. Any claim available to us, and any test of that claim, does indeed take place within experiencing. Yet this establishes a limit on justification, not the logical impossibility of every alternative ontology.

Monophenomenalism **adopts** the one phenomenon as a philosophical starting point. It does not present that choice as a theorem every philosopher is compelled to accept.

*Model of the Phenomenon* offers one way of investigating questions within this position. The position and the model are related but not identical.

## Five Theses and the Meaning of Distinction

*Model of the Phenomenon* consists of five theses:

1. Everything is one phenomenon.
2. The phenomenon is sensory experiencing.
3. A distinction within sensory experiencing is a concept.
4. Concepts structure the phenomenon into experience.
5. Experience is the phenomenon structured through concepts.

Here *sensory experiencing* includes not only sight, sound, and touch, but also thoughts, emotions, and states—everything that is experienced.

The five theses do not describe five things or successive stages in time. Concepts do not take initially formless experiencing and manufacture a finished experience from it.

**A distinction is how sensory experiencing is. A concept is a system of distinctions.**

Green does not become green because it is named. Pain does not become pain through a mental decision to classify it as pain. Being distinguished does not presuppose a separate agent who manufactures distinctions.

Here “conceptual” does not mean merely verbal, invented, or nonexistent. Thoughts, people, time, distance, and concrete circumstances retain their significance in experience.

The model is itself a concept. Applying it to its own formulations does not independently prove its truth beyond the adopted philosophical position.

## “I,” World, and Consciousness in the Model

The ordinary statement “a person has consciousness and experiences a world” distinguishes the person, consciousness, and the content of experiencing.

But such language does not by itself posit three independent ontological foundations. It is entirely useful in everyday life.

*Model of the Phenomenon* lets us examine **how consciousness, observer, and observed are distinguished in experiencing, and how these distinctions are mutually determinate**.

“I” is distinguished through relations of mine and another's, inside and outside, body, memory, actions, and other determinations of experience. Nor does the word “consciousness” automatically make consciousness an independent owner of the phenomenon.

For experiencing to be, we need not additionally posit an independent observer to whom it is presented.

This does not erase the person. It does not imply that different people's experiences are identical, or that one person's pain is interchangeable with another person's report of it. Differences remain differences.

The model does not search beneath them for a final witness or establish pure consciousness as fundamental reality. It examines the determinacy of relations involving observing, consciousness, “I,” and world.

## Reality, Knowledge, and Error

Experience distinguishes an object from its picture, dreaming from waking, a claim from its test, and an expectation from its outcome.

Calling these distinctions conceptual does not make them equivalent.

A person can genuinely make a calculation error, misidentify an object, or encounter a result that conflicts with a prediction.

The model enables us to investigate relations among claims, evidence, and methods of testing without requiring an independent judge beyond the phenomenon to make them meaningful.

At the same time, the five theses do not yield a single ready-made criterion of truth for every field of inquiry. Substantive testing remains part of investigating particular cases.

**A concept is not ignorance merely because it is a concept.** Experiencing need not be freed from distinctions in order to be what it is.

Likewise, Advaita itself does not demand the destruction of all thinking: removing avidyā cannot be equated with the disappearance of every distinction.

## Freedom: Neither a Property Nor a Compulsory Goal

In the model, freedom is distinguished through its relations with limitation, dependence, and the possibility of action.

Freedom of movement, the experience of choosing, and release from a particular obstacle have determinate meanings in their respective contexts. The experience of free choice is understood through conceptual distinctions; this does not assert the existence of an ontologically independent free will.

Absolute freedom is not distinguished as an ordinary experienced state bounded by an opposite. It is not declared a special property of the phenomenon or an object that some separate subject must possess.

Within the adopted position, person, obstacle, and the possibility of release are distinguished in one phenomenon. The determinacy of a particular limitation does not establish the absolute bondage of all existence. Overcoming an obstacle does not require leaving the phenomenon.

This does not make obstacles imaginary. A locked door really restricts movement. Pain may call for attention, care, or help. Actions have consequences.

But a particular difficulty does not automatically become a verdict on existence as a whole.

We can change circumstances, protect ourselves, make decisions, care for others, and continue living with unanswered questions—without turning life into a wait for final liberation.

**This is a possible practical significance of the perspective, not a necessary consequence of adopting it.** Philosophical understanding imposes no duty to feel happy at all times and does not guarantee that suffering will disappear.

## Where the Two Approaches Genuinely Resemble Each Other

Neither philosophy simply accepts familiar individual identity as an unconditionally independent original foundation. In both, the distinguishability of the world is not by itself equivalent to a plurality of independent ultimate beginnings.

In Advaita, liberating knowledge is essential to the path. The model allows us to investigate how understanding relates to experienced limitation, but it prescribes neither a necessary transformation of experiencing nor a predetermined result. Neither approach requires the everyday world literally to disappear for nonduality to be meaningful.

But these shared directions do not establish identity between their philosophical claims.

The most important boundaries are worth making explicit.

## Brahman and the Phenomenon

**Advaita understands ultimate reality as Brahman—nondual pure consciousness.**

**Monophenomenalism, as a philosophical position, takes everything that exists as one phenomenon. *Model of the Phenomenon* is a distinct instrument of inquiry within that position: it defines the phenomenon as sensory experiencing and examines its distinctions through concepts.**

Monophenomenalism's starting proposition is not identical with the model's definitions, nor does it require adopting this particular instrument. Neither the position nor the model establishes pure consciousness as an independent foundation of the phenomenon.

For Advaita, Brahman is not a second object hidden “behind” the world. Brahman is its ultimate reality. It would therefore be mistaken to contrast the two philosophies as though Advaita added one more thing while the model merely refused to do so.

What differs is **the positive content of the starting position**. Advaita affirms the nature of ultimate reality as Brahman; monophenomenalism adopts one phenomenon without that further determination of its nature.

## Ātman, the Witness, and “I”

Advaita does not stop at questioning the limited personality. It discloses the true Ātman, identical with Brahman.

The model considers “I” as a distinction that does not require a separately established true self behind it. Witness and witnessed are likewise examined through their mutual determinacy in experiencing.

The resemblance concerns the inadequacy of treating an inner little person as the ultimate witness.

But in Advaita, pure consciousness has the status of ultimate reality. In the model, the concept of consciousness does not become an independent foundation of the phenomenon.

Thus “Ātman,” “consciousness,” and “experiencing” cannot simply be declared different names for the same thing without further arguments.

## Avidyā and Concept

Advaita investigates the erroneous identification of the true self with a limited being, and liberation from this ignorance.

The model investigates the distinctions through which self, body, world, limitation, and freedom are determinate.

But **avidyā is not identical with a concept**.

In Advaita, avidyā refers to ignorance concerning the true nature of Ātman. In the model, a concept is a system of distinctions, not an error to be eliminated.

There is nevertheless a meaningful shared question: how is a particular understanding of oneself related to the experience of limitation, and what changes when that understanding is reconsidered?

Both philosophies allow us to investigate the question without requiring the same answer.

## Liberation: A Parallel Without Identity

Both positions allow us to reconsider the idea of liberation as a future acquisition.

**In Advaita, this is connected with Ātman's always-free nature:** liberating knowledge reveals what was never lost.

**In the model, it is connected with examining what is distinguished as bondage, freedom, and the need for liberation.** That examination does not require affirming a true self.

The comparison must not imply that Advaita proposes repairing a damaged Ātman while the model alone rejects that task. Advaita explicitly rejects such an account of liberation.

The difference lies elsewhere. Advaita has a determinate object of liberating knowledge: the identity of Ātman and Brahman. The model does not establish liberation as a compulsory aim or require this knowledge in order to examine freedom.

We may recognize a closeness in the practical direction of the two approaches without declaring their grounds identical.

## Conclusion: What the Comparison Reveals

Advaita and monophenomenalism are not two translations of one teaching.

Advaita affirms Brahman, the true self, ignorance, and liberating knowledge. These claims form an independent philosophical and religious structure in which the Upaniṣads, karma, saṃsāra, Īśvara, and practice matter.

Monophenomenalism begins with a different positive claim: everything that exists is one phenomenon. *Model of the Phenomenon* investigates how it is determinate through sensory experiencing, concepts, and experience.

This is why questions familiar from Advaita may take a different shape within our position. Not because we have found a more convenient name for Brahman, but because we did not begin by requiring the identity of Ātman and Brahman as our foundation.

We can examine people, consciousness, distinctions, and freedom in depth without having to resolve every question of another tradition on that tradition's own terms.

**We can see where Advaita and monophenomenalism resonate—and at the same time understand why they are distinct philosophies.**

The comparison does not require treating either as a refutation of the other. Its result is greater clarity about starting commitments, the content of distinctions, and the possibilities of further inquiry.
